## Step 1: Hypothesis

### What this ad does RIGHT (Ranked by confidence)

**1. Best CPMs in the campaign — the camouflage is excellent (HIGH CONFIDENCE)**

$24.45 average CPM is the lowest across all six ads. The creative — a dark phone screen with late-night search text — looks exactly like someone sharing a relatable screenshot at 2am. Meta's system treats it as organic content. No health ad flags, no commercial content detection. Pure camouflage.

**2. The exhaustion inventory is deeply relatable (HIGH CONFIDENCE)**

"I have tried yoga. I have tried magnesium, ashwagandha, B-complex, iron, vitamin D, melatonin, evening primrose oil, and a $60 adaptogenic powder that tasted like dirt and did nothing."

This list is the most powerful "I've tried everything" beat in the campaign. Every item is specific. Every item is something the target audience has actually tried. The reader sees their own supplement graveyard reflected back at them. This builds instant credibility — "she's been where I am."

**3. The 2am framing creates intimacy (MEDIUM CONFIDENCE)**

"It's 2:17 AM. I'm googling 'why am I still this tired at 45' for the third time this week." — This is vulnerable in a way that feels private. The reader recognizes this moment. They've been this person. The specificity of "2:17 AM" and "for the third time this week" makes it feel like a diary entry, not a marketing message.

### What's NOT working (Ranked by confidence)

**1. Day 1 was an anomaly — CTR collapsed after launch (HIGH CONFIDENCE)**

Day 1: 5.38% CTR, $0.33 CPC, 10 LPVs. That's excellent.
Days 2-4: CTR dropped to ~1.1%, CPC jumped to $2.80-$3.35, LPVs dropped to 0-2/day.

This pattern suggests the ad performed well against the initial test audience (likely the most engaged segment of the broad target) but couldn't sustain performance as Meta expanded to broader audiences. The hook grabs a very specific person (the late-night googler), but most people scrolling Facebook at 7pm don't connect with a 2am framing.

**2. The ad is the purest "curiosity content" in the campaign (HIGH CONFIDENCE)**

This ad reads like a personal essay, not an ad. The voice is raw, unfiltered, stream-of-consciousness. That's what makes the camouflage work — but it's also why the clicks are curiosity-driven, not purchase-driven.

The discovery moment ("Tonight I clicked something different. A research paper.") sends the reader down an intellectual rabbit hole with the narrator. They click to continue the RESEARCH journey, not to buy a product. The 13 LPVs are people who wanted to keep reading, not people who wanted to buy.

**3. Baseline appears too late and with too little weight (HIGH CONFIDENCE)**

The product mention is buried near the end: "I ordered Baseline NMN at 3am that night. I chose it because there was no 'miracle' language." — This is 1 sentence about the product in a 435-word ad. The reader spends 95% of their time in the narrator's emotional state and 5% learning about the solution. That ratio is backwards for conversion.

**4. The "I expected nothing" beat undermines purchase urgency (MEDIUM CONFIDENCE)**

"I expected nothing." — This is meant to build credibility (low expectations → pleasant surprise), but it also gives the reader permission to NOT expect results. If even the narrator expected nothing, why should the reader rush to buy? It deflates urgency.

**5. Same structural problems as all ads (MEDIUM CONFIDENCE)**

Resolved story ("I'm not googling at 2am anymore"), brand-slogan CTA ("Function first. Everything else follows."), no price/offer framing, no specific product differentiation.

### What the ad DID do well

- **"If you're reading this at 2am — I see you."** — This is the most direct audience-addressing line in the campaign. It breaks the fourth wall and speaks to the reader as a peer. Powerful.
- **"My brain started finishing its own thoughts again."** — Best single-line description of cognitive improvement. Specific, visceral, not a health claim.
- **"She said 'you look like yourself again.' I didn't know I'd stopped."** — Strong emotional payoff that implies change without overclaiming.

## Step 2: Action — What Changes for B3-B4

### Keep
- The 2am framing as a hook option — it grabs a specific person hard, even if the audience is smaller
- The exhaustion inventory (the "I've tried everything" list) — this is portable to any ad. It's the best trust-building device in the campaign.
- The "if you're reading this at 2am — I see you" direct address — but move it to AFTER the product bridge, not after the resolved story
- The voice — unfiltered, raw, no polish. This is the most authentic-sounding ad in the campaign.

### Change
- **Tighten the research discovery section.** The ad spends too long on "I read a paper, then another paper, then another." This is intellectually interesting but doesn't build product desire. Condense to: "I found something buried in actual research — not a blog, not an influencer. A molecule my cells already use, that declines after 40. Nobody mentioned this."
- **Expand the product section significantly.** From 1 sentence to 3-4: name it, describe the formula, differentiate it, frame the value.
- **Same structural fixes as all ads:** open reader loop, offer framing, product CTA.
- **Consider this angle for a SHORTER ad format.** The 2am stream-of-consciousness works best as raw emotion. A 200-250 word version that hits harder and faster might outperform the 435-word version — especially since the CTR collapse after day 1 suggests the length loses broader audiences.

### Test Next
- Lower priority than B2C2 iterations. B2C3 has good camouflage metrics but no conversion signal. If retesting, use a shortened version with the structural fixes.
- The "I've tried everything" inventory could be extracted and used as a component in B2C2 V2 — the exhausted mom listing everything she's tried before finding NMN.

---

## Decision
Keep running at current CBO allocation (it's only getting ~$5/day). Not worth killing — it's not eating significant budget and the CPMs prove the camouflage works. But it's not the direction to iterate on. B2C2 is.
