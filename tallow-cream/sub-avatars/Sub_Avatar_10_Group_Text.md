## Anchor
- **Core Avatar:** Invisible Woman
- **Core Desire:** I want to be seen again — and I want to send the Sunday-morning selfie to my college roommates without thinking about whether I should

---

## Origins 5-Category Layering

### Category 1: Desire (Origins #1 priority)
- **Surface Desire:** "I want to be the one who sends a selfie in the Sunday-morning group text instead of being the one who reads the message and puts her phone face-down on the counter."
- **Core Desire mapping:** Be seen again, anchored to a long-running peer cohort — the friends who knew her at 20, 25, 30, 40, and now 52. The Sub-Avatar's specificity is the peer-cohort version of being seen: women who lived together at Penn State for four years, who survived smartphones and chain emails together, who've sent each other midnight reading they'd be embarrassed to share anywhere else. The desire is to be in the photographic record of THIS specific friendship continuity, not to be photographed in general.
- **Mass instinct activated:** Belonging (primary — be in the long-running cohort) + Status (secondary — be visible to the people who have known you longest)

### Category 2: Experience(s) (Origins #2 priority)
- **Situational Experience:** Sunday morning group text. Diane sent a selfie from her back porch. Karen sent one back from her kitchen. The narrator didn't send hers. An hour later Karen wrote: "Where's yours?" The narrator read the message and put her phone face-down on the counter. The group text has existed continuously since Penn State — through chain emails, through the first smartphones, through 32 years of friendship.
- **Product Experience:** CeraVe for a year. Then a retinol from a dermatologist she saw once and never went back to. Then an $85 Sephora serum because a woman her age at the counter said it had changed her life. Then a midnight Reddit vitamin C serum. By 50 she'd stopped buying things. Tired of throwing money at a problem nothing was solving.
- **Active or past?** Active and digital — the group text Sunday is the recurring trigger; the self-exclusion has accelerated over 2+ years

### Category 3: Emotion(s) (Origins #3 priority)
- **Primary emotion:** Sadness (specifically: self-exclusion from a cohort she still belongs to)
- **Secondary emotion + WHY:** Lonely-among-friends stemming from Sadness. The Origins secondary register here is the precise emotional dislocation of being inside a group whose love is not in question but choosing to step out of the photographic part of it. She isn't being excluded. She's excluding herself. And worse — her friends have been quietly doing the same thing on a separate thread, ordering tallow without her, because they didn't know how to bring it up. The grief is the gap that formed in a group that wasn't supposed to have gaps.

### Category 4: Behaviour(s) (Origins #4 priority)
- **Specific habit:** Offers to take the picture at every family event. Easter, niece's college graduation, parents' anniversary dinner. Has been the one for 18 months. Nobody has to ask her. She finds a better angle. She gets out of the frame. She's gotten very smooth at it.
- **Source:** Stems from Experience (the foundation cracking, the tight cheeks, the stopped selfies) + Emotion (the self-exclusion that has slid from group text to in-person)
- **Frequency / strength:** At every photo opportunity. Strength = nobody has noticed yet because the offering is so smooth it reads as helpful.

### Category 5: Demographics (Origins #5 — sparingly)
- Woman 50-58
- Has a long-running group text or peer-cohort communication channel with friends from her 20s — without 30+ years of friendship continuity, the Sub-Avatar doesn't exist
- College-educated (the Penn State framing in B15C2 is specific — same-college peer cohorts are a particular Invisible Woman subsegment)
- Has at least one adult child in her 20s who comes home and notices ("what are you doing differently" — Day 9)

## Verbatim Language Patterns

- **Phrases she uses to describe her problem:**
  - "I read the message and put my phone face-down on the counter."
  - "I'd stopped sending pictures around the time I turned fifty."
  - "We send pictures. We send articles. We send the things we read at midnight that we'd be embarrassed to read in front of anyone but each other."
  - "I told myself it was a phase. It wasn't a phase."
  - "There she is." (Karen, after the day-14 selfie returns)
  - "This is the version of you I forgot existed." (Karen at Easter)

- **Internal-monologue patterns:**
  - Tracks the friendship's communication history across years and platforms ("Before that we had a chain email. Before that we just called.")
  - Receives blunt friend-care passively when it arrives ("I wasn't mad. I was sitting there with my coffee not knowing what to say.")
  - Notices the smoothness of the photo-avoiding habit as an accomplishment that turns to grief

- **Words she'd embrace:** there she is, the version of you, look rested, the group text, the front row, the selfie back
- **Words she'd reject:** menopause, hormones, anti-aging, hiding, shame, glow up, glow, transformation, plumping, brightening

## Notes

The structural hypothesis behind this Sub-Avatar is that the long-running peer-cohort communication channel (group text, group thread, weekly call group) is its own social infrastructure, distinct from individual friendships. The Sub-Avatar's emotional engine isn't one friend noticing — it's the cohort noticing the absence of her selfie, the cohort having quietly built a tallow conversation on a side thread without her, the cohort welcoming her back with "there she is" when day 14 arrives.

This is the only B15 ad that uses parallel peer authorities (Diane AND Karen, both same age, both already using it independently) rather than a single discovery character. The hypothesis being tested: does parallel peer-use carry more weight than serial chain authority? If two friends in her same cohort were both already using it for months and didn't tell her, that's a different kind of social proof than one friend whose aunt told her.

B15C2 also deliberately re-tests the falsified B6 hypothesis. B6C1/C2/C3 falsified the hypothesis that anything other than skin-specific verbal noticing can compete with "your skin looks different." B15C2's husband-notice format is "you look rested" — a near-adjacent verbal that's been used in the converters (Day 9 in B7C1, Margaret in B9C2) but never as the close itself. By making it cumulative (3 times in 7 days, 3 settings) and naming the cumulative effect explicitly ("Ron does not say things like that in a vacuum"), the ad bets that frequency + cumulative weight can substitute for single-moment skin-specific noticing. If it works, the husband-notice rule gets loosened. If it doesn't, the rule is reinforced.

What we expect to learn:
- Whether the peer-cohort group-text hook reaches a different subsegment than the sister-blood hook (B15C1) or the heritage-artifact hook (B15C3). All three B15 ads test different relational specificities; the convert pattern reveals which relational frame this audience pool responds to most.
- Whether peer-self-investigation mechanism delivery (Diane figures it out herself at 1am, no cited outside authority) is as credible as peer-citing-physician delivery (B15C1's Lisa cites her own annual physical). If both convert, the cited-authority frame may not be load-bearing.
- Whether the cumulative husband notice (3x "you look rested") survives as a substitute for single-moment skin-specific verbal. This is the highest-stakes single-variable test in B15.
- Whether the photo-frame social-cessation beat lands at the same strength as Photo Avoider's documentary-absence beats (Sub-Avatar 7). The behaviors overlap; B15C2 may compete with B13 for the same subsegment, or may reach a different one.

Why it's awaiting launch: same as B15C1 — car repair expense delayed launch from May 15 to ~May 27.

The Sub-Avatar's name in shorthand is "Group Text." The defining feature is peer-cohort self-exclusion in a long-running digital communication channel. Future variants could test other peer-cohort vectors (book club WhatsApp, weekly walking group, college sorority alumni Facebook group).
