# Partner Page — Full Identity Plan

> **Purpose:** A "real person" Facebook page that feels like a genuine woman sharing her life — who happens to have discovered tallow after her skin changed in her mid-40s. This page exists to: (1) run native/camouflage ads that feel like they come from a real person, (2) provide organic posts that make the page look authentic when ad-clickers check the profile, (3) serve as a credible voice for multiple avatars (Invisible Woman, Working Hands Man's wife, Homesteader Mom's older friend, Product Graveyard peer).
>
> **Critical Rule:** This page must never feel like a brand account or a skincare influencer. It should feel like a real woman's Facebook page where she posts about her life, her kids, her dog, and occasionally mentions the stuff she puts on her face. The product is a background detail in her life, not the center of it.

---

## FTC Disclosure Compliance

**The FTC requires that material connections between endorsers and brands be disclosed clearly and conspicuously.**

**Rules for this page:**

1. **Per-post disclosure required** on any post that mentions, shows, or features the tallow product. Bio-only disclosure is insufficient per FTC guidance.
2. **Disclosure must appear early in the post** — not buried at the bottom or in hashtags below the fold.
3. **Facebook branded content tag** ("Paid partnership with [brand]") is the gold standard — use it on all product-featuring posts when available.
4. **Posts that don't mention the product** (personal, parenting, life stuff) need no disclosure. She's a real person posting real content — only brand-connected content triggers the requirement.
5. **Reference:** FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255), updated June 2023.

## Why This Identity Works

1. **She IS the Invisible Woman.** 48, skin changed around 44-45, tried everything, spent more money than she'll admit, found tallow through a coworker's wife at a holiday party. Her audience doesn't look up to her — they recognize themselves.

2. **She's the Working Hands Man's wife.** When a native ad describes a man whose wife handed him the jar, or whose wife noticed his hands — Julie is that woman. Ads about men's cracked hands coming from her page feel completely natural.

3. **She's the Homesteader Mom's slightly older friend.** Not a homesteader herself, but she respects the ethos. She buys from farmers markets. She reads ingredient labels. She's the person a Homesteader Mom would trust because she's practical, not ideological.

4. **She's a Product Graveyard peer.** She had the drawer. She spent the money. She can talk about what failed because she lived it.

5. **She's NOT in the wellness industry.** Office manager at an engineering firm. This is critical. When someone clicks through from a native ad and sees her page, they see a normal woman with a normal job — not a skincare account, not an influencer, not someone trying to sell them something.

## Backstory (Internal — For Content Consistency)

Julie's skin started changing around 44. She'd had normal, slightly oily skin her whole adult life. Within about 18 months, everything flipped. Dry. Tight. Her foundation stopped sitting right. The skin on her cheeks and around her eyes felt papery by mid-afternoon.

She did what most women do — bought more products. CeraVe. Then Olay Regenerist. Then a $65 retinol serum her coworker swore by. Then Drunk Elephant. Each one worked for about a week and then her skin went right back to tight and dry.

She mentioned it to her doctor at her annual checkup. The doctor said: "That's normal. Estrogen decline. Your skin's going to change." No solution offered.

December 2024, she went to her husband's company holiday party. Got talking to another wife — Denise, late 50s, ranch background. Denise had a small jar in her purse. Tallow balm. Four ingredients. She said her daughter-in-law made it but the one she really liked was from a company. Told Julie to try it.

Julie almost didn't. "Beef fat on my face" sounded ridiculous. But Denise's skin looked better than hers, and Denise was ten years older. So she ordered a jar.

Two weeks later her husband said her skin looked different. That had never happened. Not with CeraVe, not with the $65 retinol, not with anything.

She's been using it since. She doesn't understand the science. She doesn't care about the science. She just knows her face stopped being dry and tight for the first time in three years.

## Voice & Tone Guidelines

### DO:
- Talk like you're texting your sister or your best friend from college
- Use "I" language exclusively — "I tried," "I noticed," "I don't know why"
- Be self-deprecating about the skincare thing — "I never thought I'd be the person posting about face cream"
- Reference specific, real details — the name of the engineering firm's holiday party, Biscuit chewing a shoe, her son leaving dishes in the sink when he visits
- Admit what she doesn't know — "I have no idea what calendula is, I just know the jar has four things in it"
- Keep posts short — she's not writing essays. 3-5 sentences for most posts.
- Let the product mentions feel incidental — like she's mentioning it the way she'd mention any product she likes

### DON'T:
- Sound like ad copy. Ever. No "game-changing," "holy grail," "obsessed," "you NEED this"
- Use skincare influencer language — no "routine," "regimen," "self-care Sunday," "glow up"
- Post only about skincare — 70%+ of content should have nothing to do with the product
- Make health claims — "my face stopped being dry" not "it cured my skin"
- Be preachy about ingredients or "clean" living — she's not on a mission, she just found something that works
- Over-explain — she doesn't know the science and doesn't pretend to
- Use the word "journey." Ever.

# PART 3: HOW ADS CONNECT TO THIS PAGE

When a user sees a native/camouflage ad in their feed and clicks through to see who posted it, they land on Julie's page. What they see:

1. **Profile photo:** A real woman, not a brand logo
2. **Recent posts:** Normal life stuff — dog, kids, work, hiking
3. **Occasional skin mentions:** Natural, not salesy, feels like a real person's discovery
4. **No product-heavy grid:** The product is a background detail, not the identity

This is what makes camouflage ads work. The page confirms the story the ad told. If the ad says "a woman like me tried this and it worked," and the page looks like a woman like her — the trust loop closes.

**For Working Hands Man ads:** Julie is the wife, the woman who discovered the jar and handed it to her husband. The ad narrator's wife = Julie's page voice. A man clicks through, sees a normal woman's page, thinks "oh, this is like my wife" — trust confirmed.

**For Invisible Woman ads:** Julie IS the narrator. Same age, same experience, same discovery. A woman clicks through, sees someone who looks like her — trust confirmed.

**For Homesteader Mom ads:** Julie isn't a homesteader, but her page feels real and practical. A homesteader mom clicks through and sees a normal woman who cares about ingredients without being preachy about it — trust confirmed.
