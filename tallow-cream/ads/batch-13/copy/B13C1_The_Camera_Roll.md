## Body Copy

On a Sunday in January, Hannah was home from college for winter break.

She was making a photo book for my mother's seventy-fifth birthday. She'd been sitting on the couch for two hours scrolling my iCloud library on her phone.

She looked up.

She said: "Mom. I've scrolled through eight hundred and forty-seven photos from this year. You're in nineteen of them. That's two percent."

I was standing in the doorway holding a mug of coffee.

I didn't know what to say.

========

I'm Maggie. I'm fifty-one. I've been a librarian at Jefferson Elementary in Grand Rapids for fourteen years. My husband Steve owns a plumbing business. Our daughter Hannah is twenty-two, finishing her last semester of college.

My skin changed around forty-seven. Not gradually — or at least it didn't feel gradual. One month my face was fine. The next month it was tight by nine in the morning and my foundation was cracking along my mouth before lunch.

I tried CeraVe for six months. The blue tub. Nothing held past noon. Then a sixty-dollar retinol the internet told me would fix it — it burned for two weeks and made the tightness worse. Then a drugstore balm my sister mailed me with a sticky note that said "I promise, this is the one." I gave it three weeks. My face went back to tight.

My doctor looked at my skin for about sixty seconds. She said: "That's very common at your age. Your skin is going to keep changing." She offered nothing else.

I stopped wearing makeup on weekends. I told myself it was good to give my skin a rest. That was part of it. The other part was that I didn't want to watch my foundation crack at the grocery store.

The other thing I stopped was being in pictures. It happened slowly enough that I never noticed I'd decided anything. I got smooth at being the one who grabbed the phone when someone said group photo. Nobody had to ask anymore. I just moved.

========

Hannah put her legs down off the couch and patted the cushion next to her.

We sat together at the kitchen island.

She handed me the phone and scrolled.

Christmas morning, 2024. Every photo from that morning on my screen. Steve in his flannel. The tree. The light coming through the back window at seven in the morning. Two mugs on the counter. I wasn't in any of them.

Hannah's college parents' weekend in September. Thirty-eight photos. Steve talking to Hannah's advisor. Hannah's dorm hallway. Hannah and her roommate laughing at something. I was in two. Both from across the room.

She found one beach photo. One out of thirty-one.

She said: "I found something. Sarah Whitfield — my RA senior year. Her mom had the same thing. Your exact thing. I'll send you the name. Don't roll your eyes at me."

I rolled my eyes.

She texted me the name anyway.

========

That night after Steve had gone to bed we sat at the kitchen island. Hannah put her phone face-down on the counter between us.

She said: "Here's what Sarah's mom told her."

She said: "Your skin makes its own oil. Always has. That's why you never thought about it at thirty. The oil was just there. After forty it makes a lot less. And every single thing you've tried — the CeraVe, the retinol, whatever Aunt Kay sent — the first ingredient on every one of them is water. Flip them over. Water, then glycerin, then something you can't pronounce. Water sits on your face for an hour. Then it evaporates. That's why nothing holds. That's not the cream failing. That's what water does."

I said: "So what's different about this one."

She said: "It's fat. Tallow. Same structure as the oil your skin stopped making. Sarah's mom said your face takes it in like it's supposed to be there. Not sitting on top. Actually absorbed."

She slid the product page across the counter toward me. Four ingredients. Forty-nine ninety-nine.

Then she Venmod me forty-nine ninety-nine before I could say anything.

"Just order it. If you don't I will."

========

I ordered it Sunday night.

Day 1. It arrived Thursday. I put it on before bed. It felt like warm honey pressed into my palm and went in faster than I expected. No greasy residue. I lay there in the dark waiting to feel something and didn't.

Day 4. Monday morning. I touched my cheek before I got up. Not tight. I went to the bathroom to check. Still not tight. I pressed my finger into my cheekbone and watched the color come back. I stood there longer than I needed to.

Day 9. Tuesday night. Steve looked up from his dinner plate and said: "Are you doing something different?" I said no. He said "okay" and went back to eating. He had never asked me that before. Not once in twenty-three years of dinners.

Day 14. Parent-teacher conference night. Foundation on at four in the afternoon. I stood on a gym floor for four hours talking to parents. At eight, between my second-to-last and last appointment, I went to the women's restroom. The foundation was still where I'd put it. It hadn't cracked along my mouth. It hadn't moved on my forehead. That hadn't happened in over a year.

Day 21. A Tuesday at lunch. I was sitting at my library desk and the light from the window was on my face. I picked up my phone and took a selfie. I looked at it. Then I texted it to Hannah without any words. She sent back three question marks. I sent a thumbs up.

It was the first selfie I'd taken in almost two years.

========

Here's what I didn't see coming.

For about three years I'd been doing the same thing at every dinner, every party, every moment when someone said group photo. My hand moved toward my bag before anyone else had looked up. I'd gotten very smooth at it. Nobody had to ask me. I just moved.

March. About three weeks after I'd stopped counting the days. Dinner with four friends at a restaurant we'd been going to since our kids were small. We finished dessert. My friend Gail said: "Wait, group photo."

My hand moved toward my purse.

Gail held up her own phone and pointed it at the table.

I sat where I was.

The photo went around in a group text an hour later. There I was. In the middle, between Gail and Dana.

I looked at it for a long time.

I hadn't grabbed the phone. The thought just hadn't come. I'd been sitting there and then I was in the photo.

========

A week after that dinner, Gail texted and asked what I was doing differently. She'd been looking at the photo.

I told her. Hannah. Sarah Whitfield's mom. The jar.

She went quiet for a minute. Then: "My skin has been just off for about a year and a half. I couldn't name what was wrong."

I said: "That's the name."

She ordered one that afternoon.

========

One year later. January again.

Hannah is home for Christmas break. She's making a photo book for my fifty-second birthday. She's been on the couch with my phone for about an hour.

She looks up.

She says: "Mom. You're in four hundred and twelve photos out of nine hundred and six from this year. That's forty-five percent. And you're smiling in all of them."

I'm at the kitchen island with a mug of coffee. The same spot I was standing last January when she looked up.

She puts the phone down on the cushion next to her.

"Did you notice?" she asks.

I think about it.

"No," I say. "I didn't."

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's your daughter on the couch in January, scrolling your camera roll, counting the photos you're in — and the number being forty-five this year instead of two.

It's the dinner in March where your hand moves toward your purse and then stops because someone else already has the phone and you're just there, at the table, in the picture.

It's the Christmas morning you stop standing in the doorway and walk into the room.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. Sixty-day money-back guarantee. You email them, you get every dollar back. No argument.

Link is below.

Hannah is still counting.
