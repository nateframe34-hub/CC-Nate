# B13C2 — The Christmas Card

**Sub-Avatar:** The Photo Avoider (Invisible Woman sub-sub-avatar)
**Angle:** Annual ritual as documented absence — four consecutive Christmas cards with three people in them, not four
**Character type:** Solution-Aware Invisible Woman
**Hook format:** Annual ritual + accumulated evidence + reversal
**Awareness level:** Solution Aware

## Body Copy

Every year since 2001 I've taken the family Christmas card photo.

I set up the tripod. I frame the shot. I clip the remote to my sleeve, run across the room, click it, run back to check, adjust, run back again. We've done it in front of the tree, out front with the snow, in front of the fireplace. Twenty-three years of the same routine.

Four years ago I stopped running back into the frame.

I'd set it up, take a test shot, look at the preview, and delete every shot I was in. My skin looked tired. I don't mean "not at my best." I mean I looked like I hadn't slept in a week and I was wearing yesterday's makeup.

I tried running back in anyway. I tried a different angle. I tried more foundation. I deleted them all.

For the last four years I've just stopped trying. I send my husband Rick and my boys to stand in front of the tree. I click the remote from behind the tripod. We print the card.

Four Christmas cards. Three Martins in the photo. No Diane.

========

I'm Diane. Fifty-four years old. I've been a dental hygienist at the same practice in Omaha for twenty-two years. My husband Ron is fifty-six. Our sons are twenty-four and twenty-six, both moved out. The same Christmas card routine since 2001. The same tripod. Different tree stands over the years, same routine.

My skin changed around fifty. I noticed it in the spring, then again in the summer. By fall it was tight by eight in the morning. My foundation cracked in the crease under my lower lip by noon. My makeup sat on my face differently than it had before — drier, like it had nowhere to go.

I tried what you'd expect. The CeraVe. A retinol my sister-in-law swore by for six months. A cream I ordered at eleven at night after going down a rabbit hole online. My doctor looked at my face in November and said: "That's normal at your age. Your skin is changing." She said it in a way that meant: and it's going to keep changing, and there's nothing I'm going to give you for it.

I stopped using the tripod in 2022.

Not all at once. I told myself we'd do outdoor photos that year. Then the weather was bad. Then it seemed easier to have the boys take turns with Ron. Then the cards went out. I wasn't in them. Nobody said anything.

That was the thing about being behind the tripod. Nobody said anything.

========

Thanksgiving at Ron's sister Karen's house in Lincoln.

Karen is sixty-one. Three Christmases ago she looked her age. That's not a dig — I mean she looked like a woman in her early sixties who'd been cleaning teeth and raising two kids and being married for thirty years. Normal. The way most of us look.

I hadn't seen her in eight months. She opened the front door on Wednesday afternoon and I stood there.

I'm not going to overdramatize. But her face looked different. Not pulled. Not done. Just — easier, somehow. Better than I'd seen it.

I watched her for two days. I wasn't being weird about it. I was just looking. On Thursday I kept thinking: what is she doing? On Friday morning she was in the kitchen at six-thirty making coffee before anyone else was up. I came downstairs. We were the only two people in the house who were awake.

I said: "Karen. What are you using."

She put the carafe down and said: "I wondered when you were going to ask."

She filled my cup first. Then she walked me upstairs to her bathroom. She opened the medicine cabinet and pulled out a small amber jar.

That was the whole cabinet. The jar and some toothpaste.

========

We sat on the edge of the guest bed and she told me while I held the jar.

She said: "Here's the thing about your skin. It makes its own oil. That's what it's always done. Before fifty you didn't think about it. The oil was just there. After fifty, for most of us, it's basically gone. It drops way off. And every cream you've been putting on — every single one — is water. That's the first ingredient. Water. Then glycerin. Then some thickener. The water sits on your face for an hour and then it evaporates. That's not the cream doing something wrong. That's what water does."

I said: "So this isn't water."

She said: "It's fat. Tallow. Same kind of fat skin makes on its own. Your face absorbs it because it recognizes it. It's not sitting on top of something dry. It's going in."

She said: "I threw out everything else I had eight months ago. I kept one jar. That's it."

She said: "Get in the Christmas card this year, Diane."

I drove home Friday night with the jar in my bag. I'd ordered my own before I reached the city limits.

========

I used it Friday night at home.

It didn't feel like a cream. It rubbed in fast. No scent I was expecting. No film left. I went to sleep and lay there thinking: I have no idea if this is going to do anything.

Day 5. Ron looked at me across the breakfast table on a Wednesday and said: "You look rested." I hadn't slept any differently. I asked him what he meant. He said: "I don't know. You just look rested." He went back to his toast.

Day 10. I got the tripod out of the closet. That hadn't happened in four years. I set it up in the living room. I took twelve shots — some test shots, some real ones. I kept three. Ron in all three. Both boys in all three. Me in all three.

I looked at them for a long time.

My face didn't look tired. It looked like my face.

Day 16. I took the files to the print shop. The woman behind the counter looked at the photo on the screen and said: "Beautiful family." She printed sixty cards.

Day 22. Cards went out in the mail the week before Christmas. My mother-in-law called me three days later. Ruth is seventy-nine and she calls when something is worth calling about.

She said: "You're in it this year."

That was all she said. She didn't explain what she meant by it. She didn't need to.

========

Here's the part I didn't expect.

For about three years I'd been the first person to offer to take pictures at anything. Baby showers, birthday parties, retirement lunches at the office — someone would say "we should get a photo" and I'd say "I'll take it" before anyone else moved. I'd gotten good at it. Good at the angle. Good at getting everyone in. Good at not being in it.

About three weeks after the Christmas cards went out I was at a baby shower at work for one of the hygienists. We'd finished cake. Someone said "group photo." My coworker Carol held out her phone and looked around the room.

She looked at me.

She said: "Can you get one?"

I said: "Sure."

I took the phone.

Then I stopped and thought about it.

Carol hadn't handed me the phone because I was the photo person. She'd been at this practice for two years — she didn't know I was the photo person. She handed it to me because I was standing closest to the birthday cake.

She would have handed it to anyone.

I took two shots. Then I said: "Wait, someone else take one and get me in it."

Carol looked a little confused. She took the phone back.

I stepped into the frame.

========

Karen has been on it for about a year and a half now. I've been on it for eight months. My daughter-in-law asked me about it at Christmas dinner — she'd noticed something was different but couldn't name it. I told her. She ordered one that night.

Ron has said something three times since that breakfast when he said I looked rested. Once at dinner with friends. Once in the car. Once in the kitchen when I was doing dishes and he walked in and just said it, unprompted, with nothing leading up to it.

He said: "You look like yourself."

I didn't say anything back. I didn't know what to say back.

========

This Christmas, the tripod came out again.

But I didn't set the remote up. Ron knows where it is. He knows the drill by now. He clicked it while I was already standing in front of the tree with both boys.

He didn't have to come get me.

I was already in the frame.

The card went out. Ruth called again.

She said: "Two years in a row. You look like yourself again."

I look like myself again.

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the Christmas card with all four of you in it. Not three people and one gap where you stood behind the tripod.

It's your mother-in-law calling to say you look like yourself — meaning you look like the version of yourself the people who love you have been missing.

It's the tripod coming out of the closet because you're going to be in the picture. Not because you're going to frame it so you can hide behind it.
