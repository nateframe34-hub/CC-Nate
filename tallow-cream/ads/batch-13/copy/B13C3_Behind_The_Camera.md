# B13C3 — Behind The Camera

**Sub-Avatar:** The Photo Avoider (Invisible Woman sub-sub-avatar)

**Character type:** Solution-Aware Invisible Woman

## Body Copy

Saturday night. Mark's laptop was open on the couch.

He'd been scrolling Google Photos for twenty minutes. 2023 — our year in review, the way the app puts it together. I was half-watching from across the couch with a book.

He slowed down. Scrolled back. Slowed down again.

He scrolled back a second time.

He said: "When did you stop being in pictures?"

I looked at the screen.

He kept scrolling. Banff, summer of '22. Eighty-four photos. I was in three. He kept going. Our daughter's graduation that spring. Forty-two photos. I was in two. He kept going. Every album was the same shape. Lots of photos, the same number of people I was in.

He didn't say anything else. He closed the laptop.

I sat with my book and didn't read.

========

I'm Ellen. I'm fifty-two. I run HR at a regional hospital in Spokane. My husband Mark is fifty-five — he's a nurse at the same hospital, different floor. Our daughter is twenty, at college in Seattle. Mark and I have been married for twenty-six years.

My skin changed at forty-eight. The way most of my friends describe it — a shift. The foundation I'd been using for years started cracking by two in the afternoon. My face felt tight most mornings before I'd done anything to it. I tried the usual things. Drugstore first. Then a retinol that burned the first two weeks and I assumed meant it was working. Then an oil someone at work swore by. My doctor looked at me for ninety seconds and said: "That's normal. Your skin is going to keep changing."

She wasn't wrong. It kept changing.

In 2022 we went to the coast with some friends for a week. My daughter posted a photo of the four of us on the beach to Instagram. I saw it on my phone an hour later. I stared at it for a long time. I didn't sleep well that night.

After that I stopped being in photos.

Not all at once. I started staying in frame a little less. I started being the one who offered to take the picture when someone wanted a group shot. The more I did it, the smoother I got at it. By 2023 I didn't have to think about it. I was just the one behind the camera.

========

Monday morning at work. I was in the break room filling my water bottle. The microwave above the counter is stainless and I caught my reflection in it. I was doing what I'd been doing for a while — checking my face. Jaw, under my eyes, forehead. A quick scan.

Pam was behind me.

Pam is fifty-eight. She's a charge nurse on Mark's floor. She's been here longer than most of the doctors. She is the bluntest person in this building.

She said: "I know that look. I used to do it. Come to my office after shift."

I said: "You don't have to do that."

She said: "I know I don't have to. Come anyway."

========

After shift she closed the door and pulled a small amber jar out of her desk drawer. Set it on the desk in front of me.

She said: "My sister told me about this a year ago. I didn't believe her for three months. Then I tried it. I've told four other women on this floor since then. We're all using it."

She said: "Here's what's happening with your skin. It makes its own oil — that's what it's always done. When you were thirty you never thought about it. The oil was just there. After forty it drops. A lot. And every cream in every bottle you've tried has water as the first ingredient. Water and glycerin and something to hold it together. That water sits on your face for an hour and then evaporates. That's why nothing has worked. That's not those creams failing. That's just what water is."

I said: "What does this one have instead."

She said: "Fat. Tallow. Same structure as the oil your skin used to make. Your face takes it in. It doesn't sit on top. It absorbs the same way the oil did."

She pushed the jar across the desk.

"Four ingredients. Forty-nine ninety-nine. My sister found it first. I thought she was losing it. Now I've told four people who work ten feet from me."

She said: "Get back in the pictures, Ellen."

I looked at the jar for a minute.

I ordered one from my phone in the parking lot before I started the car.

========

Monday night I used it.

It didn't feel like a cream. It rubbed in almost immediately, the way warm wax goes into wood. No residue. I went to bed and thought: okay, we'll see.

Day 4. Thursday morning. I got up and put on makeup without looking in the bathroom mirror first to check my face. I had just done it — foundation on, mascara, done — before I realized I hadn't done the check. The check I'd been doing every morning for over a year. Foundation takes two minutes. The check had been taking five.

Day 9. Saturday. Mark was at the sink in the bathroom and I came in to get something. He turned and put his hand on my cheek for a second — not a gesture, not a move, just put it there. His thumb near my ear. He didn't say anything. He left it there for a few seconds. Then he took his hand back and left the room.

He didn't say anything about it. I didn't ask.

Day 14. Twelve-hour shift. I walked out to the parking lot at seven in the evening. I caught my reflection in the window of my car as I reached for the door. The fluorescents in the parking garage are bad light. They show everything. I looked the same as I had at seven in the morning. Same foundation. Same face.

I stood there at my car door for a minute longer than I needed to.

Day 21. My daughter texted me a photo on a Saturday afternoon. We'd gotten coffee the week before — just the two of us. She'd taken it without telling me. I was laughing at something. She'd captioned it: "mom, we should do this more." I looked at the photo for a long time. I looked like myself in it.

========

Here's what I didn't notice until a month later.

I used to think about where I was standing in photos. Not consciously. More like an automatic adjustment. If someone was about to take a picture I'd shift to my left — to be on my better side, the side with better light, the angle that hid the part of my jaw I didn't like. I'd been doing it for two years. It wasn't a big move. Just a step, maybe a small turn. I'd gotten smooth at it.

At my nephew's baptism in April. Big family photo after the service. Everyone gathered on the steps. I walked over and stood where there was space.

I was on my right side.

The wrong side.

I didn't move. I didn't think to move. The picture happened. Someone texted the photo around the family group chat a week later. I saw it and looked at my face and then remembered I'd been on the wrong side and thought: oh. I didn't even think about it.

That was the thing I'd gotten back. Not my face. The not-thinking-about-it.

========

Pam's still using it. Three of the four women she told are still using it. The fourth one I don't know. My daughter asked me about it after I told her — she said her skin had been "doing something weird" since she'd started a new semester. I told her what Pam told me. She ordered one that night.

Mark has said something twice more since day 9 in the bathroom. Once in the kitchen when I was pouring coffee. Once in the car on the way to a friend's dinner. He didn't build up to it either time. He just said it. "You look good."

Twenty-six years. He doesn't say things like that in a vacuum.

========

Saturday night again. Same couch. Mark's laptop open.

He's scrolling Google Photos. Last month this time.

He says: "There you are."

He keeps scrolling.

"There you are."

"There you are."

He stops. He turns the laptop toward me.

He says: "When did this start happening again?"

I look at the screen. Twelve photos from the last month. I'm in seven of them. Not posed. Not staged. Just in them.

I think about when it started.

"I don't know," I say. "I didn't notice."

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the Saturday night on the couch. The person who has been looking at your face for twenty-six years scrolling the last month of photos and turning the laptop toward you and asking when you started being in pictures again.

It's not having an answer.

Because you didn't notice you'd come back.

Link is below.

Mark closed the laptop.

He picked up his wine.

He looked at me again.
