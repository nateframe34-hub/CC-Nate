## Body Copy

I almost bought it at the farmers market in October.

It was a Saturday. I was there for winter squash and eggs. A woman had a small table set up near the back — farm skincare, amber glass jars, handwritten cards. I walked past, then stopped. I picked up the jar. Four ingredients on the label. I turned it over and saw the price: $38.

I put it back.

I had canning lids still to buy. The feed bill was due. The gaskets on the pressure canner needed replacing and I'd been putting that off for three weeks.

I said to myself: not for me right now.

I kept walking. I had eggs to find.

========

I am thirty-eight years old. I have been married for fifteen years. We have three kids — Ellie is eleven, Mae is eight, Sam is five. We keep twelve acres, two dairy goats, thirty chickens, a kitchen garden that runs eight months of the year, and a pantry I put up every fall.

I also have a quiet pattern I hadn't fully named until recently.

The Christmas list I filled out for everyone in the house. I crossed myself off.

The gift card from my mother-in-law last February — a good one, meant for me. I redirected it to the kids' winter coats.

My insulated boots. Left one started leaking two winters ago. I've been duct-taping that seam instead of replacing them. The boots still work.

The jar at the farmers market.

These aren't big things. None of them are. But I'd been doing it steadily for about six years — treating care of myself as the line item that didn't quite fit. I hadn't thought of it that way. I just kept walking.

========

Every fall for ten years I drive to Aunt Pat's to help with the last of the apples.

She's in her mid-seventies. She's kept a homestead three counties over for forty years. She's small and wiry and she still splits her own kindling. When I pull in the driveway her hands are always already going — something soaking, something drying, something being sorted.

I went the first weekend of November this year. We spent Saturday on the apples — peeling and slicing for the dehydrator, filling up tray after tray while we talked. Late at night when the last tray was loaded, she looked at me across the table and said, without much preamble: "Your face looks tired. And your hands."

She didn't say it like a criticism. She just said it the way she says everything — plain, and then waiting.

She got up and went to her bathroom. She came back with an amber glass jar and set it on the counter between us.

========

"This lives on my counter," she said. "Put some on tonight and some tomorrow morning. I've got three jars — take this one home. It doesn't cost you anything to try it."

I turned it over. She read the ingredients out loud before I could.

"Tallow. Honey. Olive oil. Calendula." She looked up. "Safe enough to eat. That's always been my rule."

I smelled the jar. I was expecting something farmyard. There was nothing like that — just a faint honey note and something green and mild.

She said, "I render it slow — that's what takes it out. What you smell is the honey."

Then she said one more thing, brief, before we turned off the kitchen light.

She said: the fat in the jar is almost the same structure as what skin makes on its own. That's why it absorbs instead of sitting on top. Not a cream going on. Something going back in. One minute and she'd said enough. She wasn't selling it. She was just explaining.

I put the jar in my bag.

========

I drove home Sunday afternoon. After the kids were in bed I sat at the bathroom counter and opened the jar.

There was a small flicker — this is for me, not for the kids, not for the house. I let it sit there for a second. Then I put a small amount on my palms, the way Aunt Pat had shown me, and pressed it into my face and jaw.

I turned off the light and went to bed.

========

Day 5 I noticed something I hadn't been expecting.

The skin along my jaw — just below the ear, where the jaw hinges — had been tight since June. That was when we brought the dairy goats in and the early mornings changed. Four-thirty milkings, weather in the face, wind off the back field. I'd gotten used to the tightness. Stopped registering it.

On Day 5 it wasn't there. The skin along the jaw felt like it had released something it had been holding. I pressed my fingers into it and the give was different. Not dramatic. Just a thing I'd been carrying that I wasn't carrying anymore.

Day 11 was a Sunday. Nancy runs the lunch at our church — she's been doing it for fifteen years, she knows everyone and everything. She stopped me at the coffee urn after the service. She said, "You look like you've had a good week. Your face looks rested."

I thanked her. I didn't explain yet.

Day 19 I was washing up before breakfast and I caught myself looking in the mirror without doing the small wince I apparently always do.

I hadn't known I was doing it. I only noticed when it stopped. The wince, and whatever I was reacting to when I winced — I couldn't point to a specific change in what I was looking at. I just wasn't bracing. I was looking and then looking away without that small clenching moment in between.

I stood there for a minute. Then I went and made breakfast.

========

Week 4, my husband split firewood during the first cold snap in December. Three days outside at the chopping block, getting the woodstove stocked for the month. His knuckles cracked open the way they do in cold and dry — deep, at the joints. I handed him my jar without saying much. Four nights later the cracks were closed. He asked where I'd gotten it. I told him: Aunt Pat's kitchen. November.

He ordered a small tin for his work coat.

Mae is eight. She does dishes most nights. Her knuckles had been splitting at the joints the way her dad's do. Five nights of a small dot before bed and they closed up. She held her hands out to show me when they were better, the way kids do with a healed scrape.

When Sam comes in from the chicken coop with the wind-red lips he gets in January, a small dab before his nap.

One jar on the counter. A travel tin in my husband's coat. Four people. Four ingredients.

========

I've been thinking about the October farmers market.

I set the jar down because I had a feed bill. Because I had canning lids. Because the left boot still mostly worked.

All of those things were true. The feed bill was real. But I had been doing this for six years — quietly treating care of myself as the thing that didn't quite fit the budget, the list, the month.

Aunt Pat gave me a jar because she looked at my face and saw that I hadn't gotten around to it. She didn't lecture me. She just set it on the counter.

The body that does all of this work — the milking and the canning and the garden and the kids and the wood and the chickens — it was allowed to be taken care of the whole time. I just hadn't given myself permission.

========

I take care of the animals. I take care of the kids. I take care of the food and the land and the house.

The body that does all of that gets four ingredients before bed.

That's not vanity. That's maintenance.

========

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee. Link below.

Aunt Pat is 76 and still on it. Nancy from church ordered one after that Sunday at the coffee urn. My mother-in-law asked what I was using when she came for Thanksgiving. I've bought Aunt Pat her Christmas jar as a thank you.

I walked past a jar in October because the budget was tight and the boots still mostly worked. Now there's one on my bathroom counter that Aunt Pat gave me, and another on the way for Aunt Pat that I bought for her. That jar had a place in my house the whole time. I just hadn't known it yet.
