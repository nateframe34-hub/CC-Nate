B29's three image prompts specify a **hand-drawn sketch on a napkin, a receipt back, and a paper placemat** — with instructions to fight any "clean infographic" look, and only two words of text ("water" and "fat").

## Avatar choice: SA11 The Shine Fighter

### Why this one

**The whiteboard's power is making a mechanism visible — and it's strongest when the mechanism is counterintuitive.** B19C2's water-vs-fat is *intuitive once stated*: people read it and nod. SA11's mechanism contradicts what the viewer currently believes:

> **Your skin is oily because it's dry. Every mattifying product you use is causing the thing you're using it to fix.**

A diagram that resolves a contradiction earns far more attention than one illustrating an obvious fact. This is the difference between "huh, makes sense" and "wait, *what?*" — and the second one stops scrolling.

Four more reasons:

1. **It's a loop, not a comparison.** Strip → skin panics → makes more oil → you strip again. That's a genuinely different diagram from B19C2's two-column layout, so we're not re-running the same visual with new labels.
2. **It's our only real New Information payload** — the required Stage 3-4 response, and the first one found since fat-vs-water.
3. **SA4 is already covered by B29 with whiteboard.** Repeating it wastes the slot.
4. **There's a bridge to our existing 45+ base:** *"Shiny shows off wrinkles...banish the shine!"* — verbatim from a mature-skin, lifelong-oily buyer. This is not only a young-skin avatar.

### Honest risks

- **Thinnest evidence of the new avatars** — 4 outcome sources plus one esthetician's mechanism explanation. Less validated than SA9.
- **The mechanism rests on a single professional source.** It is a widely-repeated dermatology idea, but our version of it traces to one in-thread quote.
- 🚨 **Stay on SHINE. Never acne.** The same esthetician who supplied the mechanism warns that dense products clog acne-prone skin. That boundary is what makes this batch safe to run at all, and it must hold in every cell.

### 📌 Recommended: pull SA11 out of B31

B31C6 is currently SA11 as a native cell. **Running SA11 in two formats simultaneously confounds both.** Drop C6 from B31 — SA11's best expression is the whiteboard, not a native cell, and B31 falls to 5 cells, which also eases the starvation problem flagged in its own plan.

## Format: keep native. Don't switch to informative long-form.

The founder raised dropping the story for straight informative long-form. **Recommendation: don't**, for four evidenced reasons:

1. **Every converter this account has ever had is a story.** B17C1, B9C3, B5C1, B7C1, B3C2. There is not one informative-long-form converter in the history of the account.
2. **We already tested informative long-form and killed it.** The advertorials (B24/B25) are essentially that format, and they produced 1 click-through across 4 ads before being killed 7/14.
3. **Stage 5 market.** Informative copy is what every brand does; that's the noise. Camouflage is what breaks through it.
4. **B19C2's own feedback loop** found the static alone couldn't reliably carry enough persuasion without a story behind it — which is the entire reason the whiteboard-native hybrid was designed.

**But the founder's instinct is pointing at something real.** The whiteboard image sets an *educational* expectation. Click → they expect an explanation. If the copy is a personal story that takes 60% of its length to reach the mechanism, there's a click-to-copy mismatch.

**The resolution is B29's existing spec, and it's the right one:** a native story whose *purpose* is teaching, with the **mechanism front-loaded to ~25-35%** instead of native's usual 50-60%, delivered through the **diegetic-diagram device** — a character actually draws or shows the thing the ad's image depicts. Not a dry explainer. Not a pure personal story. A story that exists to explain.

## Image spec (all three cells)

**Follow B19C2's construction, not B29's napkin direction.**

Required elements, drawn from what actually won:
- A **real physical whiteboard** with a visible frame, shot straight-on, natural indoor light with a slight sheen on the surface
- **Hand-drawn in multiple dry-erase marker colors**, imperfect lines, flat line-art — *hand-drawn but organized*
- **Semantic color:** one color for the problem state, one for the resolved state, red for failure markers, black for structure
- **Labeled and legible at mobile feed size** — this is an explicit requirement, not a nice-to-have
- **A summary line at the bottom** in the plainest possible language, B19C2's *"Water sits on top. Skin lets fat in. That's it."* being the model
- No brand logos, no product shot, no text overlays outside the drawing

**C1's diagram — the loop** (the batch's core asset):
A circle of four labeled stages with arrows: `STRIP IT` → `SKIN PANICS` → `MAKES MORE OIL` → `SHINY BY LUNCH` → back to `STRIP IT`. Red arrows, the cycle drawn as a closed loop so it visibly has no exit. Then, breaking out of the circle, a single green arrow labeled `PUT THE FAT BACK` leading to `NOTHING TO REPLACE` with a green checkmark. Bottom line: *"Your skin isn't oily. It's compensating."*

The loop's visual argument is that it's **closed** — you can see there's no way out of it without leaving the circle. That's the diagram doing persuasive work rather than decoration, which is the whole point of the format.

C2 and C3 get their own diagrams following the same rules; C2 is a stacked "what each product did" list with red X's, C3 is a light-angle comparison.
