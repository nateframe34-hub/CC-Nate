## Body Copy

I added it up in October. Four hundred and ten dollars in one year, on products whose entire job was to make my face less shiny.

My face was not less shiny.

That's not a complaint about any of them. Every single one of them worked. That turned out to be the problem.

========

I'm 44. Here's the list, because I still have most of it.

A mattifying primer. A mattifying moisturiser. A clay mask, twice a week. A foaming cleanser that squeaked. Blotting papers, the ones in the little booklet, bought roughly monthly. Two setting powders, because I finished one. A charcoal thing that I now cannot account for.

Four hundred and ten dollars. I worked it out on my phone in the car and then sat there for a minute.

========

Every one of those products did exactly what it said it would do.

The primer made me matte. The clay mask pulled oil out of my face, visibly, you could see it working. The squeaky cleanser squeaked, which at the time I took as evidence of quality.

And every one of them bought me about four hours.

By eleven I'd be shining through the primer. The clay mask gave me a tight, clean-feeling evening and a face that was worse the following afternoon than it had been before. I noticed that pattern for probably two years before I let myself think about it.

========

My sister-in-law Kath has skin like mine and had stopped fighting it about a year earlier. I'd assumed she'd just given up and made peace.

I asked her about it at a birthday thing in November, mostly to be polite.

She said, "Have you got a pen?"

There was a little whiteboard on the fridge where they do the shopping list. She wiped it with her sleeve and drew two columns.

========

On the left she wrote **what you're paying for.** Under it: *removes oil.*

On the right she wrote **what actually happens.** And under that she wrote three lines, one under the other, with a red arrow going down between them.

*Oil removed.*
*Skin loses water.*
*Skin makes more oil.*

Then at the bottom of the right-hand column she wrote, in capitals: **YOU BUY MORE.**

She tapped it and said, "You've been buying the second half of that column for four hundred and ten dollars a year."

========

Here's what she explained.

The oil on your face is doing a job. It holds water in. That's the whole reason it's there.

Take it off and the water underneath starts leaving faster. Your skin registers that as a problem and does the only thing it can do about it, which is make more oil. And it doesn't make the same amount back. It overshoots, because it's correcting.

So the product worked, and then it made tomorrow worse, and tomorrow you bought another one.

Kath said, "It's not a scam. It's just a product that solves the surface and causes the cause."

========

I said, so what, you just leave it greasy?

She said no. She said if you give your skin the oil it's trying to make, it stops trying to make it. The panic stops. There's nothing to overcorrect.

She uses tallow. Four ingredients. She'd been on it a year at that point.

I said, "Put fat on an oily face."

She said, "It's close enough in structure to the oil your face is already trying to produce that it goes in instead of sitting on top. I know how it sounds. I said the same thing to the person who told me."

She said to put it on damp, straight after washing, because it absorbs better that way.

========

I ordered one that night. $49.99, free shipping, 60-day guarantee.

I want to be honest about why the price didn't stop me: I'd just worked out I was spending four hundred and ten dollars a year to make it worse. Fifty dollars to test a completely different theory was not a difficult decision. It was arguably the first cheap thing I'd done about this.

========

Night 1, damp face, thirty seconds. I fully expected to wake up slick.

Morning 2, I was not slick. I was also not any different. Just ordinary. I noted it and moved on.

Day 3, shiny by eleven, exactly as usual. I remember thinking, well, that was fifty dollars.

Day 8, I got to about half twelve. I didn't trust it.

Day 14, I got through a whole afternoon meeting and only realised afterwards that I hadn't checked my face in the reflection of my laptop before going in, which I have done before every meeting for years.

Day 22, I ran out of blotting papers and didn't reorder them. Not as a decision. I just didn't get round to it, and then didn't need to.

========

Here's the part I didn't expect.

The thing that stopped it wasn't stronger. It was the opposite of everything I'd bought. Every product on that four-hundred-dollar list was in the business of taking something away, and the one that worked put something back.

I'd been buying more and more aggressive versions of the exact thing that was causing it.

========

I told Kath in January. She asked what I'd spent since November.

Fifty dollars. One jar, still going.

She said, "So that's three hundred and sixty back this year," which is a very Kath way to respond to anything.

========

If you're reading this, I'd guess you could get within about fifty dollars of your own number if you sat down and worked it out.

And I'd guess part of the reason you haven't worked it out is that you already know roughly what it is.

You didn't waste that money. You bought products that did precisely what they advertised. Nobody ever told you what the second half of the column was.

========

I'm not telling you a jar of cow fat is a financial plan. That sounds like an ad. This isn't an ad. I'm telling you my sister-in-law drew two columns on the fridge whiteboard and I stopped buying the second one.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. 60-day guarantee, so if your number keeps climbing you get this fifty back too.

Link is below if you want it.

Kath's still on hers. The clay mask is under my sink and I genuinely don't know what to do with it.

What I want you to have isn't the jar. It's not adding it up again next October.

I thought I was buying solutions.

I was buying the next problem.

## Six Failure Modes Check

1. **Wrong engine** — ✅ Solution-Aware entry, Relatability engine with a financial edge. She tried hard and spent real money; the ad never implies she was foolish.
2. **Describing pain vs twisting** — ✅ Twisted: *"I noticed that pattern for probably two years before I let myself think about it."* Knowing and not looking is the knife.
3. **Knife without permission shift** — ✅ Present, and it directly answers the $410 shame planted in line one. Loop opened at 1% and closed at 87%.
4. **Character break at product reveal** — ✅ No break. Price handled as arithmetic in her own voice, which is the ad's whole register.
5. **Overclaiming** — ✅ Three setbacks reported, including one where she writes the purchase off entirely. Day 8 explicitly withholds trust.
6. **Wrong angle** — ✅ Belief-shifting, and the belief is unusually ready to collapse because she already has a number. The ad supplies the explanation for something she has already emotionally concluded.
