# B14C1 — It Started Burning

**Character type:** Solution-Aware Invisible Woman

## Body Copy

Tuesday night. Same ritual as six years of Tuesday nights.

Three drops of retinol serum onto her fingertips. Same bottle. Same amount. Same motion. She presses it into her cheeks, her forehead, the bridge of her nose.

Her face burns.

Not the tingle she'd pushed through for two years because Marian told her tingle means working. This was burning. Sunburn coming in. Like she'd pressed something wrong.

She went still at the sink and looked at the bottle.

Same bottle. Same serum. Six years. Nothing changed.

She ran a cold washcloth and held it to her left cheek for three minutes.

She thought: something changed. I don't know what.

========

I'm Rebecca. I'm fifty. I've been a paralegal at the same firm in Raleigh for sixteen years. My husband David is an architect. We have two teenagers still at home.

I started retinol at forty-four on my dermatologist's recommendation. She gave me a handout. I read every word. Cell turnover, collagen synthesis, the studies. I was a good student. Every third night, then every other night, then every night. My skin improved. I was glad I'd listened.

Around forty-eight it changed. The tingle became a sting. Not every night — some nights. I dropped to every other night. Told myself sensitive nights happened. Stayed on it because I'd been told this was the right thing to do.

At fifty it started burning.

Not every night at first. Then three nights out of five. Then that Tuesday where I stood at the sink holding a cold cloth to my face, looking at a bottle I'd trusted for six years, and could not explain why it was doing this now.

I didn't open the bottle for five days. That was the longest I'd gone since I started.

========

Marian is fifty-four. She's been an esthetician for twenty-five years — she works at a spa downtown. We've been friends since our daughters were in first grade.

She came over Saturday morning. I showed her my left cheek. The red patch had been there for a week and a half. Dry at the edges. Raw in the middle.

She didn't make a face. She leaned in and looked. She pressed one finger gently to the edge of it.

She said: "Your retinol isn't wrong. Your face just can't hold it anymore."

I said: "My dermatologist says it's the gold standard."

She said: "It is. But gold standards only work if your skin can still back them up. Sit down. I'm going to show you something."

We sat at the kitchen table with our coffee. She took a small amber jar from her bag and set it on the table between us.

========

She said: "Here's what retinol actually does. It makes your skin cells turn over faster. Old cells shed, new cells come up. When you're in your mid-thirties, that's exactly what you want — your skin makes enough of its own oil underneath to protect those new cells while they surface. They arrive soft. Ready."

She said: "After forty your skin stops making that oil. Not a little less — a lot less. It's a real drop. So now retinol is pushing new cells up to the surface, and there's nothing underneath to protect them. They come up raw. They dry out. They burn."

She slid the jar closer to me.

She said: "You've been peeling yourself for two years and calling it a treatment. The retinol isn't doing anything wrong. It's doing exactly what it's supposed to do. Your skin just lost the foundation it needs for retinol to work. What you need isn't stronger treatment. You need the oil back. Then, if you ever want to try retinol again, maybe. But I'll tell you — most of my clients who do this don't go back. Because once the oil is there, most of what the retinol was supposed to fix isn't a problem anymore."

I turned the jar over. Four ingredients on the back. Tallow, honey, olive oil, calendula. That was the whole list.

I said: "Is this what you use on your face."

She said: "It's what I use, and it's what I tell every client who comes in with exactly what you've got on that cheek right now."

I ordered one from my phone before she drove home.

========

Day 1. Saturday night. First use. No burn. No sting. No tingle I had to push through or decide was a good sign. It pressed in the way I'd been told things were supposed to feel — it just went in. I lay there with my palm on my cheek and felt nothing bad happening.

I lay still for a while.

Day 4. Wednesday morning. My face wasn't tight when I got up. I reached for the recovery cream I'd been layering on after retinol nights and then stopped. I wasn't recovering from anything. I put on sunscreen instead. That was a different kind of morning.

Day 9. Tuesday. David came into the bathroom while I was rinsing my face. He stopped in the doorway for a second, looking at me in the mirror. He said: "Your face looks... calm." Not younger. Not better. Calm. That was his word. I looked at my reflection and knew exactly what he meant.

Day 14. Monday. Foundation over my left cheek, where the patch had been. It went on smooth. No flaking underneath, no dry gray patch for it to sit on top of. My coworker Anna said at lunch: "Did you change your makeup?" I said no. She said, "Something." I didn't pursue it.

Day 21. Saturday. Marian came over for coffee again. She sat down across from me. She looked at my face for a moment without saying anything.

She said: "That's what I was talking about."

I said: "I know."

========

For about eighteen months I'd been flinching before I put anything on my face. Not dramatically. A small bracing — shoulders tight, eyes squinting slightly, a half-second of preparation before the sting. It happened before the recovery cream. Before sunscreen. Before anything touched my face at all.

Four weeks in. Saturday morning. SPF in my hand. I pressed it in without thinking.

My shoulders hadn't gone tight.

I stood there and tried to remember the last time they had. A week ago, maybe two. Somewhere in the last month it had just stopped. I hadn't noticed when. I'd just stopped needing to brace.

========

Anna came back around week five. She'd been watching.

She said: "Okay. Tell me what's going on."

I told her. Marian. The esthetician theory. The oil underneath. The jar.

She said: "What about everything you built up with the retinol? Doesn't stopping undo it?"

I said: "I don't know. I haven't opened the bottle since the Tuesday it burned. I haven't looked for it."

She sat with that for a second. Then: "I've been on mine for ten months and I don't love it. Send me the name."

She texted me eleven days later. Two words: "No burn."

========

Tuesday night three months later.

Same bathroom. I was looking for my husband's razor in the cabinet and my hand found the retinol bottle toward the back. I'd pushed it there after the Tuesday it burned. I hadn't moved it since.

I took it down.

I held it the way you hold something you're deciding about.

Six years. Same bottle. Same serum. Same motion.

I held it over the trash can for a moment. Then I opened my fingers.

I left the amber jar on the counter. I didn't need to replace what I'd let go.

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the Tuesday night at the sink when you don't flinch. When you press something into your cheek and it just goes in without anything in you bracing for the sting.

It's the retinol bottle going in the trash because you finally trusted yourself more than you trusted the advice that kept hurting you.
