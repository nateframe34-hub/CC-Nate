## Body Copy

Sunday afternoon in February I was reorganizing the cabinet above the bathroom sink.

I pulled everything out onto the counter to wipe down the shelves. I do this once a year. Dust the shelves, check the expiration dates, put things back in order.

I stepped back and looked at what was on the counter.

Twelve bottles.

I started adding them up. The drugstore retinol, the upgraded dermatologist-recommended one, the vitamin C serum I'd been told to layer under the retinol, the peptide serum I'd added when the retinol started stinging, the barrier repair balm I'd bought when the stinging got worse, the four different creams for "recovery." Two of them were specifically marketed for retinol users. The SPF.

I got to $680 before I stopped adding.

I was looking at $680 of skincare.

And I still reapply foundation at lunch.

========

I'm Pat. Fifty-three. I've been teaching middle school math in Madison for nineteen years. My husband Glenn is an engineer at the university. Our son lives in Chicago now.

I started with a drugstore retinol five years ago. Thirty dollars. My dermatologist mentioned it in passing — not a prescription, just a suggestion. It made sense to me. I'm good at following logic. Skin cells turn over. Retinol speeds that up. Results in a month.

The results did come, for about a year. Then my skin got used to it, or so I read, and I needed something stronger. I upgraded to the $85 dermatologist-recommended one. Then I read I needed a vitamin C serum underneath to "optimize" it. So I bought that. Then my skin started stinging on retinol nights, and I read that a peptide serum before application could buffer it. So I added that. Then my skin was reacting more than it used to, and I read that a barrier repair balm in the morning could help recovery. Two of those now.

Twelve products. Each one bought to either enhance the retinol or survive it.

And I still reapply foundation at lunch.

========

Trish visited the weekend after the shelf moment. She's my husband's sister. Fifty-six. She and her husband run a cattle operation three hours south of us.

She came to wash her hands before dinner, walked into the bathroom, and stopped.

All twelve bottles were still on the counter. I'd put them back but hadn't reorganized yet.

She looked at them for a moment.

She said: "Patty. I had three of those bottles. I threw them all out last spring."

I said: "What are you using now."

She walked to the guest room and came back with her overnight bag. She reached in and set a small amber jar on the edge of the sink.

That was what she'd brought for three days away from home.

One jar.

========

At dinner Glenn watched Trish's hands move while she explained it. She talks with her hands when she's getting into something. He said later he couldn't stop watching her face while she described it.

She said: "Here's the thing about retinol that nobody explains to you. It makes your skin cells turn over faster. Old cells shed, new cells come up. That's the whole point of it. The problem is it only works the way it's supposed to if your skin has enough of its own oil underneath — to protect those new cells while they surface, to keep them from drying out."

She said: "After forty, your skin stops making that oil. Drops way off. So you're running retinol on a face that doesn't have the oil it needs to handle it. Retinol pushes new cells up. Those cells come up raw. They sting. They burn. You buy a barrier balm. You buy a recovery cream. You're trying to patch up the damage retinol is doing because there's not enough oil underneath to protect you from your own treatment."

She looked at the counter.

She said: "The girl at the feed store in Baraboo — her mom had my exact thing. She told me about this. Tallow. Same kind of fat your skin makes on its own. One jar. Four ingredients. I threw out ten products and I haven't bought a thing since. Not one."

She said: "You've been buying products to help the retinol. You don't need any of that. You need the oil back."

Glenn said: "So the retinol was making the problem."

Trish said: "The retinol was working fine. The skin just didn't have what it needed for retinol to work. Two different things."

I ordered one before we finished dessert.

========

Day 1. Sunday night. No retinol. No serum underneath, no recovery balm after. Just the jar. It pressed in without sting, without my skin pushing back. I sat on the edge of the tub for a minute wondering what I was supposed to do now that the nighttime ritual took thirty seconds.

Day 5. Friday. I woke up and my face didn't feel tight. I stood at the mirror considering skipping foundation that morning. I put it on anyway — habit, mainly. But I thought about it.

Day 9. Tuesday dinner. Glenn set down his fork and looked at me across the table. He said: "You look rested." He asked it like a question, the way you ask something when you're not sure if you're imagining it. I said I hadn't slept any differently.

Day 14. Full school day. I teach five periods and stand for most of them. I always check foundation at lunch — I've done it for years, same as bathroom cabinet inventory, just something I do. Monday at noon I went to the staff bathroom and looked. Still there. No cracking along my lip line. No dry patches at my temples. I stood there longer than I needed to.

Day 21. Sunday. I opened the bathroom cabinet to get Glenn's dental floss. I looked at the shelf.

I went and got a trash bag.

I threw out nine of the twelve bottles. The three retinols, both serums, both recovery balms, the SPF I'd bought to layer over the recovery balm. Into the bag.

I left the toothpaste, the mouthwash, and the amber jar.

I wiped the shelf.

Closed the cabinet.

========

I'd been browsing Sephora and Ulta on my phone almost every day for three years. Not always buying — just looking. New launches. Reviews. Something that might be the thing I was missing. I'd started doing it in my lunch break between third and fourth period. Twenty minutes of scrolling product pages while I ate.

About a month in I realized I hadn't opened either app in weeks. I wasn't avoiding them. I just hadn't thought to.

I checked my screen time. The last time I'd opened Sephora was twenty-seven days ago.

I put my phone down. I thought about what I'd been looking for.

I'd found it in an overnight bag.

========

Trish called in March to ask how it was going.

I told her about the shelf.

She laughed for a long time. She said: "Glenn texted Dennis after dinner and said 'Pat ordered it, whatever happens I want credit.'"

I told her about the lunch check. Foundation still there at noon.

She said: "That's the thing I forgot to tell you. You stop checking. You stop adding more things. You just stop."

I said: "I stopped going to Sephora."

She said: "Oh, I know. I haven't been in a Tractor Supply beauty aisle in eight months."

She was kidding. Mostly.

========

Next Sunday afternoon.

Same bathroom cabinet. I pull everything out onto the counter to wipe down the shelves.

I count what's there.

Three.

Toothpaste. Mouthwash. One amber jar.

I wipe the shelf. I put the three back. I throw away the cleaning wipe. I close the cabinet.

I go out to the kitchen and start lunch.

I don't think about it again.

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the shelf with three things on it instead of twelve.

It's the $680 you'll never spend again — not because someone told you to stop, but because you don't need to keep adding.

It's the Sunday afternoon when you open the cabinet, wipe the shelf, put three things back, and close the door.

And not think about it again.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. Sixty-day money-back. You email them, you get every dollar back.

Link is below.

The shelf is clean.
