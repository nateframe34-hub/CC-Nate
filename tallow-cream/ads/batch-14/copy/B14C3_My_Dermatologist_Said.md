## Body Copy

Thursday. Fourth time in my dermatologist's office in twelve months.

She looked at my face under the light. I told her what I'd been telling her for a year — the tretinoin was burning me now. Not stinging the way it used to, not the tingle she'd told me meant it was working. Burning. Visible redness. Dry patches that wouldn't recover. I was using it every other night and my skin looked worse than when I started.

She shrugged.

She said: "That's normal at your age. Skin gets more sensitive. We can cut the dose."

I sat there.

I said: "We cut the dose six months ago."

She said: "We can cut it again."

I walked out to my car and sat in the parking lot for ten minutes.

I was thinking about the fact that the person who recommended the thing that was burning my face was telling me the burning was normal. And offering to give me less of the thing that was burning my face as the solution.

I sat in the car for a long time.

========

I'm Lauren. I'm forty-eight. I'm a nurse practitioner at a pediatric clinic in Charlotte. I've been there eleven years. My husband Mike is a high school principal. Our daughter is in college. Our son is still at home.

I trusted dermatology because I work in medicine. I understand how clinical recommendations are made. I know what evidence-based practice means. When my dermatologist prescribed tretinoin three years ago, I read everything I could find. The studies were there. The mechanism was clear. I went in with my eyes open.

It worked. For about a year it worked. Then around forty-six it started stinging on application nights. I adjusted — dropped to every other night, the way she'd told me to if irritation increased. At forty-seven it started burning. Not every time. Then most of the time. I kept going because I'd been told by someone with an MD to keep going.

At forty-eight I was on my fourth office visit in a year, being told the burning was normal and less of the burning thing was the answer.

I drove home from that appointment without turning on the radio.

I knew I was done with it. I didn't know what else there was.

========

Denise has been a pediatric nurse at our clinic for twenty-eight years. She's fifty-one. She's seen more than most of the attending physicians. She says what she thinks without making a big deal of saying it.

Two days after the dermatologist appointment I was in the break room before my first patient applying aloe vera to my left cheek. I'd been doing this between patients. Aloe, cool water, aloe again.

Denise came in for coffee.

She said: "What's on your face?"

I told her. Tretinoin. Three years. The burn. The parking lot.

She put her mug down.

She said: "My mother-in-law. Exact same thing. Doctors said the same thing — cut the dose, skin is getting more sensitive, that's normal at your age. She went a different way. I'll text you the name."

I said: "Is it something I should know about medically?"

She said: "Four ingredients. Beef fat, honey, olive oil, and calendula. There's nothing to know."

========

We ate lunch together the next day. She walked me through it.

She said: "Your dermatologist is right that tretinoin is the gold standard. The studies are real. Skin cell turnover is a real mechanism. None of that is wrong."

She said: "What she's wrong about is that your skin can still tolerate it. Here's why it can't. Skin makes its own oil — that's how it protects itself. After forty, it stops making much. A real drop. And tretinoin requires your skin to have enough oil underneath to protect the new cells it's turning over. The cells come up, they need oil to keep them from drying out, the oil isn't there. So they dry out and they burn. You can cut the dose. You can cut it again. If the oil isn't there, every dose is too high."

She said: "My mother-in-law's dermatologist told her to just keep going with a lower dose. She spent six months doing that. Then a friend told her about this balm. She stopped the prescription. Her skin healed in a month. I saw her face at Christmas. She's seventy-two. Her skin looks better than mine."

She said: "She's not on any prescription now. She's on one jar."

She pulled up her phone and showed me the product page. Four ingredients. Forty-nine ninety-nine.

I said: "So she replaced a prescription with this."

Denise said: "She replaced a prescription that was hurting her with something that didn't. Yeah."

I ordered it before I drove home.

========

Friday night I put it on instead of the tretinoin.

No burn. No sting. No application tingle I had to decide to push through. It pressed into my face the way water presses into dry ground — quick, even, nothing left behind. I lay in bed and realized I'd been bracing for an application sensation for three years.

Day 4. Sunday morning. My face didn't hurt. I said that to myself out loud at the bathroom mirror, quietly. My face doesn't hurt. I hadn't said that sentence in two years.

Day 9. Wednesday. Mike looked up from his coffee at breakfast. He said: "Is the red going away?" He asked it like it was a fact he was checking, not a compliment. I said yes. He said: "Good." He went back to his coffee. He hadn't said anything about my skin in months.

Day 14. Denise walked into the break room after my eleven o'clock patient. She stopped. She looked at my face under the break room fluorescents — bad light, no mercy.

She said: "It's working, isn't it."

Not a question.

Day 21. I had a partial tretinoin tube left — a prescription I'd paid $80 for. I stood in the bathroom holding it. I thought about the parking lot. The fourth visit in a year. The shrug.

I put the tube in the trash.

========

For over two years I'd been reading r/SkincareAddiction on my break. Every day, sometimes twice. Looking for what I was missing — the ingredient someone had discovered, the order of application I was getting wrong, the product that would make the tretinoin finally work the way it had at the beginning.

A Wednesday in March, a month after I started using the jar, I realized I hadn't opened the app in about three weeks. I hadn't been avoiding it. I just hadn't thought about it.

I checked my phone. Twenty-one days.

I thought about what I'd been looking for on Reddit every day for two years.

I hadn't been there since I stopped looking.

========

Six months later I had my annual physical. My GP — not the dermatologist, a different doctor — looked at my face as part of the general check. She said: "Your skin looks great. Are you still on that tretinoin?"

I said no.

She said: "Did you switch to something else?"

I said yes.

She said: "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."

I sat there thinking about my dermatologist and the parking lot and the shrug. The fourth visit in a year. The offer to cut the dose again.

I never made another appointment with the dermatologist.

I didn't need to.

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the GP looking at your face at your annual physical and telling you whatever you're doing is working — and you not needing the specialist anymore to tell you that.

It's the prescription tube in the trash because you finally trusted what your own face was telling you over what the prescription pad said.

It's the three weeks you didn't open Reddit looking for the next thing — because the thing was already working and there was nothing left to look for.

Seventy-two. Better than mine at forty-eight.

I believe it now.
