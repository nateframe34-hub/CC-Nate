## Body Copy

My coworker Maya caught me reapplying lotion at my desk for the third time before lunch and said, "Doesn't it seem insane that we do this every single day and it still doesn't hold?"

I didn't have an answer. I just laughed and kept rubbing it in.

========

Here's what my mornings looked like before any of this. Lotion on at seven, before I even left the house. By ten it felt thin. By noon it was gone, tight at the corners of my mouth, that specific dry pull when I smiled. Reapply. Same thing by three.

Three times a day. Every day. For years. I'd just stopped noticing it as a routine, it was just what a day was.

I did notice it once, in a bathroom mirror before a client call, catching my own reflection mid-flinch at how tight my face looked under the fluorescent light. I fixed it in thirty seconds and walked back out like nothing happened. Nobody in that meeting knew. I did.

========

Maya asked if she could show me something on my lunch break. She pulled a napkin out of her bag and drew two circles.

"This one's water," she said, tapping the first circle. "This one's fat." She tapped the second.

"Water evaporates. That's it, that's the whole thing nobody tells you. You put it on, and by the time you've had two cups of coffee, most of it's already left. Not because your skin failed. Because that's just what water does."

She drew a little arrow rising off the first circle. "Gone by ten."

Then she drew a flat line under the second circle. "Fat doesn't do that. It gets absorbed instead of sitting on top waiting to evaporate. Skin treats it like the oil it already makes on its own. Because structurally, it basically is."

I looked at the napkin for a second too long.

========

"Wait," I said. "So every lotion I've ever owned. Water first."

"Flip the bottle," she said. "Every one. Water, then glycerin, then something you can't pronounce."

I went home and flipped every bottle in my bathroom that night. She was right about every single one.

========

She'd switched eight months earlier. Four ingredients, she said, tallow being the one doing the actual work.

I said, "Cow fat. On my face."

She said, "I know how it sounds. I said the same thing. Just try it for two weeks."

I ordered one that night. $49.99. Free shipping. I was skeptical, honestly, but for that price it felt worth finding out, and there was a 60-day guarantee if it didn't work out.

========

Day 1, I put it on before bed. It melted into my palm like warm honey, thirty seconds, no fighting it in.

Day 4, I applied once in the morning and, out of habit, reached for the tube at my desk around eleven. My skin didn't feel tight yet. I put the tube back.

Day 9, I made it through an entire workday, three coffees, one long meeting, without reapplying once.

Day 14, I realized at three that I hadn't thought about my skin all day. Not once.

Day 21, I opened my desk drawer looking for a pen and found the backup tube still sitting exactly where I'd left it, untouched, for three weeks.

========

Here's the part I didn't expect. I hadn't decided to stop carrying backup. I'd just stopped needing it, and it sat there for weeks before I even noticed I hadn't reached for it once.

That was the actual thing I wanted. Not softer skin on its own. The not-thinking-about-it. A whole day going by without my face asking for anything.

========

Maya asked me at the end of week five what I was still doing differently, since she'd noticed I'd stopped doing our shared reapplying-at-our-desks thing.

I told her I hadn't reapplied in a month. She just nodded like she already knew.

A few weeks ago someone in a meeting asked what my skincare routine was, fishing for a recommendation.

"Nothing," I said. "That's kind of the whole thing now."

========

If you're reading this at your own desk with a tube sitting somewhere within reach right now, you already know the exact time it starts pulling. You've probably checked a mirror today and fixed something quietly, the way I used to, before walking back out like nothing happened.

========

I'm not telling you a jar of cow fat changes your life. That sounds like an ad. This isn't an ad. I'm telling you about a napkin with two circles drawn on it, and a desk drawer tube I haven't touched in a month.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. If nothing's different in six weeks, there's a 60-day guarantee, you email them and get every dollar back, nothing to send back first.

Link is below if you want it.

Maya's still on it. My backup tube is still sitting in that drawer, untouched.

What I want you to have isn't the jar. It's the day you stop thinking about your own face. The whole afternoon going by without the tightness at ten, without the pull at the corners of your mouth by two.

I'd stopped believing a normal day like that was still possible.

Now it just is.

## Image Prompt (Nano Banana Pro), IN DEPTH

**Type:** Bold hand-drawn whiteboard-style diagram, diegetically tied to the story, this IS the napkin drawing Maya makes in the ad copy, not a disconnected graphic.
**Visual family:** same as B19C2 (colored markers, simple line-art, mechanism made visible), NOT a photo, NOT a produced/polished illustration.

**Critical constraint, read before generating:** AI image generators default to clean, symmetrical, "explainer video" style diagrams when asked for a "diagram" or "whiteboard style," that is the WRONG look and needs to be actively fought in the prompt, not just requested once. The whole point of this image is that it reads as something a real person scratched out by hand in under a minute, not a designed graphic. If the output looks like a polished infographic, vector art, an app screenshot, or anything with perfectly straight lines/even spacing/a clean sans-serif font, it's wrong, regenerate with the imperfection constraints pushed harder.

```
A bold, hand-drawn diagram on a slightly crumpled paper napkin, photographed from directly above on a plain café or office table, as if sketched quickly mid-conversation with an ordinary ballpoint pen or blue marker. NOT a professional illustration, NOT a vector graphic, NOT a clean infographic, NOT anything that looks designed or produced. Two circles drawn freehand, visibly imperfect and slightly lopsided, not geometrically round, drawn with the small wobbles and pen-pressure variation of an actual human hand mid-conversation, not a smooth even line. One circle labeled "water" in messy, uneven, slightly slanted handwriting (not a font), with a small hand-drawn arrow rising off it toward a rough sketch of a clock face reading a losing time. The other circle labeled "fat" in the same rough handwriting, with a flat, slightly wavy hand-drawn line beneath it. Visible pen-stroke inconsistency, some lines darker where the pen pressed harder, minor smudging consistent with a real napkin sketch, maybe a crossed-out false start or a retraced line where the sketcher went over a shape twice, the kind of small imperfection that only exists in genuine handwriting, never in generated "hand-drawn style" clip art. A ballpoint pen resting on the napkin, the edge of a coffee cup and a second hand (not writing, just resting near the napkin) visible at the edge of frame, unstaged, candid. Warm, ordinary indoor lighting, real photo grain, no studio gloss, no even lighting gradient, this needs to look like an actual photo of an actual napkin someone actually drew on, not marketing material simulating that idea. No brand logos, no product shown in this image, no readable text beyond the simple "water" / "fat" labels and the rough clock sketch. Square or 4:5 (1080 x 1080 or 1080 x 1350).
```
