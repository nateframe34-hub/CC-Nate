## 1. The Strategic Job (why this ad exists)

B7C1 ("She Used Pond's") is the **highest-sales ad in the account.** The generational-mirror frame (mom used one simple jar and had better skin) is the strongest recognition trigger we've found. But B7C1 has a documented ceiling: comment mining found **3 of 5 comments were Pond's-objection variants** — "this is just expensive Pond's," "why not just buy Pond's?" The ad opens a logical door it never closes. The reader walks away thinking she can get the result for $8 at the drugstore.

**This ad's single job: close that door, in scene, before the reader can raise it.**

The mechanism for closing it is **formula drift** — the simple oil/fat-based creams that worked for our mothers got replaced on the shelf by water-based lotions. You cannot "just rebuy 1982." The thing that worked isn't what's in the bottle anymore. Tallow is the return to the *kind of thing* that worked, not a fancy version of the cheap thing.

**Secondary job (founder's explicit ask): build PRODUCT awareness.** B7C1 and B9C3 are written for a Solution-Aware reader (she knows skin is the problem, knows tallow is a category). This ad does the same but uses the formula-drift revelation to walk her one rung up the ladder to **Product Aware** — she leaves understanding *why the specific category of thing (fat-based) is the answer* and why the cheaper alternatives she'd reach for won't deliver. The education IS the objection-closer. Same beat does both jobs.

## 2. Sub-Sub-Avatar: "The Rebuyer"

**The specific shared experience:** She tried to go back to the simple thing that used to work — bought the cream her mother used, or the one she herself used at 25 — and it **didn't work like she remembered.** She assumed it was her (her skin, her age). It wasn't. The formula changed underneath her.

Why this sub-sub-avatar is the right vehicle:
- **It's a real, common, emotionally-loaded moment.** Nearly every woman 50+ has rebought a nostalgic product and felt the quiet letdown of "this isn't how I remember it."
- **It does double duty.** The inciting incident (rebuy → disappointment) IS the gap-closer. By the time the reader thinks "why not just buy the cheap one," the narrator has already tried that on screen and it failed. The objection is pre-empted structurally, not argued.
- **It's distinct from B7C1.** B7C1's inciting incident is *finding a photo* (passive nostalgia). This is *rebuying and being let down* (active investigation). Different scroll-stop, different audience-pool, no cannibalization.
- **It naturally forces the product-awareness question:** "Why didn't it work this time?" → the answer is the whole mechanism.

**Pool size check (Principle 3 Q1):** large. Anyone who's repurchased a remembered drugstore staple. Bigger than "Pond's daughter" specifically.

## 3. Awareness Arc (the product-awareness move)

| Stage | Where the reader is |
|---|---|
| **Opens at:** Solution Aware | She knows her skin changed, has tried things, knows "simple/natural" is supposedly better. |
| **Mid-ad pivot:** the drift revelation | "The thing that worked got replaced by water" reframes her entire shelf and her failed rebuy. New information. |
| **Closes at:** Product Aware | She now understands the *category* she actually needs (a fat close to what skin makes), why drugstore water-creams and even the rebought nostalgia jar can't deliver it, and that tallow is the specific return to that category. |

This is the lever Nate asked for. The ad is not just recognition ("that's me") — it installs an understanding she carries to the PDP, which is what makes a Product-Aware reader convert at a higher rate than a Solution-Aware one.

## 4. The Core Reframe (formula/category drift) — and the compliance rails

**The idea, stated plainly:** Mass-market "creams" drifted from oil/fat bases to water + glycerin + filler bases over the decades — cheaper to make, lighter feel, better margins. What made skin soft then (the fat) mostly isn't what's in the bottle now (the water). So going back to the cheap nostalgic jar doesn't reproduce the old result, because the cheap nostalgic jar isn't the old formula either.

**THE THREE RAILS THAT KEEP THIS COMPLIANT AND DEFENSIBLE (do not violate):**

1. **Drift is CATEGORY-LEVEL and NARRATOR-OBSERVED, never a named-brand reformulation accusation.** Do NOT write "Pond's secretly changed their recipe to cheap out." That's (a) a factual claim about a named competitor we can't substantiate and (b) disparagement that gets sharper at scale. Instead: the narrator *flips the bottle over and reads the label herself* — "water, glycerin, then something I can't pronounce." She observes water is first. That's true, observable, hers. The drift is framed as something "the companies" / "what's on the shelf now" did, generically.

2. **The enemy is WATER vs FAT, never "chemicals."** Formula-drift has a strong gravitational pull toward "they put a bunch of chemicals in it now" — which is **banned clean-beauty language** (Principle 6: no "chemical-free," "toxin-free," "all-natural"). Stay strictly on the functional water-vs-fat mechanism. Water evaporates; fat absorbs. That's the whole argument. Never "natural is safer," never "chemicals are bad."

3. **No health/safety claim about old vs new formula.** "The old kind was better for your skin because it's the right kind of fat" = fine (functional). "The old kind was safer / the new chemicals harm you" = banned health claim. The drift made creams *stop working*, not *become dangerous*.

**Reconciliation with the May-11 decision** ("don't center the ad on a Pond's comparison, direct competitive rebuttal amplifies the objection"): this ad does NOT attack Pond's or position as "better than Pond's." The enemy is the category's drift to water, and the resolution is a *return to the original kind of thing* — a positive, mechanism-led frame, exactly what the May-11 decision prescribed. The comparison is to "what creams used to be," not to a competitor brand.

## 5. The Gap-Closer (the load-bearing beat)

This is the beat that repairs B7C1. It must be unmistakable:

> She rebought the simple jar she remembered. She used it for two weeks. Her skin was still tight by noon. She thought it was her — her age, her skin finally too far gone. Then [discovery character] told her to flip it over. Water. First ingredient. *"That's not what your mom was using. That's the new version. They all went to water."*

That single exchange means the reader can never land on "just buy the cheap one" — the narrator already ran that experiment and it failed for a reason the reader now understands. **Everything in the ad serves setting up and paying off this beat.**

## 6. Beat-by-Beat Skeleton (~1,500 words, all 10 elements mapped)

Scene breaks = `========`. Element numbers refer to Principle 4's 10-element converter skeleton.

**BEAT 1 — Hook scene (60-100 words).** External-noticing + the rebuy letdown. Open on a concrete moment: she's at the bathroom mirror two weeks into the rebought jar, foundation already cracking at the mouth by early afternoon, thinking *it didn't work, it's me.* Name the rebought product generically/visually (the white jar, the thing from the drugstore endcap she remembered from her mom's counter). The scroll-stop is the quiet humiliation of "I went back to the thing that worked and it failed." *(Element 1 external noticing is staged slightly differently here — see note below; Element 9 accidental-discovery-of-the-problem.)*

> Note on Element 1: classic skeleton opens on *someone else* noticing the improvement. Here the cold-open is the narrator's letdown, and the external noticing comes at the discovery + the day-markers + close. Keep at least TWO external-witness beats later (discovery friend who clocks her skin, plus a husband/peer close) so Element 1 + Element 7 are satisfied across the arc. Do not let the narrator be the only one who ever notices.

**BEAT 2 — Narrator context (150-200 words).** Name, age (52-55), location, occupation, family. Brief skin history: skin changed in her late 40s, she's tried the expensive shelf (cleanser/serum/retinol-that-burns/etc. — the same 10-product inventory motif). The fresh wrinkle vs B7C1: she got *tired of the expensive shelf* and decided to go back to basics — the simple thing mom used. That decision is what sets up the rebuy. *(Element 9.)*

**BEAT 3 — The rebuy + the letdown (150-200 words).** She buys the nostalgic jar. The memory of her mother using it (decades-proof: mom used one simple jar for years and had soft, rested skin at 52 — the generational mirror, carried over from B7C1 because it's the proven trigger). She presses it in the way mom did. Two weeks. Nothing holds. Tight by noon. She concludes it's her. **This is the trap being set for the gap-closer.** *(Decades-proof authority via the mother; Element 3 peer-adjacent authority partly seeded here.)*

**BEAT 4 — Discovery scene (200-250 words).** Discovery character: a named peer who worked a department-store cosmetics counter in the '80s or '90s (LOCKED 2026-06-20). Specific physical setting (her kitchen, a walk, coffee). The narrator mentions she went back to the old jar and it didn't work. The friend tells her to flip it over. Her counter-worker history is the natural authority for the drift: "I saw what they put in everything when I worked the Clinique counter. It all went to water and glycerin." NO current credentials, NO expert title — lay authority only. **The gap-closer beat (§5) lands here.** *(Element 3 named peer-adjacent authority; Element 4 mechanism delivered BY the discovery character, not the narrator.)*

**BEAT 5 — Mechanism / the drift explained (150-200 words).** Discovery character delivers the why, in plain peer language: skin makes its own oil; after ~40 it makes much less; the oil skin makes is a *fat*, close to animal fat; creams used to be oil/fat-based and worked because they replaced what skin lost; "then everything went to water" — cheaper, lighter, but water evaporates by noon; that's why the rebought jar (the new water version) failed, and why the expensive shelf failed too. The drift reframes BOTH her failures at once. Then the bridge: tallow is a fat close to what skin makes, so it absorbs in instead of sitting on top — the return to the *kind of thing* that worked. *(Element 4; trust ladder; mechanism identical to B7C1/B9C3 — the PROVEN explainer, do not reinvent.)*

**BEAT 6 — Skeptic-convert micro-beat (within/after Beat 5, ~40-60 words).** "Tallow. Beef fat. On my face." Narrator makes the face. Friend: "I made the same face. Your mom's cream was animal-fat-adjacent too. Was that complicated?" First-person skepticism, resolved by tying back to the trusted mother frame. *(Element from B7C1 that worked — keep it.)*

**BEAT 7 — First use + day markers (250-300 words).** Day 1 (melts in like warm butter, pressed in the way mom did — callback), Day 4 (underneath-soft touch test), Day 9 (external witness #1 — coworker/friend clocks her skin, "are you sleeping better?"), Day 14 (foundation holds flat at 2pm, the thing that had failed in the cold-open — direct payoff of Beat 1), Day 21 (peer/family beat). Specific days, specific moments. *(Element 5 progressive day-marker transformation; Element 2 skin-specific noticing language in the witness lines.)*

**BEAT 8 — Habit-cessation beat (100-150 words).** The unexpected thing she stopped doing without noticing. Must be FUNCTIONAL, not vanity (Principle 4 element 6). Fresh one (don't reuse B9C3's travel packets or B7C1's reaching-for-HA verbatim). Candidate: **she stopped rebuying / stopped standing at the drugstore endcap reading labels** — fits the Rebuyer avatar perfectly (the behavior that opened the ad is the one that quietly ends). Or: stopped doing the noon foundation-touch-up in the work bathroom. Pick the rebuy-cessation — it closes the avatar's loop. *(Element 6.)*

**BEAT 9 — Household/social expansion (100-150 words).** Others on it now — the discovery friend still using it, maybe the narrator's sister or daughter asks for the name. Light social proof. *(Element 5 external validation; sets up Element 7.)*

**BEAT 10 — Close (200-250 words).** Mirrors the hook with inversion: opened on the rebought jar failing and her thinking *it's me*; closes on the understanding that it was never her — the thing that worked had been replaced by water, and she's now using the actual kind of thing that works. Identity-level payoff (Element 8) — NOT "soft skin." Something like: *she'd spent two years thinking her face had finally gone past the point of help; it hadn't, she'd just been handed water and told it was cream.* Then the product line: four ingredients, $49.99 (verify against live offer — currently compare-at $69.99→$49.99; see HANDOFF), free shipping, 60-day money-back guarantee, low-pressure "this isn't an ad" register. Final callback to the mother: she did the right thing without knowing why; the narrator does it now and knows why. *(Element 7 close mirrors hook; Element 8 identity payoff.)*

## 7. Hook + Image Concept

**Hook line direction (Meta primary text first line):** lead with the rebuy letdown, not the photo. e.g. *"I bought the same cream my mother used. I used it for two weeks. My skin was still tight by noon, and I figured that was just it for me."* — recognition + open loop + the trap, in one breath.

**Image (Principle 2 + 9):** first-person POV, iPhone-snapshot quality, looking down at her own hand holding the rebought nostalgic jar (white drugstore jar, slightly generic — recognizable as "the kind mom used" WITHOUT a clearly readable competitor logo, per the no-named-brand-accusation rail), on a real bathroom counter with the expensive-shelf products blurred behind it. Real bathroom lighting, slightly off-center, one-handed. Reads like a photo she'd text her sister: *"I rebought Mom's cream and I don't think it's the same anymore."* 4:5, 1080×1350.

> Image compliance note: if using an actual Pond's-style jar for recognition, keep any logo non-readable/ambiguous so the image isn't asserting a specific brand reformulation. The recognition we want is "the kind of simple jar from the drugstore," not "Pond's specifically did X."

## 9. Compliance Landmines Specific to This Concept (checklist before writing)

- [ ] Drift framed category-level + narrator-observed (label flip), NOT a named-brand reformulation accusation.
- [ ] Zero "chemical," "toxin," "natural," "clean," "chemical-free" — water-vs-fat ONLY.
- [ ] Zero health/safety claim about old vs new formula — it stopped *working*, it didn't become *harmful*.
- [ ] Competitor jar logo non-readable in the image.
- [ ] Mechanism delivered by the discovery character, narrator only observes (Element 4 / 10).
- [ ] Standard banned-phrase sweep (menopause, anti-aging, moisturizer/serum/routine, spa/clean-beauty, cures/treats/heals).
- [ ] No em-dashes (locked 2026-05-11).
- [ ] Verify the price line against the live offer at write-time (compare-at $69.99→$49.99 as of 2026-06-20).

## 11. Founder Decisions (LOCKED 2026-06-20)

1. **Brand naming: 3-CELL TEST (LOCKED 2026-06-20).** Three cells, one naming variable:
   - **Cell A:** Names brands (Pond's, Olay) — generational hook, mom's specific products.
   - **Cell B:** Generic nostalgic — "the simple cream my mother used," no brand named. Same generational entry point as A, naming is the only variable.
   - **Cell C:** Generic own-shelf — "my old skincare products," her own expensive failed shelf. Different entry point entirely (personal frustration vs generational trust), no brands named.
   - B vs C tests whether the objection-closer lands better through the mother's world or her own failed shelf. Same discovery character, mechanism, and skeleton across all three.
   - Compliance posture: health claims are the hard line ("their product will burn your skin" = banned). Naming brands in a category-drift frame is acceptable. Do not assert any named product is harmful — only that the category drifted from what made it work.
2. **Inciting incident: REBUYER.** She rebuys the old cream, it fails. (Photo-finder is already B7C1 — this stays distinct.)
3. **Discovery character: FRIEND WHO WORKED A COSMETICS COUNTER YEARS AGO.** Gives lay-level drift credibility ("I saw what they put in everything when I worked the counter at [department store] in the '90s") without any current credential. Natural authority, not a professional endorsement.
