## Body Copy

I was in Renee's chair on a Thursday afternoon in March. She's been cutting my hair since I was forty. I'm fifty-two now. She had the color brush in her hand and she was about to paint the first section when she stopped and leaned in.

She said, "What happened to your skin?"

She's been looking at my face under those lights for twelve years. She doesn't ask about my skin. Ever.

========

My name is Karen. I live in Denver. I have a son in his second year of law school and a daughter in her last year of college. My husband works in commercial real estate. I'm an office manager at a civil engineering firm, same job for fifteen years, same desk.

I go to Renee every six weeks. Highlights and a cut. Same appointment I've had on the books since 2014. She knows my face better than anyone who isn't my husband. She sees it from six inches away in the worst possible lighting — fluorescent overhead, no windows, mirror behind me, mirror in front of me. Any hairdresser will tell you the salon is where you see a face for what it actually is. No filter. No evening light. No mercy.

For the last two years I'd been dreading those appointments. I'd sit in her chair and see myself and look away. I'd catch her looking at my face in the mirror and I'd catch myself thinking she was noticing what I was noticing. The skin at my temples going papery. The crease between my eyebrows that doesn't go away when I stop frowning. The dry patch on my chin that foundation would never cover, just sit on.

========

I'd tried everything. I won't list it all because you already know the list. Drugstore. Then mid-range. Then the retinol serum my sister-in-law swore by. Then the $140 jar my hairdresser — Renee, the same Renee — had recommended once, years ago, back when her own skin was different.

Every one of them did the same thing. Felt nice for a week. Then my face went back to tight and dry by ten in the morning.

My doctor told me it was normal. Estrogen decline. "Your skin is going to keep changing."

I stopped looking in the mirror unless I had to. I took pictures of my kids on my phone with the camera pointed away from me. Our family trip to the Grand Canyon last summer has forty-one photos on my phone. I'm in two of them.

========

Renee finished the first section, pulled off her glove, and held up one finger. "Wait here."

She walked to her station and came back with a small amber jar. She set it on the tray next to her color bowl.

She said, "My hands. Look at my hands."

I looked at her hands. I'd been looking at her hands every six weeks for twelve years. Hairdressers' hands are the worst hands in the working world. Red. Cracked at the knuckles. The skin between her thumb and finger raw from the bleach and the dye and the water. Every Renee I've ever known has had those hands.

Her hands looked like someone else's hands.

She said, "Started using this on my hands eight months ago. Cleared it in a week. Then I put it on my face. Then I started watching other women's faces. Yours changed. It got tight. I wasn't going to say anything. But I've been waiting to tell you for about three months and today I just did."

========

She put the color bowl down and told me the rest while she set my timer.

She said, "Your skin makes its own oil. That's how skin works. After forty your body makes way less of it. A lot less. Every cream in every jar you buy is water and glycerin and something to thicken it. That's why it sits on top for an hour and then your face feels tight again. The fat in this balm is almost the same structure as the oil your skin stopped making. Your face absorbs it like it's supposed to be there."

I said, "Where did you get it?"

She said, "The same place I'd tell anyone else to get it. I wouldn't send you somewhere I wouldn't send my mother."

She wrote something down on the back of a color-card. Four ingredients. Tallow. Honey. Olive oil. Calendula.

I read the card in my car before I drove home.

========

I used it that night.

It didn't feel greasy. It didn't feel like a cream. It rubbed in the way the skin on the back of your hand absorbs sweat — it just went somewhere.

Day 4. I woke up and my face didn't feel tight. I touched my cheek lying there in bed and the skin felt softer. I didn't trust it yet. I went to the bathroom. Looked. Looked away. Came back and looked again.

Day 9. My daughter was home for the weekend. She was standing at the counter making coffee and she said, "Mom. Did you get something done?" I said no. She said, "Your skin looks different." She said it the way you say something you're not sure about.

Day 14. The foundation I'd been wearing for two years — the one that would crack on my forehead by noon — stayed. I checked at two. Still there. I checked at four. Still there. My face had something to sit on.

Day 21. I was back in Renee's chair. She looked at me in the mirror and didn't say anything for a minute. Then she said, "See?"

========

Here's the thing I didn't notice until about week three.

I used to check myself in my phone's front camera. I'd do it at stop lights. In the bathroom at the office. Before meetings. Before dinner. For about a year, maybe longer, I'd been checking my own face every few hours the way some people check the weather.

One Saturday I was in the car waiting at a light and I realized I hadn't opened the front camera in days. Maybe a week. I couldn't remember the last time I had. The habit was just gone. I hadn't decided to stop. I just didn't need to check anymore.

========

Renee's been using it for eight months. I've been using it for about three. My daughter has been asking for her own jar. My sister saw me at Easter and made me write down the name on the back of a receipt.

My husband — this is the part I didn't expect.

Last Tuesday night we were in the kitchen cleaning up after dinner. He had a glass of wine. He'd been standing at the sink and he turned around and leaned against the counter and he looked at me for long enough that I said "what."

He said, "Your face looks different."

I said, "Different how."

He said, "You look like you slept."

He said it like it was an observation he was piecing together. He hadn't said anything about my face in about eight years. He doesn't notice new haircuts. He's never commented on makeup. He didn't have any reason to bring it up. He just did.

========

I thought about it for the rest of the night.

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the Thursday afternoon when Renee stops halfway through the first color section and asks you what happened to your skin — because something changed and someone who has been looking at your face for twelve years sees it and has to say it.

It's the Tuesday at the sink when your husband looks at you like he's looking at you for the first time in a long time and tells you you look like you slept.

It's the stoplight you sit through without opening the front camera because you don't need to anymore.

Four ingredients. Tallow. Honey. Olive oil. Calendula.

Renee wrote it on the back of a color card. I'm telling you the same way.

$49.99. Free shipping. 60-day guarantee. If it doesn't work, send it back.
