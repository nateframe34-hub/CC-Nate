## Body Copy

I had been hiding from him in bad light for almost a year before he caught me.

Not in a dramatic way. Just little things. I started washing my face after he fell asleep. I kept the bathroom door half-closed when I did my skincare in the morning. If we were in the car together and the sun hit my visor wrong, I'd turn my head toward the window.

I wasn't trying to deceive anyone. I was protecting both of us from something I couldn't name.

And then last Sunday I forgot.

We were making breakfast. He was at the stove. I leaned over his shoulder to steal a piece of bacon and he turned his head at the same time and our faces almost touched. He put his hand on the side of my jaw — just for a second, the way he's done a thousand times over 24 years — and then he paused.

And he said, "Your skin feels different."

That was it. Four words. Not even a compliment. Just a fact he noticed.

========

I froze.

Because I knew what he meant. I knew it before he said it. I'd been feeling my own face every morning for months. Running my fingertips along my cheekbone and my jaw. Checking.

The skin under my hand had been tight and papery since I was 49. Like it was stretched over a frame that was too big for it. I'd gotten used to it. I thought I'd made peace with it. I thought he wasn't supposed to notice because he'd been touching the same face for so long that he couldn't see it changing.

But now he could feel it. The change was the kind you could feel.

I turned the bacon. I didn't say anything. I don't think he meant anything cruel by it — he's never been cruel about anything. But I went upstairs and sat on the edge of the bed and cried for maybe two minutes. The quiet kind of crying. The kind where you don't even know you're doing it until you feel wet on your jaw.

========

Later that week I was on the phone with my sister. She's four years younger than me and just starting perimenopause.

She was complaining about her skin. Her moisturizer wasn't working anymore. She'd tried three different ones in the past two months. She said, "It feels like everything I put on just disappears. Like my face is eating it."

I said, "It is."

And that was the first time I said out loud what I'd been trying to figure out by myself.

========

Here's what I learned after weeks of reading studies I didn't understand and Reddit threads I understood too well.

Your skin makes its own oil. It's called sebum. You have been making it your whole life without thinking about it. It's the reason your skin stayed soft in your 20s even though you "didn't have a routine." It's the reason a teenager's face looks plump. That oil is the thing.

Around 40, your body starts making less of it. Around menopause, it can drop by more than half.

Not a little. More than half.

And here's the part that made me put my phone down and stare at the ceiling. Almost every moisturizer in my bathroom was water-based. Water, glycerin, a bunch of stabilizers, some fragrance. When you put water on skin that can't hold its own oil anymore, the water evaporates within an hour. You're left with the same dry, tight, papery feeling you started with.

I had been trying to refill a bucket with a hole in the bottom. For years.

My skin wasn't failing me. My products were failing me. They were built for a kind of skin I no longer had.

========

My college roommate Maggie — I hadn't talked to her in almost a year, we're both terrible about texting back — sent me a voice memo out of nowhere in the middle of all this.

She was two years older than me and she sounded like herself again. That's the only way I can describe it. Just normal, easy, unworried.

She said she'd been using something called a tallow and honey balm for six weeks and I should try it because "it's the only thing that's made sense since I turned fifty."

I listened to the voice memo twice. Then I looked it up.

The reason it works — and this is going to sound insane if you've never heard it — is that tallow is almost identical to the oil your skin used to make. Something like 87% the same. Not "similar to moisturizer." Similar to what your body USED to produce before menopause. So when you put it on, your skin doesn't treat it like a foreign product sitting on top. It absorbs it. Because it recognizes it.

I'm not a scientist. I'm not going to pretend I understood every word in the studies. But I understood the basic idea the way you understand a lock finally turning: you were trying to add moisture from the outside, and your skin needs the oil from the inside. Water can't replace oil. Only oil can replace oil.

I ordered a jar that night. $49.99. Four ingredients on the label. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. That was the whole list. I flipped my night cream over to compare. My night cream had 31 ingredients. The first three were water, glycerin, and something called dimethicone.

I'd been spending $90 on 31 ingredients of mostly water.

========

I'm not going to do the whole "day 1, day 3, day 7" thing because honestly the first week was unremarkable. I was still cynical. I kept touching my face to see if anything was happening. I almost returned it.

Then my sister called me on a Wednesday night and I was in bed, not wearing makeup, and she said, "What did you do to your skin?"

She was calling me on FaceTime. She was seeing me in my actual bathroom lighting. And she said that.

I put my hand on my cheek and it felt different. I hadn't realized when it happened, because it had happened so gradually — the tightness had loosened. The papery quality was gone. My face felt like a face instead of a piece of fabric pulled over a frame.

========

The next Sunday my husband put his hand on my jaw again — a totally unconscious thing, he was reaching for a dishtowel behind me — and I felt him pause again.

And he said, "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."

Four different words.

I didn't cry this time. I laughed. Because I realized I'd been hiding from the wrong thing. I'd been hiding from him seeing my skin. But what he'd been feeling all year wasn't ugly. It was just… starving.

========

Here's the thing I've been wanting to say since my sister asked me what I did.

If you're in your 40s or 50s and your skin suddenly doesn't hold moisture — if your expensive cream stops working, if you're layering products, if you've started avoiding your own bathroom mirror — it is not your fault and it is not aging and it is not something you have to accept.

Your body stopped making its own oil. That's the whole story. Every moisturizer in your bathroom is probably water-based, and water can't fix an oil problem.

Maggie didn't tell me this because she was selling anything. She told me because she felt bad for not telling me sooner. She said, "I should have sent this to you a year ago."

That's what I keep thinking about. All the women my age who are quietly doing what I was doing — washing their faces in the dark, turning their heads in the car, avoiding the moment where their partner touches their jaw and pauses.

========

If this is you, the jar is $49.99. Four ingredients. 60-day money-back guarantee. Free shipping. If it doesn't work, send it back.

I ordered mine. My sister ordered hers. Two of the women in my book club have now ordered theirs after I finally stopped being weird about it and told them what worked.

Stop hiding in bad light. The change your husband can feel isn't going to fix itself. But it can be fixed.

That's what Maggie told me. That's what I'm telling you.

## Brief Self-Audit Notes

This ad is deliberately different from B1C2 in execution:

**Structural differences from B1C2:**
- B1C2 opened with direct external dialogue ("My husband looked at me..."). B3C1 opens with a scene — her hiding in bad light for a year before being caught. The dialogue comes AFTER the scene is set.
- B1C2 used "friend Lisa" as the discovery character. B3C1 uses "college roommate Maggie" who sends a voice memo.
- B1C2 used the husband noticing at a kitchen table. B3C1 uses TWO separate husband moments — the first at the stove (bacon/hand on jaw/"your skin feels different") and the second a callback scene ("whatever you're doing, keep doing it"). The double husband beat reinforces the theme without being the opening.
- B1C2 had a daughter taking a photo as the final validation. B3C1 uses a FaceTime call from the sister as external validation, plus the second husband beat.
- B1C2 discovered tallow via Lisa's selfie. B3C1 discovers via Maggie's voice memo explicitly framed as "I should have told you sooner."
- B3C1 introduces the sister as a peer going through the SAME thing (perimenopause starting) — this adds the "I should have been told sooner" theme that isn't in B1C2.

**Hook format differentiation:** B1C2 is "external dialogue." B3C1 is "scene opener" — the first line is a behavior, not a quote.

**Theme:** "Am I still visible?" → answered by TWO different moments of being felt/seen again, not just one.

**Product mentioned:** At roughly 55% through the body copy (after the sebum mechanism lands via the sister conversation and Maggie's voice memo).

**Length:** ~1,280 words. The story asked for it.
