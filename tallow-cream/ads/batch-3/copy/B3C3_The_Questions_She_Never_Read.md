## Body Copy

"It's just menopause. Try drinking more water."

That's what my dermatologist said. She didn't even look up from her clipboard.

I had a list of seven questions on my phone that I had written out the night before because I was nervous I'd forget. I never got to read a single one of them.

========

I want to tell you what I had on that list. Because the questions weren't unreasonable.

I had: "Why does my moisturizer disappear within an hour?"

I had: "What's the difference between dry skin and dehydrated skin and which one do I have?"

I had: "Is there a connection between hormones and skin oil production after menopause?"

I had: "Are there different categories of moisturizer for women my age, and how do I know which kind I need?"

These are not stupid questions. These are the questions of a woman who has been paying attention to her own body and trying to figure out what changed.

She didn't read them. She didn't ask if I had questions. I sat in the chair for nine minutes, including the time it took her to look at my face, glance at my chart, write a prescription for a retinol I'd already tried two years earlier, and tell me that "skin gets drier as we age" and I should "drink more water and use sunscreen."

I left her office and sat in my car in the parking lot for twenty minutes feeling stupid for having written the questions down.

========

That was Dr. Whitman. She's not the only one.

Six months earlier my OB-GYN had told me, when I brought up the same skin changes, "That's normal for perimenopause. There's not much we can do about it." Same energy. Same not-looking-up. Same closing of the conversation before it could open.

My mother told me, when I asked her about it last Christmas, "You just have to learn to live with it. I did."

And those three voices — the dermatologist, the OB-GYN, and my own mother — became the three voices in my head whenever I tried to bring it up to anyone. Because how do you keep asking questions after three different women have told you, in three different ways, that the question doesn't have an answer?

I stopped going to doctors about my skin entirely.

Not in a dramatic way. Just quietly. I'd schedule the appointment, and then I'd cancel it the night before. I'd schedule the next one and cancel that too. After about a year of cancellations I stopped scheduling.

I told myself I was being mature about it. Accepting reality. The truth is I'd been told to shut up so many times that I had finally learned to shut up.

========

This is the part where I get angry.

Because I wasn't crazy. I wasn't being dramatic. I wasn't making up symptoms. The questions on my phone were the right questions, and there were actual answers, and not one of those women told me what they were.

A few months ago I was at a baby shower for my niece — different niece, this isn't the textbook one — and a woman I'd never met before sat down next to me. She turned out to be a nurse. Twenty-something years on a hospital floor. She was talking about her own perimenopause and at some point I just blurted out, "Did your skin change too?"

She said, "Oh god, yes. I lost my mind for about a year."

And then she said the thing that I'm now going to say to you:

"It's not aging. It's a sebum drop. After menopause your body makes a lot less of the natural oil your skin used to make. None of the moisturizers in your bathroom can fix it because none of them are oil. They're water. You need an actual oil-based product or you're just going to chase your tail forever."

I asked her how she knew this and she gave me a look like I'd asked her how she knew water was wet. She said, "Because it's basic dermatology. It's in the textbooks. They just don't bring it up because most doctors don't get into the weeds with patients about anything that doesn't have a prescription."

I want you to sit with that for a second.

The information exists. It's in the textbooks. My dermatologist knows it. My OB-GYN knows it. They didn't tell me because there's no prescription for it.

========

I went home that night and started reading. Not Reddit. Not skincare blogs. Actual papers about sebum production over the lifespan. Studies on water-based versus oil-based moisturizer absorption. The mechanism is not contested. It's not even controversial. It's textbook biology that you can verify in five minutes.

After 40, your sebaceous glands produce less oil. After menopause, they can drop by more than half. The oil they're not making anymore is what kept your skin soft, plump, and protected your whole life. When you put a water-based moisturizer on skin that has lost its oil layer, the water evaporates within an hour and you're left with the same tight, papery feeling you started with. You're refilling a leaky bucket.

The fix isn't more moisturizer. The fix is a different category of moisturizer. One that's made of fats and oils similar to what your body used to produce. The closest thing to human sebum that you can buy is rendered animal fat — specifically tallow. Tallow's fatty acid profile is roughly 87% similar to human sebum. Your skin doesn't treat it like a foreign cream sitting on top. It absorbs it. Because it recognizes the fats.

This is in textbooks. My dermatologist knows it. She wrote me a prescription for retinol and told me to drink water.

========

I bought a jar of whipped tallow and honey balm a few weeks after the baby shower. The nurse — her name is Renee, I asked her later — sent me a text with the brand she used. Four ingredients on the label. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. $49.99 for a 4 oz jar. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee.

I didn't expect it to work. After two years of being dismissed by professionals, my expectations had calibrated themselves down to almost nothing. I was prepared to send it back.

By day five my skin felt different. By the second week the tightness was gone. By the third week I caught my reflection in the window of a coffee shop and I genuinely paused because I didn't recognize the face for a second — not because it was unfamiliar but because it didn't look tired. My face hadn't looked not-tired in two years.

I haven't been back to Dr. Whitman.

========

I want to be clear about something.

I'm not telling you to stop trusting doctors. I'm not telling you to skip dermatology appointments. I'm not anti-medicine. My OB-GYN is an excellent doctor for the things that have prescriptions, and I'm grateful for her.

What I'm telling you is this: the medical system is not built to answer questions that don't have a prescription attached. That's not a conspiracy. It's a system design choice. And if your problem is the kind of problem that doesn't have a pill — like skin that has stopped responding to anything because your body has stopped making its own oil — the medical system will dismiss you. Not because the answer doesn't exist. Because the answer isn't a prescription.

You don't owe your dermatologist your silence. You don't owe your gynecologist your patience. You don't owe your mother the comfort of "you were right, I just learned to live with it."

If your skin changed and nobody can explain why, the explanation exists. It just isn't going to come from someone with a prescription pad.

========

I'm not selling you anything I don't use. I bought my second jar last week. My third jar is going to my sister-in-law for her birthday. My fourth jar is for the woman in my book club who told me her dermatologist gave her the same line as Dr. Whitman.

Stop drinking more water. Start putting oil back in. Read the questions you wrote down. Ask them out loud, even if it's only to yourself, because the answers do exist and you deserve to hear them.
