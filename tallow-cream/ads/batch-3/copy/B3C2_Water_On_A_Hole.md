## Body Copy

After 40, your body produces more than half less of the only thing that keeps your skin from feeling dry.

Nobody tells you this. Not your doctor. Not your dermatologist. Definitely not the woman selling you the $90 night cream at Sephora.

I'm 53. I figured it out three months ago. I'm still angry about it.

========

I didn't figure it out from a beauty article. I figured it out from my niece's dermatology textbook.

She's in her second year of med school and she'd left her bag at our house over Thanksgiving. I was making coffee on Saturday morning and one of her textbooks was sitting on the kitchen counter with a sticky note in it.

I wasn't trying to read it. I just opened it the way you open a magazine in a waiting room.

There was a graph on page 412.

It showed sebum production over the human lifespan. Sebum is the oil your skin makes on its own. The thing that keeps a teenager's face plump. The thing that keeps a 25-year-old woman glowing without trying. Your body has been making it your whole life without you ever thinking about it.

The graph showed sebum production rising in your teens, peaking in your 20s, holding steady through your 30s, and then falling.

It dropped sharply at 40.

It fell off a cliff at menopause.

By 55, the average woman is making roughly 40% of what she used to make. Some women — depending on their hormones — drop even further than that.

I stared at the graph for maybe a full minute.

========

I want to tell you exactly what went through my head, because if you're my age you might have the same thought:

I had been buying water for my face for ten years.

Every moisturizer in my bathroom — I'm not exaggerating, every single one — has water as the first ingredient. Glycerin as the second. Some kind of stabilizer as the third. The "active ingredients" come at the end and they're in concentrations of 1-3%.

I'd been pouring water on a problem caused by a 40% drop in oil production.

You can't fix an oil shortage with water. You just can't. Water evaporates within an hour. That's why you have to keep reapplying. That's why your $90 night cream doesn't last until morning. That's why your foundation cracks. That's why your skin feels tight by 11am no matter what you put on.

It's not the brand. It's the chemistry.

========

The reason I'm angry is that I've been going to a dermatologist twice a year for the last seven years and she has never mentioned this.

She's prescribed me retinol. She's recommended a hyaluronic acid serum. She's suggested I drink more water (which is absurd — drinking water doesn't put oil back in your sebaceous glands, your liver doesn't ship it there).

Not once has she said: "Your skin is making less of its own oil. You need to put oil back in. Water-based products are not going to do that."

Not once.

I asked my niece about it after Thanksgiving. She was embarrassed for her textbook. She said dermatology training is heavy on prescription pharmaceuticals and very light on the kind of basic biology that would actually help most patients. She said most dermatologists are trained to treat conditions, not to explain mechanisms.

Then she said something that stuck with me: "The information is in the textbooks. It's just nobody connects it to the products people are buying."

========

Once I knew about the sebum drop, I couldn't unsee it.

My aunt has been telling me for two years that "skin just gets dry when you get older, you have to deal with it." She's 71 and she's wrong. Her skin isn't dry because she's old. Her skin is dry because she's been refilling a bucket with a hole in the bottom for forty years and nobody told her there was a different bucket.

My mother, who is 76, has actually pretty good skin for her age. I asked her what she used. She said, "I never used much. Just whatever was in the bathroom." When she was in her 50s, you couldn't buy 10-step skincare routines. There was Pond's. There was Vaseline. There was olive oil. The products were basic and most of them were oil-based, not water-based.

She wasn't using a magic product. She was using fewer products that happened to actually work for the problem she had.

We've all been sold the wrong tool.

========

So I went looking for what would actually work.

I started with the question: "What's the closest thing to human sebum?" Because that's what my skin was missing. Not water. Not glycerin. The specific oil my body had stopped making.

Almost every search took me to the same answer.

Beef tallow.

I know how that sounds. I had the exact reaction you're having right now. I almost closed the tab.

But here's why it works: tallow is something like 87% similar to human sebum at the molecular level. Not "feels similar." Chemically similar. Same fatty acid profile. Same composition. When you put it on your skin, your skin doesn't treat it like a foreign cream sitting on top. It absorbs it. Because the fats in tallow are the fats your body used to make.

It's the closest thing to refilling the bucket itself.

========

A coworker of mine — she's 58, she'd been quietly using one for about four months — finally told me what she was using when I cornered her at a meeting. It was a whipped tallow and honey balm. Four ingredients on the back. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. That's it. No water. No glycerin. No dimethicone. No fragrance. The whole list fits on a Post-it.

She told me she'd switched after spending almost $1,200 on serums in one year and getting nothing.

I ordered a jar that night. $49.99. Less than half of what one bottle of my Sephora night cream cost. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee — which I appreciated, because I was still cynical.

========

It worked the way the textbook said it would.

Not in some dramatic 24-hour transformation. The way it worked was that I stopped reapplying things during the day. I'd put it on in the morning and at 3pm I'd touch my cheek and it would still feel soft. That had not happened to me in years. I genuinely could not remember the last time I went a full afternoon without re-moisturizing.

By the second week the tightness was gone. Not "less." Gone.

By the third week I was using less of it because a tiny amount goes a long way and a 4 oz jar lasts me about two months.

I was spending roughly $90 a month on a routine that wasn't working. I'm now spending about $25 a month on a routine that is. That's not a marketing claim. That's a math problem I should have done years ago.

========

If you're 45 to 65 and your skin has stopped responding to anything you put on it, I want you to do one thing.

Flip over your moisturizer. Read the first ingredient. If it says "water" or "aqua," you're using a water-based product on skin that has lost most of its ability to make oil. The chemistry is wrong. It's not your fault and it's not your skin and it's not your discipline. You've been using the wrong tool because that's what the skincare industry sells.

The tool that matches the problem is an oil-based balm. The closest match to your skin's natural oil is tallow. The version I bought was four ingredients, $49.99, 60-day money-back guarantee, free shipping.

If it doesn't work for you, send it back and get your money back. I'm not asking you to take a risk. I'm asking you to do the experiment my dermatologist should have offered me seven years ago.

Stop refilling the bucket. Get the right bucket.
