## Body Copy

I'm 48. Last spring my daughter grabbed my hand to drag me over to the tomato starts and stopped, mid-step, and turned my hand over like she was checking something.

"Mom. When did your hands start looking like Grandma's?"

She's seventeen. Subtlety is not the strong suit. But I stood there in the driveway holding a flat of seedlings without an answer, because I hadn't looked. Not in years.

I've gardened for eleven years. Tomatoes, peppers, a rose bed I lose a fight with every single spring. Six hours a week outside from April through October, hands in the dirt most of it, because gloves are for people who don't need to feel what they're doing.

Nobody tells you what eleven years of sun and soil and hose water adds up to. You just do it, one ordinary Saturday at a time, until a seventeen-year-old does the math for you.

-------------

That night I actually looked. Knuckles rough enough to catch on my sweater. Sun spots I'd been filing under "freckles." A crack at the base of my thumb that had been there so long I'd stopped counting it as new.

And here's the part I'm not proud of. I'd made peace with it. Somewhere along the way I decided this is just what gardener's hands look like, the way a mechanic has grease under his nails. Not a problem. Just a fact about me now.

That's the real low point. Not the dry skin. The part where I stopped expecting anything different.

-------------

It's not like I hadn't tried. Over eleven years I have personally funded the hand cream industry:

O'Keeffe's Working Hands, the one every gardener swears by. Did almost nothing for me past the first hour.

CeraVe, because the internet said so.

A $9 tub of Gold Bond that lived by the kitchen sink.

Shea butter my sister sent two Christmases in a row.

Gardening gloves I bought, wore twice, and abandoned, because I'd rather have dirt under my nails than not feel the roots.

And the Vaseline-with-socks-on-my-hands overnight trick, which mostly resulted in Mark asking why I was dressed like a mime.

Every single one of them felt great going on and was gone by the second hand-wash. My friend Dana, who gardens more than I do, put it best: "I've tried every lotion possible. I keep a bottle in three rooms because I forget where the last one disappeared to."

I thought we were bad at consistency.

-------------

Then one night, annoyed, I flipped my lotion bottle over and actually read the back.

First ingredient: water.

Not third. Not fifth. First. The thing I'd been buying for a decade to fix my dry skin was mostly water with a flower on the label.

Go flip yours over. I'll wait. It's water.

Here's what nobody in that aisle is going to tell you: water evaporates. That's what water does, on skin or anywhere else. You rub it in, it feels smooth for twenty minutes, it's gone, and then the industry lets you conclude you weren't applying it often enough. I counted the bottles I could remember buying and stopped when the math got insulting. Ten years of paying skincare prices for water, blaming my own consistency the whole time.

It was never an effort problem. It was a water problem.

-------------

The actual fix I learned from a woman named Denise at Mark's company holiday party, of all places. Ranch background, hands that have done more outdoor work than mine ever will, and the softest handshake at the table.

She told me skin doesn't need water rubbed on top of it. Skin needs fat. Skin IS fat, mostly, the same kind that's in tallow, which is why tallow soaks in and stays instead of sitting on top and rinsing off. Her jar had four ingredients. Tallow, honey, olive oil, calendula. She reads labels the way I read them now.

I said what you're thinking. Cow fat. On my hands. I felt ridiculous ordering it.

But I'd just done the math on ten years of water, so ridiculous felt affordable.

-------------

First few days, nothing dramatic. It just didn't disappear. That alone was new.

Day four, I weeded the whole side bed, did two loads of laundry, washed my hands I don't know how many times, and never reapplied.

Week two, Mark grabbed my hand at dinner, the ordinary way you do without thinking, and stopped. "Since when do your hands feel like that?" I hadn't told him I'd changed anything.

Week three, the weird one: my cuticles stopped catching on fabric. I wasn't even tracking my cuticles. Then I noticed my heels, which I had definitely never mentioned to the jar.

And Dana asked what I switched to, because she wanted to steal it.

I didn't set out to fix anything. I'd already decided there was nothing to fix, just a decade to accept. What I got instead was my daughter grabbing my hand a few weeks ago, no comment this time, no stopping mid-step. Just holding it.

Eleven years of sun did what eleven years of sun does. Turns out most of what I was calling damage was skin that had been thirsty for a decade while I fed it water.

You deserve hands that don't make you flinch when someone notices them. That's not vanity. That's just refusing a deal I almost made peace with.
