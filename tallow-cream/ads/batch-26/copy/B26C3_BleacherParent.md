## Body Copy

I have sunscreen in my bag at every single game. The spray my daughter likes, the stick for her friend who always forgets hers, a backup of each.

In twelve years of sidelines, I have almost never put any of it on myself.

My friend Kara called it at a tournament in June, watching me hand out sunscreen to teenagers for the third time that day. "You know you're going to be the last person in Colorado still holding sunscreen and completely fried, right?"

She wasn't wrong. I laughed because every soccer mom I know does the exact same thing. It's a whole genre in our team group chat. Someone posts a photo from a tournament weekend and the caption's always the same: tan lines from three states. Nobody's surprised. It's part of the uniform, like the folding chair and the cooler that never has enough ice.

-------------

Here's what I don't put in the group chat.

This is my daughter's last season. She's seventeen. Twelve years of rec league, then club, practices and Saturday games and tournament weekends from April through August, her brother's baseball before that. I've done the math on my face the way you'd do math on a mortgage: hundreds of afternoons of full sun while I made sure everyone under eighteen was covered.

I knew the whole time. That's the thing. It wasn't a shock, there was no dramatic mirror moment. I burned at the big Memorial Day tournament basically annually and joked about it annually. I just always filed my own skin under "later," the way you file the garage under later. Twelve years of later.

Mark said something at a tournament last summer that stuck more than I let on: "You look wiped in every photo from these things." I laughed it off, then went back through three summers of tournament photos that night. Sunburned in June, peeling in July, tired-looking in all of them, sun spots showing up along my cheekbone like a season record.

And I'd made peace with it. Sideline face. Cost of showing up. That's the low point if I'm honest, not the sunburns, the part where I kept expecting better for everyone in the family except me.

-------------

Not that I did nothing for twelve years. I did everything, badly:

A drugstore moisturizer that lived in the glove box for exactly this purpose. Applied at red lights, gone by the second half.

A $42 "recovery" cream from a mall kiosk lady who saw me coming a mile away.

Aloe every June, like clockwork, treating week twelve of a twelve-week problem.

The kids' sunscreen, occasionally, on myself, which is how I learned SPF 50 does nothing for the years already on the scoreboard.

And a fancy serum my sister-in-law swore by that I used four times and lost to the bathroom drawer where products go to die.

My co-team-mom Priya, same bench for six years: "I've got sunscreen for three kids and myself as an afterthought maybe twice a season. Whatever I put on my own face is gone before the second game."

-------------

The explanation finally showed up in, of course, the group chat.

One of the moms, Steph, is a dermatology nurse, and somebody was complaining about her sideline skin, and Steph typed out the thing I wish someone had said in 2014. Almost every moisturizer at the store is mostly water. First ingredient on the label, go look. Water evaporates, and on a hot field day where you're sweating and reapplying everyone else's sunscreen, it's gone within the hour. Meanwhile what sun actually takes out of skin over years is the oily barrier layer, the thing that holds moisture in. So sideline parents are treating a fat problem with water, wondering why nothing works, and buying more water.

Twelve years of glove-box lotion. Water. The industry never corrected me, because me blaming myself was the business model.

Then Steph said what she actually uses on sun-damaged skin: tallow. Rendered fat, closest thing to what skin's own barrier is made of, soaks in and stays through a full day outside. The one I bought has four ingredients. Tallow, honey, olive oil, calendula.

Yes, I said the cow fat thing out loud in my kitchen. My daughter laughed at me from the counter. I ordered it anyway, because the group chat has never once steered me wrong about anything except tournament traffic.

-------------

First tournament weekend with it, nothing dramatic. Except I never once dug through my bag for something for my own face, because I didn't need to. Twelve years of sidelines and that was a first.

Second weekend, Kara zoomed in on a photo she took of the parents' tent and sent it to me: "ok what are you doing, your face doesn't look cooked."

Third weekend, the one that got me. Hotel bathroom, Saturday night of a two-day tournament, the exact fluorescent lighting that has ended better women than me. I looked normal. Day-two-of-a-tournament face, looking normal, in THAT mirror. I stood there longer than I'll admit.

And Mark, looking at a photo I sent from the hotel: "You don't look wrecked in this one." I hadn't told him anything changed.

The sun spots from twelve years of Saturdays didn't vanish, I'm not going to tell you fat and honey un-does a decade. But the skin around them stopped looking thirsty and started looking like mine, the dryness and the tightness after a day outside are gone, and in her last season, the photos of me on that sideline are ones I'll actually keep.

Twelve years putting sunscreen on everyone but me. It took until her final season, but I finally made the roster.

A lot of the moms in that group chat have started saying some version of the same thing. Sunscreen for everyone but ourselves, for years, always meaning to get around to it.

Get around to it. You've earned that much bench time. There's a 60-day guarantee, so if a season of weekends doesn't feel different, send it back and you're out nothing.
