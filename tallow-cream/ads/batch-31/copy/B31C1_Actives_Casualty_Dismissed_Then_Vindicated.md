## Body Copy

My dermatologist told me not to put anything on my face that the two of us hadn't discussed first.

Then she handed me a prescription that made my face peel for four months and told me that part was normal.

Both of those things were true. That's what took me so long to work out.

========

I'm 49. I started the prescription for a couple of scars along my jaw that had been there since my thirties.

It worked. I want to say that first, because everything after this depends on it. It genuinely worked.

But by week three I had flaking around my nose that I could feel with my fingertips. The corners of my mouth split. Not badly. Just enough that smiling had a small sharp edge to it.

Foundation made it worse. It caught on every dry patch and sat there in flakes, so I stopped wearing it, which meant I'd traded the scars I was covering for a face I didn't want to put anything on at all.

========

I did what you do. I bought the drugstore cream everyone recommends, the one in the tub. Then a second one. Then a thick ointment in a yellow tin.

None of it was wrong exactly. It just sat there.

I'd put it on and my face would feel coated for about ten minutes and then it would feel tight again underneath, like the cream was on top of the problem instead of in it.

By month two I was Googling how to stop. Not how to fix it. How to quit.

That's the part I'd want you to hear if you're somewhere in the middle of this right now. It's not the peeling that makes people give up. It's the arithmetic. You start doing the math on how many more months of looking like this you're willing to trade, and the number keeps getting smaller.

========

My sister-in-law Dana has been on the same kind of thing for six years. Six. I'd never once seen her flake.

We were at my mother-in-law's in April and I finally asked her, in the kitchen, quietly, the way you ask something you're a little embarrassed to still not know.

She said, "What are you putting on after?"

I told her. The tub, the second cream, the ointment.

She said, "No, I mean what are you putting on *after*. Are you sandwiching?"

I didn't know what that word meant. She could tell.

========

Here's what she explained, standing there with a dish towel in her hand.

The prescription works by turning skin over faster than it normally would. That's the whole point of it. That's why it does what it does to scars.

But skin holds its moisture in with its own oil. Turn it over fast enough and there's less of that oil at the surface at any given moment. So the water in your skin leaves faster than your skin can hold it.

You're not dry because your skin is failing. You're dry because you removed the thing that was keeping the water in, on purpose, for a good reason.

Dana said, "So put fat back on top of it. Not water. Fat. Water evaporates, that's just what water does, and every one of those creams you named is water first on the label. Go check."

I went and checked that night. All three. Water, first ingredient, every one.

========

She told me she uses tallow. Four ingredients.

I said, "Cow fat. On my face. While I'm on a prescription."

She said, "I said the exact same thing to the person who told me. Try it two weeks. And do it in the right order or it won't work."

The order matters, so here it is the way she gave it to me. The prescription goes on completely dry skin. Then you wait until it has properly dried down. Then the balm over the top. And she told me to put it on while my face was still slightly damp from washing on the nights I wasn't using the prescription, because it absorbs better into damp skin than dry.

She also said to put a little into the three places that split first. Nose creases. Corners of the mouth. Corners of the eyes.

I ordered one that night. I was skeptical, honestly. But it was $49.99 with free shipping and a 60-day guarantee, so the worst version of this was that I'd spent two weeks finding out.

========

Night 1, I put it on after the prescription had dried down. It melted in my palm before it got to my face.

Morning 2, I woke up and the corners of my mouth didn't have that tight sharp feeling when I yawned. I noticed it before I was properly awake, which is not a thing I'd have believed if you'd told me.

Day 4, the flaking around my nose was still there. Less, but there. I'm including that because I remember being annoyed that it wasn't gone.

Day 9, I put foundation on for the first time in two months. It sat flat.

Day 16, I realized I hadn't opened the yellow tin in over a week.

========

Here's the part I didn't expect.

I never stopped the prescription. Not for a night. That was the whole thing I'd been trying to work out how to do, and in the end I just never had to make the decision.

Somewhere in there the question quietly stopped being a question. I was three months further into the treatment than I'd planned to get, and my jaw was doing what it was supposed to be doing, and I hadn't done the math about quitting in weeks.

========

I went back to my dermatologist in June for a follow-up.

She looked at my face for longer than usual and asked what I was using.

I told her. She didn't love it. She said something careful about not being able to endorse anything she hadn't tested.

Then she said, "But whatever you're doing for the barrier, keep doing it."

I don't think she'd have said that in March.

========

If you're on one of these right now and you've been quietly doing the arithmetic on how many more months you can stand, I'd guess you've also got two or three creams in your bathroom that you keep using even though you already know they aren't doing anything.

You bought the second one because the first one didn't work. And the third one for the same reason.

None of them were going to work. They're all water.

========

I'm not telling you a jar of cow fat makes a prescription easy. That sounds like an ad. This isn't an ad. I'm telling you what my sister-in-law asked me in a kitchen in April, and the fact that I never had to quit.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. There's a 60-day guarantee, so if two weeks doesn't show you anything, you email them and you get it back.

Link is below if you want it.

Dana's still on hers. I'm still on the prescription. The yellow tin is still in the cupboard and I have no idea why I'm keeping it.

What I want you to have isn't the jar. It's not having to decide.

I'd made peace with the idea that I was going to quit something that was working.

I didn't have to.

## Six Failure Modes Check

1. **Wrong engine for awareness level** — ✅ Clear. Problem-Aware entry, and the problem is dramatized through concrete daily loss (foundation abandoned, mouth corners splitting) before any solution appears.
2. **Describing pain vs twisting the knife** — ✅ Twisted. *"It's not the peeling that makes people give up. It's the arithmetic."* That's the internal experience, not the symptom.
3. **Knife without permission shift** — ✅ Permission shift present and explicit, placed immediately after the mechanism.
4. **Character break at product reveal** — ✅ No break. Price and guarantee arrive inside her own reasoning ("the worst version of this was that I'd spent two weeks finding out"), not in marketing voice.
5. **Overclaiming** — ✅ Modest and qualified. Day 4 explicitly reports incomplete results. No scar claim is ever made by us; the prescription gets that credit.
6. **Wrong angle for avatar** — ✅ Confirms the belief they hold hardest (the treatment is worth it) instead of fighting it. Research-confirmed: *"it'll be worth it in the end."*
