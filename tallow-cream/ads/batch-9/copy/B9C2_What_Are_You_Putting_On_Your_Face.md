# B9C2 — What Are You Putting On Your Face

**Sub-Avatar:** The Invisible Woman
**Angle:** External-dialogue hook — a painfully-honest friend of 22 years notices the change at Sunday brunch before the narrator has realized the change is visible
**Character type:** Peer + Skeptic Convert
**Hook format:** External-Dialogue (B5C1 family)
**Awareness level:** Solution Aware

## Body Copy

My friend Deanna hugged me at the diner on Sunday and stepped back. She looked at my face and said "what are you putting on your face? Your skin looks softer."

I told her I wasn't putting anything new on my face.

She said "you're lying."

I wasn't lying. I didn't know what she meant.

========

I'm 52. Deanna has known me since we were both 30. She saw me at my worst two Septembers ago when my father was sick. She saw me at my best at my daughter's wedding in 2021. She has never, in 22 years, complimented my face.

Her first husband once told her the kindest thing she could do for someone was tell them if their skin looked tired. She took that to heart and has been painfully honest with every woman she knows since 2004.

So when she said "your skin looks softer" at the diner on Sunday, I didn't wave it off. I went home and stood in the bathroom and looked.

========

I didn't see what she saw. Or I did, and I hadn't been letting myself.

My face didn't look tight the way it had all winter. My foundation wasn't settling the way it had been. The skin along my jawline — the part my foundation had been creasing on since last spring — looked different. Not young. Not younger. Just at ease.

I stood there thinking about what had changed.

It took me a minute to remember.

========

Six weeks earlier, I'd helped my sister Sharon move apartments.

She's four years older than me. Just turned 56. Newly single after thirty-one years of marriage. She'd downsized to a one-bedroom in a building with a courtyard and I spent a Saturday packing her bathroom.

I was wrapping jars in newspaper when I picked up an amber glass jar with a simple label. Heavier than I expected. I turned it over.

Four ingredients. Tallow. Manuka honey. Olive oil. Calendula.

That was the whole list.

I said: "Sharon. What IS this?"

She came in from the bedroom, took it out of my hand, and held it up.

She said: "I've been using that for eight months. It's the only thing I use on my face now."

========

I said: "Tallow. Beef fat. On your face."

She said: "I know. Just let me explain it."

She sat down on the edge of the tub and explained it like she'd been waiting to tell someone for eight months.

Here's what she said. I want you to read this slowly.

Skin makes its own oil. It always has. It's the reason you didn't think about moisturizer at twenty-five. The oil was just there. Working. Quiet.

After about forty, skin makes a lot less of it. The drop happens slowly. Most women don't connect what's happening on their face to what changed underneath.

Sharon flipped over a bottle of night cream I'd already packed. She pointed to the first ingredient.

Water.

She flipped the next one. Water. Glycerin second. Something unpronounceable third.

She said: "Every cream you've been buying is mostly water. It sits on top for an hour and then it's gone. You're putting water on a problem caused by skin making less oil. That's why nothing's been holding."

I said: "And this works because — "

She said: "Because the fat in it is almost the same structure as the fat skin makes on its own. So it absorbs in. Skin treats it like its own oil. Because, roughly, it is."

I finished wrapping her jars in silence. I went home that night and thought about it for three days. Then I ordered one.

========

$49.99. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee.

Day 1 I put it on before bed. It melted into my palm like warm honey. I pressed it into my cheeks. My jaw. My forehead. Thirty seconds total.

Day 5 I woke up and touched my face before I opened my eyes. Soft. Not bathroom-mirror soft. Underneath soft.

Day 10 I was standing in the produce section at the grocery store when a woman I'd known from my kids' carpool years ago walked up to me. We hadn't seen each other in three years. She said "you look amazing. Are you working out?" I told her no. She said "something then." I didn't know what.

Day 16 I put on foundation at 7am for a client lunch. At 1pm in the restaurant bathroom I checked. It sat flat. It hadn't cracked around my mouth. That hadn't happened since before the pandemic.

Day 20 Deanna noticed at the diner.

========

Here's the part I didn't expect.

I'd been reaching for my lip treatment first thing in the morning for about two years. Before water. Before the bathroom. It was the first thing my hand did when I opened my eyes — fumble for the tube on the nightstand.

One morning about day fourteen I reached for it and couldn't find it. I'd knocked it off the nightstand a day earlier and forgotten to pick it up.

I walked into the bathroom without it. I brushed my teeth. I washed my face. My mouth didn't feel tight. My lip didn't crack. I made coffee.

I didn't pick the tube up off the floor for another three days. I only picked it up to put it in a drawer.

That was the thing I wanted back. Not the soft skin. The not-reaching.

========

My sister-in-law Margaret came for Easter. She's 61 and she has never been kind about any woman's face. She's our family critic.

She hugged me at the door, stepped back, and said: "You look rested."

My husband raised his eyebrows at me from the kitchen. Margaret doesn't say "rested." Margaret says things like "you look tired" and "you've lost weight" and sometimes "your hair looks like your mother's did."

I told her thank you. Then I went into the kitchen and put my face in my husband's shoulder and laughed for a full minute.

========

My husband said something on a Tuesday morning six days later.

We'd slept in. The light was coming through the window slats. He opened his eyes before I did and I felt him looking at me. I opened my eyes. He was propped on one elbow.

He said: "You look good this morning."

I said: "I just woke up. I don't have anything on."

He said: "I know. That's why I said it."

He put his hand on the side of my face for a second and then rolled out of bed to make coffee.

I lay in the light and didn't move for a while.

========

That was a month ago. He's said something two more times since. Once on the drive back from dinner with friends. Once in the car on the way to church.

I'm not telling you a jar of cow fat will fix your life. That sounds like an ad. This isn't an ad. I'm telling you what Deanna said at the diner and what Sharon said in her bathroom and what happened in the six weeks between the two.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee. If it doesn't do anything in six weeks you email them and you get every dollar back.

Link is below if you want it.

Sharon is still using it. Deanna ordered one after the diner. Margaret texted me from Easter dinner on the drive home and asked for the name.

What I want you to have isn't the jar. It's the Tuesday morning. The person who has been looking at you for decades waking up in the light and saying you look good with nothing on.

I'd stopped expecting that.

Now I have it again.

## Compliance Audit

- ✅ Zero symptom words (crepey, papery, flaky, saggy, sunken, damaged) — only functional descriptions ("tight all winter," "foundation settling," "didn't hold")
- ✅ Zero hiding/avoiding/shame behaviors as low point — the low point is the external foundation-creasing beat and the fact that the narrator had stopped expecting her husband to notice (functional + relational, not hiding)
- ✅ Zero "If you're 45, 50, 55+" close — social proof framing ("Sharon is still using it. Deanna ordered one after the diner. Margaret texted me from Easter dinner")

- ✅ Zero anti-competitor / active-harm framing — "mostly water" is neutral category description, not "your cream is damaging you"
- ✅ Zero disgust language ("throw up," "gross," "sick")
