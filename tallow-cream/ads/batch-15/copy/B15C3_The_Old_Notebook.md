## Body Copy

I found my mother's notebook in a box in the attic on a Sunday in February.

The box had been sitting under the eaves for three years. Mom died in 2018 and Steve and I had brought boxes home from her house in Cleveland over the course of a weekend. I'd put them in the attic and then I'd left them there.

The notebook was from 1979. She'd written about her skincare routine on one page. Six lines. She was forty-seven. I'm fifty-two.

That morning I'd spent forty-five minutes in my bathroom with seven jars open on the counter.

========

I'm Ellen. Fifty-two, paralegal at a litigation firm in Columbus, eleven years. My husband Steve is fifty-three, math teacher at the high school. Our son is nineteen at Ohio State, dorm life, doing fine.

My mother was Diane. She taught high school English in Lakewood, Ohio for thirty-eight years. She kept notebooks her entire adult life. Not journals. Notebooks. Things she was thinking about, recipes she wanted to try, lists of books to read, the weather on the day my brother was born. She'd kept them in a wooden cabinet in her hallway. There were six notebooks in the box I'd brought home. I hadn't opened any of them.

She died at eighty-six of pancreatic cancer. Three months from diagnosis. She had nice skin until the end. I hadn't thought about that in years.

========

My skin changed at forty-eight. Tight by 11am. Foundation cracking around my mouth by lunch. I tried CeraVe, a retinol that burned, a vitamin C serum, a hyaluronic acid serum, an eye cream, a lip treatment, a brightening spot serum I'd bought online at midnight. Seven products. They were what I'd been standing in front of that morning.

My doctor looked at my face during my last physical and said, "Your skin will keep changing. There's not really a fix for it." I'd written down what she said in the back of my own notebook on the drive home. I'd given up.

For the past eighteen months I'd been getting up at five in the morning instead of six so I'd have an extra hour for my skincare routine. Cleanser, toner, vitamin C serum, hyaluronic acid, eye cream, moisturizer, sunscreen. Forty-five minutes most mornings. I told myself it was working. It wasn't working.

========

I sat on the kitchen floor that Sunday afternoon and read the notebook page out loud. Steve was at the table behind me with the newspaper.

Six lines.

"Wash face evening. Pond's cold cream. Two fingers. Forehead, cheeks, jaw. Glass of water before bed. Sleep eight hours."

That was the whole entry. She'd dated it March of 1979. She was forty-seven.

Steve put the newspaper down. He said, "Your mother had really nice skin."

I hadn't thought about that in years.

I called my friend Karen that night. Karen and I have known each other since our kids were in second grade together. She'd come to my mother's funeral. She'd sat next to my mother's best friend Sheila at the reception and they'd talked for almost an hour.

I asked Karen what Sheila had said about my mother's skin.

There was a pause.

Karen said, "Ellen. I'm going to tell you something."

========

"Sheila told me your mom used one thing her whole adult life. Not a routine. One thing. She kept it on her dresser. I asked Sheila what it was. She had a small jar in her purse at the funeral and she gave it to me before she went back to Akron. I've been using it for five years."

I asked Karen why she'd never said anything.

"Because for the first three years your mom had just died and it felt strange. Then I forgot it was even a thing to mention. I'm sorry. I'm telling you now."

I let her keep talking.

"Here's what's happening to your face. Your mother had it figured out without knowing why. She used something simple and oil-based and it worked because that's what skin needs. Your skin makes its own oil. Always has. You didn't think about moisturizer at thirty because the oil was just there. After forty you make a lot less of it. Every cream you've been buying is water, then glycerin, then a thickener. Water sits on your face for an hour and evaporates. That's why nothing has held. Water can't replace oil. Your mother's instinct was right. The science just caught up later."

She gave me a minute to sit with it.

"The thing I've been using is called Whipped Tallow and Honey Balm. Same idea your mother had. Four ingredients on the back. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. The honey in it is manuka, which is a different thing from regular honey, that one matters here. They give you sixty days to send it back. Your mother would have laughed at me using the same thing for five years. I think she would have approved."

I ordered one that night.

========

Day 1 was Sunday night. I washed my face the way the notebook described. Two fingers. Forehead, cheeks, jaw. Thirty seconds. I went to bed.

Day 4 was Wednesday morning. I woke up and touched my face before I opened my eyes. Not tight. I lay there for a minute thinking about my mother's hallway cabinet.

Day 9 was a Tuesday. Steve looked at me across the dinner table and said, "Did you cut your hair." I hadn't cut my hair. I told him I hadn't. He said, "Then I don't know."

He didn't try to figure it out. He just kept eating.

Day 14 was a Saturday. Our son's basketball game at OSU. Big stadium, fluorescent lights, the kind of lighting that shows everything. I went to the vending machine in the lobby at halftime and caught my reflection in the glass. I looked like myself at seven that morning. Not like myself at three in the afternoon.

Day 21 was a Sunday. I went back up to the attic and got the other five notebooks down. Carried them all to the kitchen table. I read parts of three of them that afternoon while Steve was at his brother's. I cried twice. I laughed once. I went back to the 1979 one and read the six lines again.

========

Here's what I didn't see coming.

For eighteen months I'd been setting my alarm for five in the morning so I'd have the extra hour for the routine. Six products in order. Wait between each. Sunscreen last. I'd done it Monday through Friday, religiously, almost two years.

One Wednesday in April I woke up at six-fifteen. I'd forgotten to set the early alarm. I had forty-five minutes instead of an hour and forty-five. I showered. I washed my face. Two fingers. Forehead, cheeks, jaw. I got dressed. I left the house at seven-ten the way I always do.

In the car on the parkway I realized what had happened. I hadn't laid out the seven products. I hadn't waited between layers. I hadn't done the routine in three weeks. I hadn't thought about it. I'd been going to bed at ten-thirty and waking up at six-fifteen and putting one thing on my face like a normal person.

That was the thing I wanted back. Not the soft cheeks. The hour of sleep.

========

I told my sister at Easter dinner. She'd noticed something but hadn't said anything. She ordered one before dessert. My aunt Joanne asked me at the same dinner what I'd done. I told her about the sixty days. She's seventy-eight. She said, "If it doesn't work I send it back. Your mother would have liked that part."

She ordered one before we cleared the table.

========

A Sunday in May. Three months after the attic.

I was at the kitchen table with three of my mother's notebooks open in front of me. Reading them one at a time. Steve walked in for coffee. He stood at the counter for a minute looking at me reading.

He said, "You look like your mom."

I didn't say anything. I looked down at the 1979 notebook. Six lines.

He poured his coffee and walked out.

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's the Sunday in your kitchen with your mother's notebook open in front of you and your husband walking in and saying you look like her, and you not having anything to say back because you already know.

It's the five-in-the-morning alarm that doesn't go off because you don't need it anymore.

It's the seven bottles on the counter becoming the question instead of the answer.

Whipped Tallow and Honey Balm. Four ingredients. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. Forty-nine ninety-nine. Free shipping. Sixty days to send it back if you hate it and they refund every dollar. The sixty days is the reason to try it at all.

Link is below.

I keep the 1979 notebook on my nightstand now.
