# B15C1 — The Sister Visit

**Sub-Avatar:** The Invisible Woman
**Angle:** Family-witness blunt comment with authority-referenced mechanism delivery, per-use cost math, light-avoidance habit cessation
**Character type:** Family (sister)
**Hook format:** Face-to-face blunt comment from a younger sister, after a long visit gap
**Awareness level:** Solution Aware

## Body Copy

My sister Lisa drove up from Cleveland for the long weekend. We hadn't been in the same room in eighteen months. She's forty-nine. I'm fifty-three. Saturday morning we were at the kitchen table with coffee and she sat there looking at me for what felt like a long time.

Then she said, "What's happening with your skin? You look ten years older than me."

She didn't say it to hurt me. She said it because she was alarmed.

========

I'm Maggie. Fifty-three, accountant at a mid-sized firm in Pittsburgh. My husband Steve is fifty-five. Our son is twenty-one and away at school. Our daughter is sixteen and still at home. I've been at the same firm for nineteen years. I run a tight life. Lisa and I have been close since we were kids, and I'd just been avoiding her, in the small way you avoid people who'd see something you don't want them to see.

My skin changed at forty-nine. Not gradually. One season my face was fine. The next season my foundation was cracking along my mouth by 11am and my cheeks felt tight before I'd even taken my second sip of coffee. I tried CeraVe for six months. The blue tub. Nothing held past noon. I tried a retinol my dermatologist prescribed at a six-minute visit. It burned for two weeks and the cracking got worse. I tried a hyaluronic acid serum my coworker swore by, an eighty-dollar jar that ran out in six weeks.

By fifty-two I'd given up. I told myself it was fine. It wasn't fine.

I stopped meeting Lisa for lunch when we were in the same city. Twice. I told myself I was busy. I wasn't busy.

========

Lisa was staying through Tuesday. Sunday morning I was making the coffee and she went into my bathroom. Came back out holding three bottles. Set them on the kitchen island.

"Maggie," she said. "These are all water. Every one of them."

I told her she was crazy. She told me to flip them over. The first ingredient on every one was water. Second on every one was glycerin. Third on every one was something I couldn't pronounce.

Lisa had switched to tallow two years ago. She'd mentioned it to me twice. The first time I'd told her I had a routine. The second time I'd changed the subject.

I hadn't listened.

She sat down at the kitchen island and waited for me to listen.

========

"Here's what's happening to your face," she said. "Your skin makes its own oil. Always has. You didn't think about moisturizer at thirty because the oil was just there. After forty you make a lot less of it. Way less. I asked Dr. Trent about it at my annual physical last year. She told me skin's job is to make its own oil, and the older you get the worse it gets at the job. She told me water can't replace oil. That sentence sat with me for a week. It's why none of those bottles have worked. Water sits and evaporates. That's not the bottle failing. That's just what water does."

I didn't say anything.

"This stuff," she said, holding up her phone with the product page on it, "is fat. Same kind of fat your skin used to make. It absorbs in. Doesn't sit on top. Four ingredients on the back of the jar. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. The honey in it is manuka, which is a different thing from regular honey, that one matters here."

She turned the phone toward me.

"I've been on this for two years. A jar lasts me almost three months. Forty-nine ninety-nine. That works out to about fifty cents a day. I was spending a hundred dollars a month on stuff that didn't hold and now I spend twenty. The reason I actually tried it is they give you sixty days to send it back. Two months. Most places don't even give you thirty. I figured worst case I lose nothing."

She sent me forty-nine ninety-nine on Venmo before I could argue.

========

Day 1 was Sunday night. I put it on after Lisa went to bed. It felt like warm honey pressed into my hand and went in faster than I expected. No greasy film. I lay in the dark for ten minutes waiting to feel something and didn't.

Day 4 was Wednesday morning. I touched my cheek before I opened my eyes. Not tight. I touched my forehead. Not tight. I went to the bathroom and pressed my finger into my cheekbone and watched the color come back the way it's supposed to. I stood there for a minute longer than I needed to.

Day 9 was a Friday. Steve looked up from his dinner plate and said, "Your skin looks different. What are you using." Steve has been looking at my face for twenty-six years. He has never asked me that. Not once.

Day 14 was a Wednesday in April. I put foundation on at seven in the morning. I had a nine-hour day at work, three meetings, fluorescent everything. At four in the afternoon I went to the bathroom on the third floor and looked in the mirror. The foundation was still there. It hadn't cracked at my mouth. It hadn't moved on my forehead. I hadn't seen that since I was forty-eight.

Day 21 was a Sunday. Lisa texted me. "How's it going." I took a selfie at my desk on Tuesday at lunch and sent it without writing anything. She wrote back three exclamation points and a heart. It was the first selfie I'd taken in over a year.

========

Here's what I didn't see coming.

For three years I'd been turning off the overhead light in the bathroom before I did my makeup. The harsh light bothered me. I didn't want to see what I'd look like under it. I'd done it every morning, automatically, for so long I didn't think about it anymore.

One Wednesday in May I was getting ready for work and Steve walked into the bathroom while I was putting on mascara. He flipped the overhead light on without thinking about it. I didn't ask him to turn it off. I didn't notice the light was on. I finished my mascara. I clipped my hair back. I walked out.

In the car on the way to work I realized what had happened. I hadn't turned the light off. I'd just done my makeup with the overhead light on like a normal person.

I sat in traffic on the parkway and didn't know what to do with myself.

That was the thing I wanted back. Not the soft cheeks. The not-needing-the-light-off.

========

I told my best friend Karen at lunch that week. She ordered one that night. My mother-in-law Joanne asked me at Easter what I was doing. I told her about the sixty-day window and she ordered one the same day. She's seventy-one and the sixty days was the reason she did it. "Worst case I send it back," she said. "Best case I stop spending money on stuff that doesn't work."

========

Three months later, Lisa drove up again.

We sat at the kitchen table on Saturday morning with coffee, same as before. She looked at me for a long time, same as before.

This time she said, "You look like yourself again. I almost didn't recognize you in a good way."

I didn't know what to say. She didn't need me to.

========

What I want you to have isn't the jar.

It's your sister you haven't been in the same room with in eighteen months sitting across the kitchen table from you on a Saturday morning, looking at you for a long time, and not having anything alarming to say.

It's the overhead light staying on in the bathroom because you don't think about it anymore.

It's the selfie you send back without writing anything because there's nothing to explain.

Four ingredients. Tallow, manuka honey, olive oil, calendula. Forty-nine ninety-nine. A jar lasts about three months. Free shipping. Sixty days to send it back if you hate it and they refund every dollar. No questions. The sixty days is the reason to try it at all.

Link is below.

Lisa is coming up again for Thanksgiving.
