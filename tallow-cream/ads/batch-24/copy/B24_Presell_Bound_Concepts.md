## Cell A: The Peer-Secret Ladder (the account's single best-proven angle)

**Angle:** The envy/discovery ladder from B9C3/B17C1, the account's 7-purchase outlier. Not the object-discovery hook specifically, the underlying ladder: a peer visibly solved the problem with no selling involved, the secret is withheld before it's given, authority is laundered through a specific unGoogleable person (Winnie), the payoff is being noticed again.

**Why this should work:** it's the only angle on the account with a real, repeated purchase history behind it (7 lifetime purchases across two names). Every other cell in this batch is a bet on a validated-but-thinner angle; this one is the closest thing to a known quantity. The presell page can carry the FULL ladder (all five rungs) the way B9C3's inline copy does, something a single ad image alone never could, which is exactly the gap this batch structure is built to close.

**Creative description:** A sharp, well-lit photograph of a single amber glass jar sitting on an otherwise bare stone counter, shot at a slight angle like a real bathroom counter, label legible but not the hero of the frame. Small red "VIRAL" or "TRENDING" badge tag in the corner. Warm, editorial lighting, not clinical. This is the literal object from the story, presented as a real product shot rather than disguised as candid.

**5 headlines:**
1. "The jar that replaced fourteen products, and the reason nobody talks about it."
2. "She had one thing on her bathroom counter. It took me three weeks to ask why."
3. "A 76-year-old beekeeper has been quietly using this since 1987."
4. "Why the woman with one jar has better skin than the woman with ten."
5. "The four-ingredient jar that's replacing entire skincare shelves."

## Cell B: The Generational Mirror (Pond's angle, proven but understated)

**Angle:** B7C1's angle, a mother's one-product routine outperforming a daughter's ten-product routine, reframed as a direct product/nostalgia comparison for the ad itself rather than the full narrative (the narrative lives on the presell).

**Why this should work:** proven converter, and founder's own math suggests its true purchase count is understated (ran alongside B9C3 on the banned account's blended 1.2 ROAS). The generational-comparison visual is also an extremely strong, legible thumbnail concept, unlike the peer-secret ladder (which is hard to show in a single frame, it's a story), a mother's jar next to a daughter's shelf of products is instantly readable at thumbnail size, which should help CTR specifically, the metric this batch is trying to fix.

**Creative description:** A split composition: on one side, a single vintage-style jar (evoking Pond's, an amber tallow jar standing in for it) sitting alone on a simple counter, warm nostalgic tone; on the other side, a cluttered modern counter with 8-10 skincare bottles crowded together, cooler and busier lighting. A bold vertical divider line down the center. Small badge tag: "THE OLD WAY VS. THE NEW WAY" or similar.

**5 headlines:**
1. "Our mothers used one jar. We use fourteen products and still lose."
2. "What our grandmothers knew about skin that we spent fifteen years forgetting."
3. "One old jar vs. a $400/month skincare shelf. The jar won."
4. "She found her mother's old cream in a box. It explained everything."
5. "Before serums, before retinol, before any of it: one jar did the whole job."

## Cell C: The Direct Confrontation (pushes past house style, presell absorbs the compliance weight)

**Angle:** Borrows the swipe file's most aggressive structural device, the direct injustice/confrontation hook ("when a man has X, he gets Y, when a woman has X, she's told Z"), applied to Tallow's actual non-medical mechanism instead of a medical claim. This is the account's first use of that confrontational structure at all. Also borrows the label-legibility/trust angle (B20C3 + Amazon review language) as the secondary proof point.

**Why this should work:** every ranked CPM/engagement analysis this account has done points the same direction, direct, tension-forward, zero-throat-clearing hooks outperform gentle ones, and this account has never actually tried the most confrontational version of that structure, only softer versions. The risk is real (this is the furthest the account has pushed a headline), which is exactly why it's isolated to one cell in a 3-cell test rather than rolled out account-wide.

**Where the line gets pushed, specifically:** direct age callouts ("after 40") stated plainly instead of softened, a mild adversarial framing against "the skincare industry" (not against doctors, not a medical claim, an industry-critique frame, which is compliance-safer than the swipe file's doctor-villain device while keeping the confrontational energy), and one headline that uses a specific, blunt number claim tied to ingredient count (a factual, verifiable claim, not a fabricated stat). Nothing here is a health/medical claim, nothing fabricates a testimonial, nothing implies a cure. The presell page is where the actual explanation and compliance-careful mechanism language lives, same as the other two cells.

**Creative description:** Sharp, high-contrast product photograph, the amber jar held up at eye level against a plain dark background, dramatic single-source lighting (more graphic and confrontational than Cell A/B's soft editorial lighting). Bold red badge tag: "THE TRUTH ABOUT" or "WHAT THEY WON'T TELL YOU." No soft nostalgic tones here, this one is meant to look like it's picking a fight.

**5 headlines:**
1. "The skincare industry doesn't want you to know your cream is mostly water."
2. "After 40, your skin needs fat, not more serum. Nobody in the industry says this out loud."
3. "Four ingredients beat a fourteen-step routine. Here's the part the industry skips."
4. "Your $80 moisturizer is 70% water. This jar isn't."
5. "The real reason your skincare routine stopped working after 40, and why almost nobody will tell you."
