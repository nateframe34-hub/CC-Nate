## Body Copy

I was at Elaine's house for book club last month and I went upstairs to use the guest bathroom.

There was one thing on the counter.

One jar. Amber glass. Simple label.

I stood there longer than made sense.

========

I'm 54. Elaine is 56. We've been in the same book club since our daughters were in preschool in 1998. I've been in that bathroom a hundred times.

Eighteen months ago the counter had a toner, two moisturizers, a face oil, a retinol, and a spot treatment.

Now it had one jar.

I dried my hands and came downstairs and didn't bring it up.

========

When I got home that night I went into my own bathroom and counted what was on my counter.

Fourteen things.

I didn't know the number off the top of my head. I had to count.

My skin was tight by noon every day. My foundation cracked by two. And somehow fourteen things was not fixing that.

I sat on the edge of the tub and thought about Elaine's counter for a long time.

========

I waited three weeks. Next book club was at my house. Elaine came early to help me set out food.

I waited until she was pouring herself wine in the kitchen. Then I asked.

"What's the one jar in your bathroom?"

She laughed and put the bottle down.

She said: "I wondered if you'd notice."

========

She said she'd thrown everything out about nine months earlier. Everything. The toner, the serums, the retinol. She kept one jar.

She'd asked her Aunt Winnie about it at her daughter Jess's wedding last June.

Winnie is 76. She lives in Vermont. She keeps bees and reads more than anyone Elaine knows.

Elaine cornered her in the kitchen during the reception and asked: "What do you do to your face?"

Winnie said: "I've been using the same thing since 1987. Four ingredients. I bought my first one by accident at a farmer's market in Brattleboro."

Winnie is 76 and her skin looks like a woman in her early 60s. Elaine had been watching her at family things for a decade.

Turns out it was one jar.

========

Here's what Elaine told me Winnie had told her.

Skin makes its own oil. It always has. It's the reason you didn't think about moisturizer when you were twenty-five. The oil was just there.

After about forty, skin makes a lot less of it. The drop is real. Most women don't connect what's happening to their face to what changed underneath.

Every cream in Elaine's bathroom had water as the first ingredient. She flipped each bottle over one afternoon and checked. Water. Glycerin second. Something unpronounceable third.

Water evaporates in an hour. That's what water does. You put it on at 7am and by noon it's gone. That's why nothing was holding.

Winnie told Elaine the fat in the balm is almost the same structure as the fat skin makes on its own. So when you put it on, it absorbs in instead of sitting on top. Skin treats it like its own oil. Because, roughly, it is.

Elaine ordered one the week after the wedding. She'd been on it nine months by the time I asked her in my kitchen.

========

I said: "Tallow. Like cow fat."

She said: "I know. I made the same face. Just try it."

I went upstairs after everyone left and ordered one. $49.99. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee.

========

Day 1 — I put it on before bed. It melted into my palm like warm honey. I pressed it into my cheeks, my jaw, my forehead. Thirty seconds total.

Day 4 — I woke up and touched my face before I opened my eyes. Soft. Not the thirty-minute soft that disappears in the shower. The underneath kind.

Day 9 — My coworker Sylvia asked me over lunch what I was doing different. We've worked two desks apart for eleven years. I told her nothing. She said "something then."

Day 14 — I put on foundation at 7am for an offsite. At 2pm I went to the restroom. It sat flat. It hadn't cracked around my mouth. I didn't reach into my bag.

Day 21 — Elaine hugged me at book club and smiled and didn't say anything. She didn't need to.

========

Here's the part I didn't see coming.

I used to think about my face constantly. Not in a vain way. In a managing way.

Before I left the house: check the mirror, blot, reapply. Before every Zoom call: tilt the camera up so the lighting was better. Before every meeting: quick bathroom run to make sure nothing had slid. Every reflective surface I passed — a window, a car door, a darkened phone screen — I'd glance.

I'd been doing all of it for two years without naming it. It was just how I moved through the day. Checking. Adjusting. Managing.

Around week five I was getting out of my car to go into the grocery store. I walked past the side mirror and kept going.

I didn't check.

I didn't notice I hadn't checked until I was inside, standing in the produce section.

I stopped in the middle of the aisle and stood there.

That was it. That was the thing I hadn't known I was waiting for. Not softer skin. The quiet. The absence of the checking. My face just — not being something I had to manage anymore.

========

My husband and I had dinner outside at our usual place on a Saturday in May. Candle on the table. He was finishing his wine.

He looked up at me and stopped mid-sentence.

He said: "You look different."

I said: "Different how?"

He said: "I don't know. Lighter. Something."

He reached across and put his hand on the side of my face for a second.

He said: "Whatever you're doing, I can see it."

I sat there and didn't move.

He pulled his hand back and picked up his wine.

I finished mine slowly.

========

I'm not telling you a jar of cow fat will fix your life. That sounds like an ad. This isn't an ad. I'm telling you what Elaine had in her bathroom and what Winnie told her at her daughter's wedding and what I found out on a Tuesday afternoon in the produce section.

Four ingredients. $49.99. Free shipping. 60-day money-back guarantee. If it doesn't do anything in six weeks you email them and you get every dollar back.

Link is below if you want it.

Elaine is still on it. Sylvia ordered one after I told her. My daughter Lily asked me for the name last Sunday.

What I want you to have isn't the jar. It's the grocery store aisle. The moment you walk past your reflection and just keep walking because there's nothing to check.

I hadn't believed I'd stop thinking about it.

Now I don't think about it.
