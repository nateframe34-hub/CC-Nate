## What this batch is actually optimising for

**Not profitability. Repeatable demand.** Founder note 2026-09-23: consistent weekly orders unlock a private supplier and COGS near $25, which drops breakeven ROAS from 1.78×/2.17× to roughly **1.39×/1.52×**. So the outcome we want from batch 1 is *purchases that repeat*, not a strong first-week ROAS.

The kill rules still govern this spend at today's economics. But **"consistent 1-2 orders/week at breakeven" is a PASS**, and should not be read as failure.

# AD SET 1 — Angle A3: "You don't need a contractor"

**Why this angle leads the batch:** it is the **best-evidenced angle we have.** Sourced verbatim — *"somewhere between $3-4,000"*, *"quoted over 3k"*, *"That seems rather high and I don't like the broad range"* — plus a documented case where the professional's fix (a sheet-metal baffle) *"didn't help much"* and the homeowner ended up on a 500W space heater anyway.

**The insight the whole ad set rests on:** their distrust is not only of the *price*. It's of the **vagueness**. "Somewhere between $3-4,000" is a $1,000 shrug. So every ad here answers vagueness with precision — our number is exact and small; theirs is large and wobbles.

---

## B1C1 — Native long-form ⚠️ needs persona page

**Format:** Native / camouflage, long primary text · **TOF** · **Right column EXCLUDED** (can't render primary text)

**What it tests:** whether the full argument, told as one person's sequence, out-converts a single-frame claim. This is the only format that can carry the whole chain — the quote, the vagueness, the failed professional fix, the thing they did instead.

**Thought process.** The account's best-ever ad (B17C1) worked on the **attribution lever**: the same mechanism sentence produced 12 purchases when quoted from a named person with nothing to gain, and 0 across ~1,002 impressions when asserted by the advertiser. A native is the only format where that lever is available at full strength. The story premise is handed to us by the research — someone got a quote, balked at the range, and solved it for under a hundred dollars.

**Story premise (to be written through `native-ad-writer`):**
Homeowner with one cold room. Two winters of putting it off. Gets a contractor out; the quote is "somewhere between $3,000 and $4,000" and the vagueness bothers them more than the number. They don't do it. A third winter. Then they find out the room is simply last on the duct run — nobody had told them that — and that the fix is a vent cover with fans in it. Room closes most of the gap. **Not all of it.** That partial outcome is the credibility.

**Beat structure:**
1. The quote, and the word "somewhere"
2. Two winters of not doing it
3. The workarounds that stacked up in the meantime
4. The explanation nobody had given them — the duct run
5. What they did instead: two screws and an outlet
6. **Partial result, stated honestly** — the gap closed from six degrees to about one
7. No offer terms in the image; price appears in text only, late

**Image:** Format F — authorless POV. A hand held flat against a floor register, phone-camera quality, real domestic room slightly out of focus behind it. No product. No text.

> **Image prompt:** *Candid iPhone photo, first-person point of view, looking down at a beige metal floor register in a carpeted bedroom. A woman's hand is held flat, palm down, an inch above the vent slats, the way you'd check whether air is coming out. Late afternoon winter light from a window out of frame, slightly cool and flat. The carpet is a real lived-in beige, one corner of a bed visible at the top edge, a power outlet on the baseboard behind. Ordinary, unstyled, slightly imperfect framing. Real phone-camera quality with mild grain. No text, no overlays, no product.* 4:5, 1080×1350.

## B1C2 — Solution Exaggeration (statement static)

**Format:** Typographic statement · **TOF** · all placements

**What it tests:** whether the angle survives with **no image at all** — the cheapest, most placement-portable carrier we have. If this performs, we have a format we can produce ten variants of in an hour.

**Thought process.** The swipe file's Solution Exaggeration format ("14+ HOURS. NO SWELLING.") works through **staccato declaration plus a specific, odd number.** Round numbers read as fabricated; measured numbers read as true. We have a real number from the research and it's usefully specific. The structure also inverts the trust dynamic: our figure is exact, theirs is a range. And it sits maximally far from a native in visual register, which is the anti-familiarity job.

**The creative:**

> ## Two screws and an outlet.
> ## Not $3,400.

Terracotta on warm off-white, Archivo, very large, nothing else in frame. `$3,400` in mono tabular figures. No product, no logo lockup beyond a small wordmark, no offer terms.

> **Design direction:** *Plain typographic static, 4:5. Warm off-white background `#FAF7F2`. Two lines of large, tightly-set Archivo bold in near-black `#1C1A17`, left-aligned, generous margins. "$3,400" set in monospace tabular figures, in terracotta `#B4553A`. Small Evenroom wordmark bottom-left in terracotta at 40% scale of body. Nothing else. No product, no icon, no badge, no price, no CTA.*

**Primary text direction:** the short version of the native — the quote, the vagueness, the duct run, the partial result. 120-180 words.

**Headline:** *The quote was "somewhere between $3,000 and $4,000."*
**Description:** *It's the last room on the duct run.*

## B1C3 — Minimal Us vs Them (vs the quote) ⚠️ leans MOF

**Format:** Split comparison · **TOF, but see caveat** · all placements

**What it tests:** whether a comparison works on a cold reader when the "them" is **a service quote rather than a competing product.**

**Thought process.** The Carepod structure is the strongest version of this format: list *their* problems, then say almost nothing about yourself. *"None of that."* The persuasion is that the viewer infers your benefit themselves — self-generated conclusions are more trusted than stated ones. Using it against a contractor quote does two things at once: it keeps us clear of the competitor-naming rule entirely, and it targets the vagueness rather than just the price.

**⚠️ Honest caveat.** Comparison formats assume the reader is weighing options, which is MOF behaviour. This is the one ad in the batch that may underperform cold for structural reasons rather than because the angle is wrong. **If it underdelivers, do not read that as a verdict on angle A3** — read B1C1 and B1C2 for that.

**The creative:**

Left panel, labelled **The quote**:
- "Somewhere between $3,000 and $4,000"
- Three days
- Someone in your house
- Booked for next month

Right panel, labelled **This**:
- **Two screws.**

> **Design direction:** *Split static, 4:5. Left half warm grey `#EDE9E3`, right half terracotta `#B4553A`. Left: four short lines in near-black, each with a small dash, "The quote" as a small caps label above. Right: "This" as the label, then "Two screws." in large white Archivo bold, huge whitespace beneath. Dollar figures in monospace tabular. No product, no price for our item, no CTA, no badge.*

**Headline:** *Two screws.*
**Description:** *The other option was three days and a range.*

# AD SET 2 — Angle A1: "You were right about the room"

**Why this angle exists:** the **New Information play** — the one thing genuinely unowned in this category. Nobody explains *why* a specific room starves. Competitors claim improvements ("300% more airflow"); none explains the problem.

**⚠️ What this angle is NOT any more.** It used to be *"It isn't you, and it isn't your AC."* **The self-blame premise was retracted 2026-09-18** — three direct searches found nothing, and every self-diagnosis quote blames the house, the builder or the duct run, never the occupant. An ad opening with absolution answers an objection these people don't have. The surviving beat is **confirmation plus explanation**: you were right, and here's the part nobody told you.

## B1C4 — Native long-form ⚠️ needs persona page

**Format:** Native / camouflage · **TOF** · **Right column EXCLUDED**

**What it tests:** whether the explanation lands harder as a story than as a diagram. Same format as B1C1, different argument — so across the batch we also get a clean native-vs-native angle read.

**Thought process.** This is the angle where a native has the most room, because the mechanism takes a few sentences to land and a static has to compress it to a line. The story should arrive at the explanation the way the research subjects did — from a stranger, not from a company. The strongest real-world beat available: contested advice. Some forum posters recommend closing vents in other rooms to force air toward the cold one; more knowledgeable posters say flatly *"You really can't close registers and think air will be 'forced' into a different part of the house,"* and one person who tried it made a different room worse. **Correcting a piece of advice the reader has probably already been given is the most valuable thing in this ad.**

**Beat structure:**
1. The room, and the comparison that makes it obvious
2. Their own theory — corner room, over the garage, farthest from the furnace
3. The advice they were given: close the other vents
4. It didn't work, and one other room got worse
5. **The explanation** — last on the run, pressure lost at every branch, a trickle instead of a push
6. Hand on the vent — the checkable moment
7. What changed, partially

**Image:** Format F variant — the vent itself, no hand. Authorless.

> **Image prompt:** *Candid iPhone photo of a white wall register low on a bedroom wall, shot slightly off-angle from standing height, as if someone crouched to look at it. Winter afternoon light, cool and flat. A strip of beige carpet, a baseboard, the corner of a nightstand leg at the frame edge. Utterly ordinary, slightly imperfect framing, mild grain. No text, no overlay, no hand, no product.* 4:5, 1080×1350.

## B1C5 — Duct-run diagram (infographic) ⭐

**Format:** Explanatory diagram · **TOF** · all placements

**What it tests:** the **New Information response delivered at the lowest possible comprehension cost.** If this wins, the category's whole opportunity is education, and the PDP's mechanism section becomes the most important thing we own.

**Thought process.** The tallow precedent is the reason this is designed carefully rather than confidently: **B32C3 put an optical claim on a whiteboard and died at $36.53 with 0 purchases**, because there was no physical process to draw — the board carried no information and became decoration. The test the post-mortem implies: *is there a real spatial process here?* Yes. Air loses pressure at every branch and bend; the last room gets a trickle. That is genuinely drawable, and drawing it is the clearest explanation a reader can receive in one glance.

Hand-drawn register, not corporate infographic — a corporate one reads as a brand asserting; a sketched one reads as someone explaining.

> **Design direction:** *Hand-drawn-feeling cross-section diagram, 4:5, on warm off-white `#FAF7F2`. A simplified house in section: furnace/air handler at the left, one main duct running right, four branch ducts peeling off upward into four rooms. Arrows in terracotta `#B4553A` show airflow, drawn THICK at the first room and progressively thinner at each branch until the last arrow is a thin wisp. The final room is tinted with the diagram-cool slate `#5B7185`; the first three rooms are untinted. Small mono tabular labels on each room: 70°, 69°, 68°, 62°. One handwritten-feeling line beneath: "Every branch takes its cut." Ink-like irregular strokes, not vector-perfect. No product, no logo beyond a small wordmark, no price, no CTA.*

**Headline:** *Why the last room never keeps up*
**Description:** *It isn't the furnace. It's the distance.*

## B1C6 — This Is Not [The Product] ⭐ strongest single non-native

**Format:** Category reframe · **TOF** · all placements

**What it tests:** whether **reframing the category** beats explaining the mechanism. It also carries the box-fan pre-empt — the objection nearly every one of these buyers holds, because nearly all of them have already tried a fan and concluded fans don't work.

**Thought process.** The Huel structure — *"This is a meal. Not a protein shake."* — works by anticipating a misread and correcting it, which opens a curiosity gap: *if it's not that, what is it?* The swipe file's warning on this format is explicit: *"it has to make sense. Do not overstretch it. If the dots don't get connected, the ad will flop."*

Here the reframe is **literally true**, which is why it's the strongest non-native in the document. And it does two jobs in one line: it reframes the category *and* it turns the reader's failed box fan from evidence against us into evidence for us. Left alone, that failed fan is the single biggest unspoken objection in the funnel.

**The creative:**

> ## This isn't a fan for the room.
> ## It's a fan for the vent.

> **Design direction:** *Split static, 4:5. Left two-thirds: a real photograph of a plain white wall register, straight on, warm domestic light. Right third: a terracotta `#B4553A` panel carrying the two lines in white Archivo bold, the word "vent" emphasised. Beneath, three short mono lines: "Moves the air you already paid to heat." / "Runs when the duct is running." / "Two screws." No price, no guarantee, no CTA, no badge.*

**Primary text direction:** open on the box fan they already own. *A fan moves the air that's already in the room. In winter that air is cold — moving it around makes it feel worse.* Then the pivot: this one sits in the vent and pulls. Close on the partial, honest outcome.

**Headline:** *You already tried a fan. That's not this.*
**Description:** *It pulls from the duct, not the room.*

## B1C7 — Native long-form ⚠️ needs persona page

**Format:** Native · **TOF** · **Right column EXCLUDED**

**What it tests:** the unevidenced angle in the format most able to make a weak argument land. If the angle can't work here, it can't work.

**Thought process.** Giving the weakest angle the strongest format is deliberate. If we ran it only as statics and it lost, we wouldn't know whether the angle failed or the compression did. The native removes that ambiguity.

**Beat structure:** the thermostat creeping up over a winter · the rest of the house too warm while one room stays cold · someone else in the house complaining it's stuffy · the realisation that the whole house is being conditioned for one room · the explanation · the partial fix · **the thermostat going back down.**

**Image:** a hallway thermostat, authorless POV.

Warm indoor lamp light mixed with cool daylight from a doorway. A hallway wall, a light switch, the edge of a doorframe. Ordinary, unstyled, mild grain, slightly imperfect framing. ## B1C8 — Two-panel thermometer ⭐ the batch's most research-backed image

**Format:** Documentary comparison · **TOF** · all placements

**What it tests:** the single most directly evidenced *visual* idea in the entire project.

**Thought process — this is the one I'd bet on.** Phase 4 of the journey research ran four targeted searches for metaphors — icebox, meat locker, fridge, sauna — and **found none in customers' own words.** What they reach for instead is a thermometer reading: *13° overnight*, *55 degrees*, *"5 degrees colder"*, *"Waaaay cooler."*

So a number-versus-number image is not a creative idea we imposed. **It is literally how these people describe their own problem to strangers on the internet.** A viewer scrolling past sees the exact comparison they have made in their own house.

It also requires **no product, no persona page and no supplier photography** — two $10 thermometers and an afternoon — and it carries in every placement because the message is in the image.

**The creative:** two identical cheap thermometers, photographed in two rooms of the same house, same afternoon. Hallway reads warm; the problem room reads several degrees colder. Presented side by side like evidence, not like an ad.

> **Design direction:** *Two-panel static, 4:5. Each panel is a real iPhone photograph of an identical small white digital thermometer sitting on a surface — left panel on a hallway console table, right panel on a bedroom nightstand — shot the same way, same distance, same light conditions, same afternoon. Left display reads 70°, right reads 62°, both legible. A thin warm off-white gutter between panels. Small mono labels beneath each: "HALLWAY" and "BACK BEDROOM". One line across the bottom in near-black Archivo: "Same house. Same afternoon." No product, no price, no CTA, no badge, no arrows, no annotation.*

**Primary text direction:** short. *Eight degrees, one hallway apart. The furnace is fine — the room is last on the duct run and gets whatever's left.* Then the partial outcome.

**Headline:** *Same house. Same afternoon.*
**Description:** *The room at the end of the run.*

## B1C9 — Relatable Hook

**Format:** Situation-naming, typographic + photo · **TOF** · all placements

**What it tests:** whether **naming the situation** beats explaining or demonstrating it.

**Thought process.** The swipe file's example is "12-HOUR SHIFT SHOES" — the hook names a lifestyle reality rather than a product or a benefit, and the headline does the filtering. If you don't live that reality you scroll; if you do, you can't not stop. Strong executions pick a situation specific enough to feel personal and broad enough to scale.

Ours: **"THE ROOM YOU CLOSE THE DOOR TO."** It's specific, it's behavioural rather than descriptive, and it comes from the research — shutting the door is the single most recommended free fix across every source, and one poster who did it then worried the sealed cold room might be *"hurting the house in someway."* The behaviour is real and it's theirs.

> **Design direction:** *4:5. Top 60%: a real photograph of a closed interior door in a domestic hallway, plain, slightly off-centre, ordinary daylight, a strip of carpet beneath. Bottom 40%: warm off-white panel carrying "THE ROOM YOU CLOSE THE DOOR TO." in large Archivo bold near-black, with three short mono lines beneath in terracotta: "Coldest in the house." / "Last on the duct run." / "Gets whatever's left." Small wordmark. No price, no guarantee, no CTA.*

**Headline:** *The room you close the door to*
**Description:** *It's last on the duct run.*
