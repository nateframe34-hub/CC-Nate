## Body Copy

There was a sticky note on our thermostat for most of last summer.

It said DON'T TOUCH. My husband Dave wrote it. He underlined TOUCH twice.

It was meant for me.

----------

Here's how it got there.

Our thermostat is in the hallway at the bottom of the stairs. Dave likes it at 74. Every afternoon around two, I'd go up to my office to work, and by three I'd be sweating at my desk.

So I'd walk downstairs and turn it to 70.

Then 69.

One day in July I put it on 67, and Dave came up from the basement in a hoodie and a blanket. In July. He didn't say anything. He just stood there holding his coffee with both hands.

That night the sticky note showed up.

----------

I want to be fair to Dave, because I was the one being unreasonable. I just didn't know it yet.

When I turned it down, the whole house got colder. The kitchen got colder. The living room got colder. The basement, where Dave works, turned into a meat locker.

And my office? It barely moved.

I bought a little $9 thermometer to prove my point. I put it on my desk. Then I did the dumbest, most useful thing I did all summer. I stood in the hallway and looked at the thermostat. Then I walked upstairs and looked at my thermometer.

Hallway: 72.

My office: 81.

Same house. Same afternoon. Nine degrees apart.

The thermostat said everything was fine. It was fine. For the hallway.

----------

That's the part that took me two summers to get.

Our thermostat doesn't know my office exists. It only knows the air right next to it, in the hallway at the bottom of the stairs. When the hallway hits 74, it thinks the whole house hit 74. So it shuts the AC off.

Meanwhile my office is still sitting at 81, and nobody in the house is asking it how it feels.

So every time I turned the thermostat down, I wasn't cooling my office. I was freezing the hallway, the kitchen, and my husband, so a little more cold air might drift upstairs.

It didn't. Not much, anyway.

----------

I'll be honest about what I tried next, because I think a lot of people do the same things.

A box fan on the floor. Then a tower fan by the window. The fans made me feel better while they blew on me. But the room stayed at 81. A fan can't make a room cooler. It just moves the hot air around.

We had an HVAC guy out in June. Nice man. He checked the system and said it was running great. He said the thermostat was fine too. And he was right. Both were fine. That's what made it so frustrating.

He did say one thing I didn't understand at the time. "That room's just a long way from the unit."

----------

My sister Kerry is the one who explained it. She had the exact same problem in her back bedroom.

Your AC cools the air in one place. Then it pushes it through ducts to every room. The rooms closest to the unit get the cold air first, and they get a lot of it. Every turn and every branch takes a little away. The room at the end gets what's left.

My office is at the end.

So the cold air was being made. Air was coming out of my vent. I could feel it with my hand. It was just a trickle, and a trickle can't beat an afternoon of sun through a west window.

"So the thermostat is lying to us," I said.

"It's not lying," Kerry said. "It's just standing in the wrong room."

I think about that sentence a lot.

----------

Kerry's husband had put a new vent cover in their back bedroom. It has two small fans built into it. It pulls the cold air out of the duct and into the room, instead of waiting for it to drift in.

But the part that got me was the little screen on the front.

It has its own thermostat. For that one room.

You set the number you want in that room. When the room is warmer than that, it runs. When the room gets there, it stops. The main thermostat in the hallway does its job. This does the job for the room the hallway can't see.

I'll admit I rolled my eyes. I'd already bought two fans, a thermometer, and a set of blackout curtains for that room. I didn't want to buy one more thing.

But it was $89.99, and the HVAC quote for "rebalancing the ducts" was a lot more than that. So I ordered one. It's called an Evenroom.

----------

It took me about ten minutes on a Sunday morning. Two screws came out. The old cover came off. The new one went in the same hole with the same two screws. I plugged it into the outlet a foot away and set it to 73.

The first day, I didn't notice much. I was waiting for the room to feel like a fridge, and it didn't.

By Wednesday I looked at my thermometer at three in the afternoon. It said 75.

By the end of the first week, I realized I hadn't gone downstairs to touch the thermostat once. Not once. I didn't need to. The room had its own.

The part I didn't expect was the door. I used to keep my office door shut all afternoon, trying to hold in whatever cold air made it up there. Now I leave it open. I didn't even notice I'd started doing that until Dave pointed it out.

----------

About two weeks in, Dave came upstairs to ask me something. He stood in my doorway for a second and said, "Huh. It's not bad up here."

Then he went back downstairs, and a few minutes later I heard the thermostat beep.

He put it back to 74.

The sticky note came off a few days later. I didn't see him take it down. It was just gone.

----------

It's not magic. My office is still the warmest room in the house on a really bad afternoon. It has a big west window, and there's only so much you can do about the sun.

But it's a room I can work in now. The thermostat stays where Dave likes it. The fans are in the garage. And nobody in this house has touched that thermostat in a month.

I told my friend Renee about it at work, and she bought one for her son's room over the garage. She texted me a picture of the little screen a week later. It said 72. Then she wrote, "Why did nobody tell us this?"

That's how I feel too.

----------

If you've got one room that's always hotter than the rest, try what I did. Put a cheap thermometer in that room and look at the thermostat in the hallway on the same afternoon. If the numbers don't match, the thermostat isn't wrong. It's just standing in the wrong room.

Ours was nine degrees wrong for two summers.
