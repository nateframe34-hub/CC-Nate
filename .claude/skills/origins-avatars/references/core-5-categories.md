# The Core 5 Categories — Full Reference

Source: Origins avatar module (Spencer / Easy A Media), grounded in Eugene Schwartz's
*Breakthrough Advertising*.

Avatars exist on a spectrum with effectively infinite combinations. On one end you can build
an avatar so specific it describes a single person. On the other, so broad it includes
everyone. Both extremes are useless. The skill is in choosing where on that spectrum to sit,
which is governed by one rule:

- **Broader avatar** → your product, offer, brand, and marketing skill must all be better.
- **More specific avatar** → you can win with a less exceptional product, because
  relatability is doing the work.

A brand *earns* the right to go broad. Below $100k/month, go specific: one desire-based
Core Avatar, with sub-avatars built around it.

The infinite-combinations property is a feature, not a problem — it guarantees there are
always untapped avatars available to target.

---

## Why order matters

Traditional avatar building starts with demographics. That is exactly why most avatars are
never used again after they're made. They are not actionable.

Ordering the categories desire-first does two things:
1. It anchors the avatar to something that actually drives purchase behavior.
2. It avoids alienating buyers on logistics. A 22-year-old woman and a 54-year-old man can
   share a desire (find a partner), an experience (can't meet the right person), an emotion
   (fear they never will), and a behavior (dating apps). Add a demographic and you cut one
   of them out for nothing.

Meta targeting is now done almost entirely through the creative, not through audience
selection at the ad set level. So the avatar has to be targeted *in the creative itself* —
which means the avatar must be specific enough to write from. Meta also rewards relevance:
the more the creative resonates with a specific avatar, the lower the costs and the higher
the conversion rate.

The goal of every avatar is a single reaction: **"that's exactly me."** The mental model is
a relatable meme or TikTok — it works because it calls out a specific avatar and the viewer
identifies with it.

## Category 1: Desires

The "I want X" / "I need Y" statements.

**Checker:** If you cannot phrase it as "I want…" or "I need…", it is not a desire.

Example: research says *"Why does it take so long to brush your teeth?"* → the desire is
*"I want to brush my teeth faster."*

### Core desires (the six mass instincts)
These are permanent. They existed 10,000 years ago and will exist in 10,000 more. Only the
*way* they get satisfied changes.

Ranked by present-day power:
1. **Health** — survival, staying alive, staying safe. Still the ultimate; health scares
   move everyone regardless of income.
2. **Status** — being important, respected, better than others. Massively amplified by
   social media.
3. **Relationships** — being attractive, finding a mate, feeling desired. Fundamental but
   subtler in modern marketing due to platform/ad regulations.
4. **Comfort** — making life easier, avoiding pain and work. Elevated because technology
   made comfort both possible and expected.
5. **Control** — having power, predicting outcomes, being in charge. Boosted by modern
   complexity; people want control over chaotic systems.
6. **Belonging** — fitting in, having friends, not being rejected. Important but less
   survival-urgent.

### The elevation strategy
Moving a product *up* this hierarchy generally increases scale. Moving it *down* reduces
emotional urgency, weakens positioning, lowers achievable price, and pushes you into
commodity competition. Positioning a luxury watch (Status) as "convenient timekeeping"
(Comfort) makes it feel cheap.

**But the elevation must feel authentic.** Force it and customers sense the manipulation.

### Surface-level vs core
Build avatars on **surface-level** desires. They are specific enough to be actionable.
Always know which core desire sits underneath, because that tells you the emotional register
to write in.

| Surface-level desire | Core desire |
|---|---|
| "I want to get better sleep" | Health |
| "I want my dog to stop pulling" | Control / Comfort / Status |
| "I need to lose this baby fat" | Health / Status |
| "I want to be in control of my body" | Control / Health |

Note that a single surface desire can map to multiple core desires depending on context.
The other four categories are what disambiguate it — the experiences, emotions, and
behaviors present will tell you which core desire is actually operating.

### Evaluating a desire (3 dimensions)
- **Urgency** — how desperately do they want relief? (minor cut vs broken bone)
- **Staying power** — does the desire renew continuously? (hunger vs wanting to see Paris)
- **Scope** — how many people share it? (collecting fine antiques vs making money)

Pick the desire with the highest combination of all three.

### Where desires come from
Two permanent forces plus two forces of change:

**Permanent force 1 — Mass instincts.** The six above.

**Permanent force 2 — Mass technological problems.** Technology always creates the same
six problems, in every era:
| Problem | Feels like |
|---|---|
| Complexity — too hard to figure out | You feel dumb |
| Overwhelm — too many choices, beeps, distractions | You feel anxious |
| Fragility — breaks right when you need it | You feel frustrated |
| Maintenance — always needs fixing, updating, paying for | You feel tired |
| Incompatibility — won't work with your other stuff | You feel trapped |
| Obsolescence — gets old and useless fast | You feel cheated |

**Forces of change** — these determine *how* permanent desires get expressed right now:
- **Style trends** — what's currently fashionable
- **Mass education** — what society has recently learned

Mass Instinct + Mass Education tends to produce the biggest breakthroughs. Example: people
learned collar-pulling damages a dog's trachea (mass education) + Health instinct →
*"Why are you still choking your dog in 2025?"* — a real winning headline.

**You cannot create desire. You can only channel existing desire onto your product.** Never
fight this; going after a nonexistent desire fails every time.

## Category 2: Experiences

Situations people have gone through or are currently going through. The longest and most
complex category, and an extremely powerful source of relatability.

Two types:

**Situational experiences** — circumstances the person is in or has been through.
- The bedroom gets very bright in the morning
- Dog pulls on walks
- Had a baby
- Lived in a small apartment with thin walls
- Got divorced two years ago

Active experiences create **urgency**. Past experiences create **relatability through
nostalgia**.

**Product-based experiences** — a solution was tried and a specific outcome resulted.
- Tried blackout curtains and still woke up tired
- Used a dog harness and it didn't work
- Lost 30 pounds on keto and gained it back
- Hired a contractor and got burned

Product experiences **always have an outcome** — even "I'm still testing it" is an outcome.

### The critical isolation rule
For core avatar building, **isolate the experience**. Strip out:
- What they want as a result → that's the **Desire**
- How they feel about it → that's the **Emotion**
- What they do in response → that's the **Behavior**

"Tried blackout curtains and still woke up tired" has an *implied* desire (sleep better), an
*implied* emotion (frustration), and an *implied* behavior (searching for something better).
For this category, record only what happened.

**Checker (2 questions):**
1. Does this describe a circumstance or an event?
2. Have you removed the emotion from it?

| Statement | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "Just had a baby" | ✅ Experience (event) |
| "Lives in a noisy apartment" | ✅ Experience (circumstance) |
| "Tried Ozempic and it worked" | ✅ Product experience |
| "Wants another baby after having one" | ❌ Experience + desire |
| "Hates living in a noisy apartment" | ❌ Experience + emotion |

### Why experiences matter so much
Experiences unite people because they've "been through the same thing." They also generate
desires, emotions, and behaviors — and they are what drives market sophistication upward
across a population, including **indirect** experiences ("my friend tried one and it didn't
work").

An experience alone can be a Core Avatar, because sometimes the desire underneath isn't
obvious. "Went to Disneyland" could sit on top of wanting to create memories for kids
(Belonging), reliving childhood nostalgia (Comfort), or getting Instagram-worthy photos
(Status). Narrow with the other categories to find out which.

**Worked example — narrowing an experience by emotion:**
- Core Avatar: someone who went to Disneyland
- Sub-Avatar 1: went to Disneyland and thinks it's overrated
- Sub-Avatar 2: went to Disneyland and thinks there's no better place on earth

These take opposite copy. *"It's like going to Disneyland every day"* lands with SA2.
*"It's like if Disneyland was actually fun"* lands with SA1. Same experience, inverted
message.

**Worked example — desire + product experience:**
- Desire: want to sleep better
- Experience: tried magnesium, it didn't work
- Core Avatar 1: wants better sleep (desire)
- Core Avatar 2: tried magnesium, didn't work (experience)
- Sub-Avatar: wants to sleep better, but magnesium didn't work

Note that magnesium is studied for migraines, bone health, heart health, blood pressure,
anxiety, sleep, and mood — so an experience with a multi-benefit product can lead to many
different desires. Understand which one is operating before you build on it.

## Category 3: Emotions

How people feel about, or in response to, their experiences, desires, and behaviors.

Emotions bypass logic. Most people would never slash someone's tires — until emotion runs
high enough that it stops seeming unreasonable. Emotions create instant "that's me"
recognition.

### Primary emotions (6)
Joy, sadness, fear, disgust, anger, surprise.

Universal, simple, in-the-moment. You can make all six as facial expressions.

**Emotions are universal; triggers are individual.** Every human knows what fear feels like.
What activates it differs completely person to person — and *that* is what makes emotion
useful for avatar creation.

### Secondary emotions
Complex combinations that mean different things to different people. Whenever you see one,
it is a **signal to dig deeper** until you reach a primary emotion and its trigger.

| Secondary | Usually decomposes to |
|---|---|
| Anxious | Fear of a specific experience, or future-pacing sadness that hasn't happened |
| Frustrated | Joy that was present and went away; or anger at repeated failure to attain a desire |
| Overwhelmed | Fear they can't handle the demands, or anger at demands placed with no control |
| Confident | Joy from past successes, or evidence that removed fear about the outcome |
| Excited | Anticipated joy from a future outcome |
| Stressed | Fear of consequences of not meeting expectations, or anger at pressure |
| Worried | Fear of a specific negative outcome; future-pacing loss |
| Disappointed | Sadness from unmet expectations; anticipated joy that didn't arrive |
| Insecure | Fear of judgment/rejection, or sadness from past failures causing self-doubt |

**Checker:** Does this describe how the person feels ABOUT or IN RESPONSE to something? If
it's not one of the six primaries, ask WHY and WHAT until you find the primary underneath.

### Emotions narrow every other category
- **Desire + emotion:** a man wants a six-pack. Some feel *motivated* (they believe they can).
  Others feel *defeated* (they believe they never will). Two completely different ads.
- **Experience + emotion:** a woman's dog pulls on walks. Some feel *embarrassed*. Others
  feel *scared* the dog will get hurt. Two different ads.
- **Demographic + emotion:** turning 40. Some feel *excited*. Others feel *anxiety*. Two
  different ads.

Emotion can anchor a Core Avatar on its own, but emotions are broad and non-specific, so
they are most powerful combined with the desire, experience, or behavior that caused them.
That combination is what makes people feel you're reading their mind.

**Worked example, emotion-first:**
- Emotion: feels tired
- Experience: baby wakes at 5am because of morning light in the nursery
- Desire: wants the whole family to sleep better
→ Sub-Avatar: wants the family to sleep better, but the baby wakes at 5am from morning
light, and the parent feels exhausted.

## Category 4: Behaviors & Habits

Regular action patterns developed in response to experiences, emotions, desires, or
demographics. What people **do** — often predictably. Can be physical or mental
("constantly overthinking things" is a behavior).

How they form:
- Experience → "I tried X and Y happened" → Behavior: "so now I do Z"
- Desire → "I want X" → Behavior: "so now I do Z"
- Emotion → "I feel anxious around people" → Behavior: "so now I never go to social events"
- Demographic → "45-year-old male" → Behavior: "so now I take testosterone supplements"

### Why behaviors are the most underused lever
Behaviors are **core to self-identity** — they're tied to ego (a person's sense of
self-esteem and self-importance). People do things out of sheer "that's the type of person I
am." When an ad calls out the behaviors that make someone who they are, the buy-in is far
stronger than a desire or emotion call-out.

Behaviors are also **predictable**. If you understand someone's behavior, you can tell them
their own future in the ad — you know what they're going to do next.

**Checker:** Does this describe what the person does? Then gauge strength: *how often do
they do it?* More frequent = stronger = more relatable.

### The same behavior, different origins
A woman doing quick 15-20 minute home workouts might have arrived there from:
- Postpartum experience + desire to lose the weight, **or**
- Kept leaving the gym early for school pickup (experience) + wants to be seen as someone in
  shape (desire)

This is valuable: you can target the **behavior alone** to go broader, or drill into it with
the other categories to go extremely specific.

### Behavior-based hooks outperform desire-based hooks on specificity
| Category | Example headline | Breadth |
|---|---|---|
| Desire | "Do you want to lose 20 pounds by summer?" | Very broad |
| Behavior | "If you still meal prep every Sunday, then…" | More specific |
| Combined | "If you're trying to lose 20 pounds by summer and your Sunday meal prep isn't getting you there fast enough…" | Sub-avatar level |

| Category | Example headline | Breadth |
|---|---|---|
| Emotion | "If your skin makes you feel insecure…" | Very broad |
| Behavior | "If you constantly order new skincare products…" | More specific |
| Combined | "If you're constantly ordering new skincare products because you feel insecure…" | Sub-avatar level |

**Note on sophisticated markets (skincare, supplements, etc.):** the obvious 1-2 category
avatars are already being called out by everyone. In these markets you must stack **more**
of the five categories to achieve real relatability.

## Category 5: Demographics

Static, factual characteristics: gender, birth date/generation, race and ethnicity, age,
location, language, religion, marital status, education, employment, income, disabilities,
health conditions, height, weight.

**Use last. Usually don't use at all.**

Demographics drive nowhere near the purchase motivation of the other four categories. The
single biggest mistake in avatar building is isolating buyers on demographics alone.

They are also frequently **assumed** by the other categories — "gave birth" already narrows
gender without you stating it. Let the experience do the work.

**When a demographic genuinely earns its place:**
- The product performs differently for that group (skincare formulated for melanin-rich
  skin — real needs, real differentiation)
- The demographic itself is the relatability hook (Hallow Socks → construction workers,
  $100k+/day; Norse Organics → "moms of 15-year-olds"; "Farmers Only")

Note that even in those cases, the operator goes *deeper* than the demographic — Hallow
Socks' founder understands construction workers' desires, experiences, emotions, and
behaviors. The demographic is the entry point, not the avatar.

**Checker:** Does it fall under one of the categories listed above? If not, it's not a
demographic.

## Beliefs: why there is no Category 6

Beliefs were considered as a sixth category and rejected. A belief is not atomic — it is
**Experience + Emotion + Behavior** tied together.

"People who believe they can't lose weight" decomposes to:
- **Experience:** tried multiple diets and gained the weight back
- **Emotion:** feels hopeless about losing weight
- **Behavior:** avoids starting new diets because they expect failure

Beliefs remain crucial for *messaging*. But during avatar construction, decompose them.
When you catch yourself thinking in beliefs, ask:
1. What happened to make them believe this? → Experience
2. How does this belief make them feel? → Emotion
3. What do they do because of this belief? → Behavior

This forces specificity and actionability. Identify the resulting belief later, for copy.

## How many avatars?

| Revenue stage | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| $0-100k/month | **One** Core Avatar at a time, preferably desire-based. Build sub-avatars around it. 99% of operators belong here. |
| $100k-500k/month | 2-3 Core Avatars with sub-avatars |
| $500k+/month | Can start from core desires directly and run broader avatars |

**The lower your revenue, the more specific you need to be.**

If sub-avatars around a desire aren't working after 2-4 weeks of testing, switch the Core
Avatar to a new desire. But understand that focus and depth compound — the deeper you go,
the better you get.

**How to choose the desire:** look at your biggest competitor, identify what desire they're
scaling with, and go deeper on sub-avatars within it. **Out-avatar them rather than
out-market them** — early on, you're not a good enough marketer to out-market anyone, but
you can absolutely out-specific them.

## Market sophistication (the gate before avatar work)

Five stages, per Schwartz:

| Stage | State | Required response |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First to market | Simple, direct claim. Name it and prove it. |
| 2 | Competition emerges | Enlarge the claim. Out-promise competitors. |
| 3 ⭐ | Market has heard all claims; skepticism sets in | **New mechanism** — how you deliver the same result differently |
| 4 ⭐ | Market knows the mechanisms | **Enlarge the mechanism** — easier, faster, surer, fewer limitations |
| 5 ⭐ | Market no longer believes anyone; actively seeking reasons NOT to buy | **Identification** — identity, emotion, brand experience. Show them who they become. |

Most eCommerce sits at 3-5. Over three-quarters of consumers research before purchase, and
the average category has 15+ direct competitors. 67% of purchase decisions are influenced by
brand story and values rather than features — a Stage 4-5 signature.

**Sophistication is not something you test.** You don't run "different sophistication
levels" against each other. Your sophistication level simply *exists*, and you respond to it.

**Market sophistication is NOT market awareness.** Different concept entirely.

### The Avatar-Market Sophistication Gap
The failure this gate exists to prevent: your avatar is perfectly defined, the desires are
crystal clear, and your product genuinely delivers — but you communicate with that avatar in
a way that doesn't break through the sophistication level, so the ads don't work.

### The three strategic responses (Stages 3-5)

**⚙️ New Mechanism** — a new process, technology, or delivery system that makes a familiar
promise fresh and believable. Sophisticated buyers crave a clear understanding of HOW. Give
them the "secret thing" behind the solution rather than a bigger claim.
- Purple's GelFlex Grid (visibly, demonstrably not foam)
- Seed's ViaCap dual-capsule system (95% survival vs competitors' 20%)
- Apple: South Korea invented the MP3 player; Apple sold "1,000 songs in your pocket"

Failure modes: over-technical communication that alienates, claiming uniqueness for
industry-standard processes, explaining a mechanism without connecting it to customer value.

**💡 New Information** — education that reframes the problem, reveals problems the customer
didn't know existed, or explains why previous solutions failed. Builds authority and
triggers reciprocity with skeptical audiences.
- AG1: $5M+ in studies, educational content on 75 ingredients, $600M+ business
- The Ordinary: radical ingredient transparency, products named for actives
- Ritual: full supply-chain transparency with interactive sourcing maps

Requires real expertise. Surface-level education undermines credibility — your customers are
already educated, but you can still be the expert.

**👤 New Identity** — emotional connection through lifestyle and tribe. Focuses on who the
customer *becomes*, not what the product does. Heavily influences status. Emotional ad
responses have ~23% greater influence on purchase intent than rational ones.
- Liquid Death: $1.4B water brand built on punk identity
- Glossier: "skin first, makeup second"
- Peloton: exercise equipment as status symbol

Fails when the positioning is inauthentic, values misalign with actual behavior, or the
attempt to appeal to everyone produces meaningless positioning.

### Resetting sophistication with a new avatar
You don't need an untapped market. Stanley Cups were Stage 5 with construction workers
("keeps ice 2 hours longer than YETI," "double-wall vacuum insulation"). Repositioned to
Gen Z, they became Stage 1 again ("your emotional support water bottle"). TrueClassic did
the same by targeting the underserved "dad bod" avatar in a Stage 5 t-shirt market where
everyone else claimed "softest / highest quality / best fit."

**Two ways to stand out: innovate the product, or innovate the avatar.**

## Product understanding (prerequisite to all of the above)

**You can't sell what you don't understand.** Every product is two products:

- **Physical product:** materials, ingredients, components, size, weight, construction,
  specs. What you can touch and manufacture. This only justifies the price and documents
  quality.
- **Functional product:** what it DOES for people. The benefits, results, performances, and
  experiences it creates.

**People never buy the physical product. They buy what it does for them.**

A hair dryer's physical product is carbon fiber, wires, a motherboard. Its functional
product is shiny, smooth, celebrity-style hair.

**Product mechanism** = the specific WAY your product creates its result. Not what it does,
but HOW.
- Bad: "Our supplement helps you lose weight."
- Good: "Our supplement blocks sugar absorption so your body burns fat instead."

The mechanism is what creates believability in a sophisticated market, and what gives people
new hope in a saturated category.

**Every performance your product delivers is a potential avenue to a different avatar.**
Without clear performances, you will channel desires your product can't live up to — which
is the number one cause of "the desire clearly exists but my ads don't work."

Note that many of the largest brands start with the customer and the desire, then build the
product around it (Hexclad, Ridge, Grubs).
