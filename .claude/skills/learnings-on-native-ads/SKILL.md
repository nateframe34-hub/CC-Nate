# Learnings on Native Ads — Analysis Skill

---

## CORE BEHAVIORAL RULES (Non-Negotiable)

**Clean slate every time.** Do not reference, compare, or correlate anything from previous ads analyzed or scripts written unless explicitly instructed. Each ad is its own isolated object.

**Active cross-reference check while writing.** This is a non-negotiable rule. As you write each section, actively check whether you are about to reference another ad — even subtly, even by analogy, even by comparison. If you catch yourself about to write something that draws from a different ad analyzed in this workspace, STOP and remove it before it reaches the page. Cross-referencing corrupts the analysis. Examples of cross-referencing to watch for: "this ad does X like the previous ad," "in another ad we saw Y," using a phrase or insight that originated in a different breakdown, comparing structure to a previous script. Catch it during writing, not after.

**Read before writing.** Go through the ad multiple times across different lenses before writing a single word:
- Pass 1: Overall impression and emotional feel
- Pass 2: Structure and architecture
- Pass 3: Emotional journey — what does the reader feel at each stage?
- Pass 4: Language, specificity, and voice
- Pass 5: Mechanisms — hook, villain, proof, CTA, urgency, open loops, angle

**Three-brain process.** Generate the insight → challenge it against the frameworks → challenge it again. Only output what survives all three passes.

**Go underneath.** If the user has provided their own notes, engage honestly — confirm what holds up, then find the layer beneath what they identified. There is always a deeper reason why something works.

**End with a point of view.** Every analysis ends with a final summary answering: *what is the single most important thing that made this ad work?* Not a list. A clear argument.

## NATIVE ADS ARE A SILENT-READING FORMAT

**This is the foundational principle of the entire category and must be applied to both analysis and writing.**

A native ad is primary text that the audience reads in their head. It is never spoken aloud, never narrated, never delivered as audio. The audience encounters it the way they would a Facebook post from a stranger, a Reddit comment, or a long-form text post on Instagram. They read silently, scanning down the page on their phone.

This is the core difference between native ads and UGC video scripts. A UGC script is built for spoken delivery — verbal cadence, conversational tics, rhythm that requires a voice to land. A native ad is built for the eye. Different rules apply to every sentence.

**The rules of silent-reading flow:**

**Shorter sentences.** The eye fatigues faster than the ear. A 30-word sentence that flows naturally when spoken aloud will feel long and dense when read silently. Native ads use short sentences punctuated by even shorter ones for rhythm.

**More paragraph breaks.** Phone screens are narrow. A wall of text causes the reader to bounce. Native ads use frequent paragraph breaks — sometimes after every two or three sentences, sometimes after a single sentence for emphasis.

**Fewer comma-stacked clauses.** Spoken language layers ideas through commas because the listener can hear the pauses. Silent reading processes commas as visual interruptions. A sentence with three comma-separated clauses reads slower silently than spoken. Break it into multiple sentences instead.

**No verbal tics.** Phrases like "I mean," "let me tell you," "literally," "you know," and "okay so" are spoken-word filler. They work in UGC scripts because the speaker's tone carries them. In a native ad they read as awkward typed-out speech. The exception is when the entire ad is voiced as casual social media writing where a single "I mean" or "honestly" lands naturally — but they should be rare, not constant.

**Sentence fragments are stronger in print than in speech.** "Smooth. Clear. Healthy." reads with more impact silently than spoken aloud because the reader's eye gives each word its own beat. Use fragments deliberately for emphasis.

**Quote marks need their own breathing room.** Dialogue inside narrative needs a comma before the quote and ideally a small pause built into the sentence structure. The eye prepares for a quote differently than the ear does.

**The diagnostic question.** When writing or critiquing any line in a native ad, always ask: *does this read clean silently, or does it require spoken rhythm to land?* If the line only works when you say it out loud in your head, it needs to be rewritten for the eye.

**Reference standard:** the Rosabella long-form native ads (blood pressure, beetroot) demonstrate this principle at scale. Read any paragraph of those ads and notice how rarely a sentence runs over 15 words, how frequently paragraphs break, how little verbal filler appears. That is the standard for the format.

**When analyzing a native ad:** include a silent-reading check on the script. Flag any sentences that read like spoken word transposed into text. Flag any paragraph that runs long enough to fatigue the eye on a phone screen. Flag any verbal tics that should have been edited out.

## OUTPUT FORMAT (Critical — Follow This Every Time)

The analysis is delivered as **one long, continuous analytical piece** — not a checklist, not bullet points, not a template being filled in. It reads like a senior copywriter thinking out loud at the highest level.

**The sequence every analysis follows:**
1. Awareness Level
2. Narrator Type
3. Market Sophistication Stage
4. The Angle
5. The Concept / Door
6. The Hook (deep dive)
7. Full ad breakdown section by section
8. Pains / Symptoms / Habits / Beliefs / Experiences / Desires mentioned
9. Copywriting techniques used (with quotes + resource references)
10. Summary of what worked, what didn't, what could be tested in a new variation
11. Final summary (the single most important thing that made this ad work)
12. Key learnings extracted

**Within each section:** flowing analytical prose, not bullets. For every observation: name the technique → quote or reference the specific line from the ad being analyzed → explain the function underneath it → anchor it to a specific named resource from the project knowledge base or documents in the conversation.

**The resource-referencing standard is non-negotiable.** Every meaningful observation must be grounded in a named source. This separates analysis from opinion. The full library to draw from includes: Breakthrough Advertising (Schwartz), the Eugene Schwartz video notes, the short-form native/ugly ad SOP, the long-form native ad SOP (V2), the angle transcripts, the market awareness documents (Easy A Media), the market sophistication documents, the UGC scriptwriting skill, the Evolve copywriting module, the avatar deep dive docs, the winning ad deconstruction workbook, and any other resource uploaded to the project or conversation. Before writing any section, actively cross-reference what is available and apply the most precise resource to each observation.

**Format for referencing:** "Per the short-form native SOP, the permission shift is the structural load-bearer of the Twist the Knife engine — without it, pain amplification creates resentment, not conversion. This ad executes it here: '[exact quote from ad].'"

**The standard:** *What psychological job is this doing, why does it work at that level, and which specific resource confirms it?*

Weak: "The hook creates an open loop."

Strong: "Per Schwartz in Breakthrough Advertising, the headline has one job — to get the reader to read the next line. This hook does that by opening a loop the reader doesn't realize they're carrying: '[exact quote].' When it resolves 80% through, it hits with double emotional weight because the reader has lived the entire story — what the long-form native SOP calls advanced loop architecture, a loop that spans at least half the ad."

Every observation needs the resource that confirms it alongside the line from the ad that demonstrates it.

**The Final Summary:**
A real argument. 1–2 paragraphs answering: what is the single most important thing that made this ad work? Not a recap. A position, argued for. If someone read only that paragraph, they should understand the core insight well enough to apply it to a completely different product in a completely different niche.

## SECTION 1: AWARENESS LEVEL

**Identify before anything else.** The awareness level is not cosmetic — it determines the entire architecture of the ad. Wrong awareness level diagnosis = wrong analysis.

**The critical rule: awareness level is determined by where the ad ENTERS the conversation — the hook, the first 1–5 sentences. Not by what appears later.**

As Schwartz states directly: "It is at this precise point of awareness that your headline begins." The ad's job is to move the reader from their starting awareness toward most aware. That movement happens inside the ad. The destination is always most aware. The label is always the entry point.

This means: if a product or solution is mentioned inside the ad but not in the hook, that does not change the awareness level classification. A blood pressure ad that opens on the emotional problem of a husband deteriorating is problem-aware — even if beetroot supplements appear at line 400. The ad entered at problem-aware. That is its classification.

A practical test: read only the first sentence or two. Ask — what does the reader need to already know and believe for this to land? That is the awareness level.

**Modern reality (from the Evolve module):** In today's landscape, people move through awareness stages faster than ever. Someone problem-aware can become solution-aware within minutes by Googling. This doesn't change how to classify an ad — but it affects how much runway you need. Problem-aware ads must work harder to dramatize the problem before the reader bounces to search for alternatives.

**The five stages:**

**Unaware** — They have the problem or symptoms but don't recognize it as a problem. The hook cannot mention the problem directly — it enters through story, curiosity, or a relatable situation. Goal: make them aware a problem exists. The Evolve module: "Call out the core emotion, educate them on the problem causing that emotion, then the solution."

**Problem-Aware** — They know they have the problem and it impacts their life, but they don't know a solution exists — or they haven't yet looked. The job: dramatize the need so vividly the prospect realizes how badly they need a solution. Schwartz: "Dramatize the need so the prospect realizes just how badly he needs the solution." Hook enters directly in the pain or through a story that mirrors the pain.

**Solution-Aware** — They know solutions exist. They may have tried some. They're comparing options and skeptical. Hook can reference solutions or the category. The job: explain why everything else failed and position this as the only thing addressing the real root cause.

**Product-Aware** — They know this specific product exists but aren't fully convinced it works or works for them. The job: build trust, differentiate, show proof, introduce future pacing of the result.

**Most Aware** — They know the product, basically want it, haven't bought yet. The job: make a great offer, reduce risk, get out of the way.

State the awareness level explicitly, identify the evidence from the hook and opening lines that confirms it, and explain what structural choices that awareness level required the ad to make.

## SECTION 2: NARRATOR TYPE

The narrator is not a stylistic choice. It is a structural decision that determines what emotions are accessible, who the villain can be, what the reader is allowed to feel, and whether the trust architecture holds.

**Identify which narrator type this ad uses:**

**First-person personal story (about themselves)** — The narrator tells their own story. Creates direct identification. Strongest for solution-aware audiences who need to trust the person before they trust the product.

**First-person story about someone they love (spouse, parent, child, pet)** — The narrator is a proxy fighter. They are helpless, fighting for someone else, unable to fix the problem directly. This creates a different quality of urgency and frustration than self-directed pain. The villain (doctor, system, institution) becomes more infuriating because she is being dismissed while fighting for someone she loves. The buyer is often this narrator, not the person with the problem.

**Third-party educational/authority** — A professional, doctor, nurse, trainer, or expert speaking from credential. Not a personal story — structured as education delivered from authority. Handles skepticism differently. Works when the avatar's primary barrier is "I don't believe this is real" rather than "I haven't found the right solution."

**Community/peer recommendation** — Written as if from a peer with no commercial agenda. A stranger in a store. A friend texting. Highest credibility for product-aware audiences who have already been educated and just need social proof from someone like them.

**Analyze why this specific narrator was the right choice for this specific avatar.** What does this narrator give access to that another narrator couldn't? What emotional territory is only available because of this choice?

## SECTION 3: MARKET SOPHISTICATION STAGE

Sophistication is different from awareness. Awareness is about what they know about the problem. Sophistication is about how many similar products and solutions they have already seen, heard claimed, and potentially tried. It measures the market's accumulated skepticism.

**The five stages (from Schwartz via Easy A Media):**

**Stage 1 — Brand new market.** First to market. The prospect has never seen this product category. Simple direct claims work. Name the claim and prove it works. No mechanism needed — the claim itself is novel enough to create interest.

**Stage 2 — Competition emerges.** The market is starting to see similar products. Claims saturate. The job is to make a bigger, stronger claim than competitors. One-up the field.

**Stage 3 — Claims are saturated. Mechanism required.** The prospect has heard every claim and believes none of them. The job is to introduce a new mechanism — a new explanation for HOW the product works differently at the root level. Not a bigger claim. An entirely different explanation. This is where most DTC health and wellness brands live.

**Stage 4 — Mechanisms are saturated.** Multiple brands now claim unique mechanisms. The job is to enlarge and improve on the mechanism — make it easier, faster, more complete, better than competing mechanisms. "Widget 2.0."

**Stage 5 — Complete skepticism.** The market actively resists all claims and mechanisms. Empathetic, identity-level marketing is the only option. Stop selling the product. Sell the version of themselves the prospect wants to become.

**The shirt analogy (Easy A Media):** When all shirts look identical, there's no obvious choice — they pick randomly. When one shirt is clearly different (new brand, new color, new design), it stands out automatically. Stage 3–4 markets are rooms full of identical shirts. Your mechanism or angle is what makes yours different enough to be chosen.

**How to identify the stage:** Determine how many similar products and solutions the avatar has been exposed to and tried. The more they've tried and failed, the higher the stage. A high-stage market requires more mechanism depth, more specificity in why previous solutions failed, and more proof before the product is introduced. Evidence shows in how early and how deeply the mechanism is introduced in the ad.

State the sophistication stage, explain what evidence in the ad confirms it, and identify what that stage required structurally.

## SECTION 4: THE ANGLE

This is the most foundational classification in the entire analysis.

**What an angle is:** The core belief — either already held by the avatar or ready to be shifted — that the entire ad is built on. It is the foundational idea that makes the product feel like the obvious, inevitable answer. The angle is not a hook, not a claim, not a feature, not a format, not an offer. Those are expressions of the angle. The angle is the belief underneath everything.

**Formula:** Core emotion + Core problem/desire + Unique framing = The Angle

**Two types:**

*Belief Proving:* The avatar already holds a belief and the ad confirms it and shows how the product proves it right. This creates fast trust because you're never going against them. "I knew medications were making things worse — and here's proof." The ad's job is to validate what they already suspect and show the product as the logical next step.

*Belief Shifting:* The avatar holds an old belief that is ready to collapse (because the solutions built on that belief have failed repeatedly). The ad gently dismantles the old belief and installs a new one that makes the product the obvious answer. The shift must feel like a natural conclusion the reader reached themselves, never like something being forced on them.

**What an angle is NOT:** A hook ("don't scroll"), a claim ("made hair grow back fast"), a feature ("all natural ingredients"), a credibility marker ("doctor approved"), an offer ("20% off"), or an ad format ("UGC testimonial"). These are elements that can express an angle. They are not the angle itself.

**Angle scoring (when evaluating which angle a brand chose and why it likely won):**
- Emotional voltage: How hard does this hit the avatar's core emotion? (1–5)
- Mechanism alignment: How directly does this tie to the product's unique fix? (1–5)
- White space: How fresh is this angle in the current market? (1–5)
- Priority score: Voltage × Alignment × White space

**When analyzing:** Name the angle in one sentence. State whether it's belief proving or belief shifting. Identify what old belief it's dismantling or reinforcing. Explain why this angle was the right choice for this specific avatar at this specific awareness and sophistication level.

## SECTION 5: THE CONCEPT / DOOR

The concept is the DOOR used to enter the angle's belief. Same belief can be entered through many different doors. Each door catches a different segment of the avatar.

**The critical distinction:** the angle is the belief. The concept is the door. The hook is the threshold. These are three separate things and conflating them produces sloppy analysis.

**Per the concept framework:** "A concept is the DOOR you use to enter the same belief. Same belief. Completely different door. Different energy. Different pull. Different segment of the avatar responds to each one."

**Example — same belief about a skincare product:**
- "The Refusal" door: "I refused to keep using the prescription my dermatologist gave me after what it did to my skin." This is a journey. The reader follows along because she is also refusing. The pull is "let me follow her path because I want to walk it too."
- "The Doctor Visit" door: "I saw four dermatologists and not one of them asked the question that would have saved me years of scarring." This is a mystery. The pull is "what did they miss? What's the question nobody asked?"

Both concepts hit the same belief. But they enter through completely different doors. Different energy. Different pull. Different segment of the avatar responds to each one. The refusal concept catches the woman who is actively resisting. The doctor visit concept catches the woman who is frustrated with the system.

**The rule for testing new concepts:** pick the door that is FARTHEST AWAY from your winner. If your winner is a doctor-visit concept and you make another doctor-visit concept with a slightly different hook, you are just making a worse version of what already works. You won't move the needle. Different door = new segment captured. Same door slightly tweaked = same segment, weaker execution.

**When analyzing an ad's concept:**
- Name the door in one sentence (e.g. "the cornered mother door," "the gym locker room door," "the chronic sufferer who tried everything door").
- Identify what segment of the avatar this door pulls.
- Identify what other doors could enter the same belief but would catch different segments.
- Assess the white space — is this door over-used in the category, or is it relatively empty?

The concept analysis is critical because it determines what the next variation should look like. If the user is going to write a new ad after the learnings, they need to know which door this ad uses so the next ad can use a different one.

## SECTION 6: THE HOOK (Deep Dive)

The hook is where the angle first makes contact with the reader. It is not the angle — it is the entry point into the angle.

**The standard:** The first 1–5 sentences or 0–3 seconds determine everything. This is where the ad declares who it's for, how it's delivering the story, and what question the reader cannot leave unanswered.

**Analyze second by second or line by line:**

For video: What is the first visual? What are the first words? What emotional trigger fires in second 1? What changes in second 2? What commits the viewer at second 3?

For long-form text: What is happening in the first sentence? What emotional trigger? What self-selection signal? What open loop is installed?

**The questions to answer about every hook:**

Does it qualify the avatar instantly through self-selection — meaning the right person knows immediately this is for them, and the wrong person self-exits?

Is there any hint of a product, benefit, or category? There should not be in most native ad hooks. If the reader can guess what's being sold in the first sentence, the hook needs to be rewritten.

What is the specific emotional trigger being pulled? Not just "curiosity" — what specific fear, loss, identity threat, relationship tension, or life event is being activated?

Is this hook functioning as a planted loop that resolves later in the ad, or is it a one-time pattern interrupt? The best hooks in long-form native ads open a loop the reader doesn't even realize they're carrying — and when it resolves much later, it hits with accumulated emotional weight.

Does the hook signal the narrator type immediately? Can the reader tell from line 1 whether this is a personal story, a spouse's story, an authority voice, or a peer recommendation?

**Specificity check:** "My husband looked at me across the dinner table and said 'I think you need to see someone'" is categorically different from "I was always tired." Name exactly what makes the specific version work and the generic version die.

**Voice-of-customer mirroring check:** Are the exact words and phrases in the hook sourced from real avatar language — the kind found in Reddit threads, Facebook groups, Amazon reviews, support tickets? Or do they sound like a copywriter's approximation? This distinction is audible to the avatar and it determines whether the hook creates a "this person knows my life" moment or a "this sounds like an ad" moment.

## SECTION 7: FULL AD BREAKDOWN

Work through the ad section by section. Every section gets:
- What technique is being used
- What psychological job it is doing
- Why it works at that level
- Connection to frameworks where relevant

**The World-Building / Setup**

In long-form ads, the section between the hook and the inciting incident is the most invisible and most important. Analyze the "before" state (who was this person before the problem — this creates the contrast that makes the loss devastating), the gradual decline (slow accumulation of specific small losses, each one a mirror, selected not for medical accuracy but for relational and emotional devastation), and the false beliefs section (the wrong explanations the narrator tried first — these pre-install the cognitive framework of "I thought I understood the problem but I didn't," which makes the mechanism land harder).

**The Villain**

The villain is almost never a person. It's a system, a molecule, an institution, a pharmaceutical incentive structure, a cultural belief. Analyze how it's constructed (earned through story or manufactured), what it takes from the avatar specifically, and whether it has a defensible position. A villain with no legitimate point is cartoonish. A villain who is both right and wrong simultaneously creates genuine narrative tension — the reader can't dismiss them, which forces them deeper into the ad looking for a way around.

The permission shift sits inside the villain section. After the knife has gone in and the villain has done maximum damage, the narrative must release the reader from guilt. This is the structural load-bearer of the Twist the Knife engine. In many sophisticated ads it's distributed across the mechanism section as a reframe — it wasn't your fault, the system was designed against you. Name exactly where it happens and what form it takes.

**The Mechanism**

The mechanism is the hidden reason — the thing the reader didn't know that explains everything they've experienced. Without it the ad is a long story that ends with "buy my product." With it the product feels inevitable.

Check: Is there a named root cause? Is the cascade built (root cause → chain of failures → why partial solutions fail)? Is it delivered through dialogue or exposition? (Dialogue keeps the reader in story mode. Exposition shifts them into "being taught" mode.) Is there at least one analogy that makes the complex concept instantly understandable and memorable?

The mechanism should answer: why did everything they tried fail? Each alternative the reader has tried should be eliminated inside the mechanism, not in a separate objection-handling section. "That's why the melatonin did nothing." "That's why sleeping more made you feel worse." Each line closes a competitor without naming one.

**The Discovery Vehicle**

How does the narrator find the solution? This is one of the most structurally critical decisions in the ad. The messenger's credibility architecture — their relationship to the problem and their motive for sharing — determines whether the solution lands as revelation or pitch.

A stranger with no commercial agenda is more credible than an expert with pharmaceutical relationships. A peer who "had the same problem" triggers proxy hope in a way an expert cannot. The discovery should feel accidental, desperate, or reluctant — not efficient. Efficiency signals a scripted ad.

Pre-emptive competitor inoculation: the strongest discovery vehicles teach the reader how to evaluate the solution category before they go looking. "Most of them are garbage — here's exactly why" is competitive destruction through education, not attack. By the time the reader closes this ad, they have been given specific criteria to evaluate every alternative — and every alternative will fail those criteria.

**The Results Timeline**

Not a list of benefits. A series of specific moments across a believable timeline. The skepticism rhythm is what makes it believable — the narrator expresses doubt before accepting each improvement. This is the Damaging Admission technique applied to a timeline. The narrator earns the right to report results by admitting uncertainty first.

Third-party observation: when a secondary character notices the change unsolicited, that is social proof of transformation, not self-reported benefit. It is 10x more believable. Name every instance.

The relationship resolution is the most powerful moment. It closes the emotional thread that started in the hook.

**The Villain Reversal**

In well-constructed ads, the villain from the setup reappears at the point of transformation and the power dynamic inverts. The doctor who dismissed her is now asking what she did. The authority who threatened him is now taking notes from him. This beat carries all the emotional weight of the entire story. The best ones are played with understatement — a frown, a confused word, a quiet concession. Analyze whether this happens, how it's played, and what the reader feels when it lands.

**The Transition to the Reader**

At some point the ad shifts from third-person story to direct address. The weakest transition: "Are you struggling with [problem]?" The strongest: telling the reader their emotional state. "You're scared. Cornered. Desperate for options." The Completion of Thought technique — finishing the sentence already forming in their mind. The reader doesn't feel sold to. They feel seen.

After the happy ending, the best ads drag the reader back into their own pain state. This is contrast amplification — the positive emotions from the story make the reader's current situation more intolerable by comparison. The gap between the promised land and where they currently stand creates motivational pressure that no benefit list could manufacture.

The question sequence (if present) functions as micro-commitments. Each yes deepens investment. By the end the reader has privately diagnosed themselves. They cannot unread their own answers. Self-drawn conclusions are stronger than any claim.

**The Product Section**

By the time the product is named, the story has done the selling. The product section is confirmation, not persuasion.

Check: When does the product name first appear? (Standard for long-form: 75–80% through.) Does each product differentiator tie back directly to the mechanism explained earlier? Does the language shift to marketing voice at the reveal? Flag any tonal break. Is price reframed against the cost of the alternative?

**The Close**

Loss aversion framing or benefit framing? The close should remind the reader what they lose by not acting, not what they gain by acting. Choice architecture (two paths, one described in passive defeated language, one in active empowered language) respects autonomy while making inaction feel like resignation. Risk reversal positioning: should appear after the mechanism has been explained, where it functions as pure risk removal, not defensive pre-handling.

## SECTION 8: THE 6 FAILURE MODES — CHECK FOR EACH

**1. Wrong engine for the awareness level.** Twist the Knife on a solution-aware audience creates resentment. Relatability on a problem-aware audience who hasn't acted creates a bookmark that's never revisited.

**2. Describing pain instead of twisting the knife.** "My girlfriend pulled her hand back with 15 strands in it" is storytelling. "I kept pretending she didn't notice but deep down I knew she did" is twisting the knife. Name which is happening and whether it's the right choice.

**3. Knife without permission shift.** Amplifying pain without releasing shame creates resentment, not conversion.

**4. Character break at product reveal.** The moment narrator voice becomes marketing voice, the spell breaks.

**5. Overclaiming results.** Modest, specific, qualified results beat dramatic transformations.

**6. Wrong angle for the avatar.** The angle goes against a belief the avatar holds firmly rather than confirming one they hold or shifting one they're ready to let go. This creates resistance no amount of good writing can overcome. If the ad is fighting the reader's existing worldview, it loses regardless of execution quality.

## SECTION 9: AVATAR MENTIONS INVENTORY

After the full breakdown, document everything the ad surfaced about the avatar. This serves two functions: it confirms the depth of the avatar research the copywriter had access to, and it gives the user a usable inventory of customer-language elements they can pull from when writing variations.

**Pains mentioned:** The specific painful experiences, situations, or emotional states the avatar lives with. Quote the exact line from the ad where each pain is referenced.

**Symptoms mentioned:** The physical or behavioral manifestations of the problem. Quote the specific lines.

**Habits mentioned:** The daily rituals, behaviors, or coping mechanisms the avatar has built around the problem. Quote the specific lines.

**Beliefs mentioned:** The wrong, partially-correct, or limiting beliefs the avatar holds about the problem, the solutions, the medical system, themselves, or their future. Quote the lines.

**Experiences mentioned:** Specific shared experiences the avatar has likely lived through — doctor visits, conversations with partners, moments of failure, late-night searches. Quote the lines.

**Desires mentioned:** What the avatar actually wants — not just symptom relief but the deeper emotional, identity, and relationship outcomes. Quote the lines.

This inventory is a quick reference, not deep analysis. Bullet format is acceptable here because the goal is comprehensive documentation, not argument.

## SECTION 10: COPYWRITING TECHNIQUES USED

For every significant copywriting technique the ad uses, do three things:
1. Name the technique
2. Quote the exact line from the ad demonstrating it
3. Reference the specific resource that defines or explains the technique, and quote what that resource says

**Format example:**

*Damaging Admission (applied to results timeline):*
- Quote from ad: "Week one. I used it every morning... Slight reduction in the overnight itching, maybe. Could have been my imagination."
- Resource: Long-form native ad SOP, results section
- What the resource says: "The narrator earns the right to report results by admitting uncertainty first. Every improvement that follows is more believable because the skepticism came before it."

This section is a complete catalogue of the techniques the copywriter applied, anchored to both the ad and the framework that explains it. The user should be able to read this section and have a usable list of techniques + examples + sources they can apply when writing a variation.

---

## SECTION 11: SUMMARY — WHAT WORKED, WHAT DIDN'T, WHAT TO TEST

A direct, structured assessment. Three sub-sections:

**What the ad did well:** The structural decisions and execution choices that made this ad work. Be specific — name the decision, explain why it worked, anchor to the resource that confirms it.

**What the ad didn't do well or could be improved:** Honest assessment of weak spots, missed opportunities, or executional problems. Don't soften this — the value of the analysis depends on its honesty.

**What could be tested in a new variation:** Specific, actionable test ideas. The most important rule from the concept/door framework: if testing a new concept, pick the door that is FARTHEST from the current ad's door, not a slight variation of the same door.

---

## FINAL SUMMARY

After the full analysis, write 1–2 paragraphs that answer one question:

**What is the single most important thing that made this ad work?**

This is a position, argued for. Not a summary of what was covered. Not a list. A real editorial point of view that a skilled copywriter could take and apply to a completely different product in a completely different niche.

The standard:

*Weak:* "This ad worked because of strong storytelling, realistic results, and a credible mechanism." (Recap — not acceptable)

*Strong:* "This ad works because it found the right person to tell the story — and then trusted that choice completely for two thousand words without breaking. Writing this from the wife's perspective is not a stylistic decision. It's a structural one that solves four problems simultaneously: it targets the actual buyer rather than the person with the problem, it creates a quality of helplessness that generates a different urgency than self-directed pain, it makes the villain more emotionally charged because she is fighting for someone she loves and being dismissed, and it makes the discovery more credible because a wife who out-researches the medical establishment has nothing to gain from being right except her husband back. Every other decision in the ad is downstream of that narrator choice." (Position — this is the standard)

## KEY LEARNINGS EXTRACTED

After everything else is written, distill the analysis into 3–5 transferable principles the copywriter can take to the next ad they write — for this brand or any brand. These are not summaries of what this ad did. They are extracted lessons that apply beyond this specific ad.

**Format:** Each learning should be one sentence stating the principle, followed by 1–2 sentences explaining why it matters and where else it applies.

The standard for a good extracted learning: a copywriter who reads only this section should be able to take the lesson and apply it to a completely different niche tomorrow. If the learning is too specific to this particular ad to be transferable, it does not belong in this section — it belongs in Section 11.

## CROSS-REFERENCE CHECK — DO THIS BEFORE FINALIZING

Before submitting any analysis, do one final pass through everything you have written and ask:

- Did I reference any other ad explicitly or implicitly?
- Did I draw a comparison to a previous breakdown?
- Did I use a phrase or insight that originated in a different analysis?
- Am I about to write something that sounds familiar from a previous piece of work?

If any of these are true, remove the cross-reference. Each ad is its own isolated object. The reader of this analysis should not be able to tell what other ads exist in the workspace.
