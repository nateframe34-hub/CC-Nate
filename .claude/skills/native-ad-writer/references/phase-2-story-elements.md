# Phase 2 — Story Elements

Structure alone doesn't convert. These 7 elements are what make copy feel real instead of hollow — what make the reader forget they're reading an ad.

## 1. Character

The reader is not evaluating the product. They are evaluating the person telling the story.

**Ask:**
- Does this product need credibility or relatability?
- Would the reader trust a peer or an expert more?
- What is the dominant objection, and which character overcomes it?

**Character types:**
- **Peer:** Someone like the reader. Wins on relatability.
- **Authority:** Expert credentials. Wins on credibility for unusual mechanisms.
- **Skeptic Convert:** "I thought this was insane. I was wrong." Overcomes category skepticism.
- **Combinations:** Authority + Skeptic Convert is the strongest combo for unfamiliar categories.

## 2. Theme (The 2am Question)

Plot is what happens. Theme is what it means. Without theme, the ad is about a product that worked. With theme, it's about something universal — identity, control, invisibility, hope, connection.

**Theme = the deeper question the reader is carrying.**

Never state the theme. Let it emerge through:
- The low point (where the question feels most urgent)
- The transformation (where it gets answered)
- Dialogue (where others reflect the change)

Examples:
- "Am I still visible?"
- "Will I ever stop feeling stupid for trying?"
- "Am I losing control of my own body?"
- "Is the person I used to be gone forever?"

## 3. Setting + Inciting Incident

Written as ONE scene. Setting makes it real. Inciting incident makes it matter.

**Setting:** Specific sensory details that place the reader in the moment. Time, place, what the character is doing.

**Inciting incident:** The moment normal life breaks. Before it, the character was coping. After it, they can't ignore it anymore.

**Rule:** Open with the scene in the first 3 paragraphs. Vague openings ("I was struggling with dry skin...") get scrolled past. Specific scenes lock attention.

Bad: "I've been dealing with dry skin for a while."
Good: "I was getting ready for my son's wedding rehearsal. I put on foundation. And it cracked. Settled into lines I didn't remember having."

## 4. Conflict and Stakes

**Conflict = the gap** between where they are and where they want to be, between what they're doing and what's actually working.

**Stakes = the cost of nothing changing.** Must escalate. Must feel inevitable.

Weak stakes: "I'll keep being tired."
Strong stakes: "Every year you wait, the gap gets wider. This doesn't fix itself."

Stakes answer: **Why does this matter NOW?**

## 5. Low Point

The most important scene in the entire ad. The hinge where the reader thinks "that's exactly where I am."

**The critical failure mode:**
- Too extreme → "that's not me, I'm not that bad"
- Too mild → "this person doesn't understand real pain"
- Too vague → reader disconnects

Most readers live in **quiet resignation**, not crisis. Write the resignation low point. "I accepted this was just how it was going to be." Not "I cried every night." The depth of the low point determines the height of the transformation.

## 6. Transformation

**Must include:**
- **Progressive timeline** (Day 3, Day 7, Week 2 — specifics, not "I felt better over time")
- **Unexpected discovery** (something they weren't tracking that improved — shoulder tension they didn't know they had)
- **External validation** (husband, daughter, coworker, friend — someone else notices)
- **Identity-level payoff** ("I felt like myself again" — not just "my skin looks better")

Progression matters. Rush through the transformation and it feels fake. Walk through it stage by stage and it feels earned.

## 7. Dialogue + Secondary Characters

**Aim for 3–4 secondary characters per ad.** Each validates something different.

**What each character does:**
- **Friend who recommended it:** Discovery and trust
- **Partner/spouse:** External validation of the transformation
- **Adult child / parent:** Inter-generational recognition
- **Different use case:** Friend using it for something else (eczema, sun damage)

One secondary character is not enough. Each one removes a different objection. The partner removes "is this real?" The friend removes "how did you find it?" The different-use-case character removes "will this work for MY skin type?"

Dialogue should sound like speech, not written testimonials. Use contractions. Use interruptions. Use the exact casual phrasing real people use.
