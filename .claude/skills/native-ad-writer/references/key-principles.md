# Key Principles (The Rules That Override Everything)

These 6 principles override all the craft rules. If any phase-level rule conflicts with these, these win.

## 1. The Prime Directive

> **The reader is not evaluating the product. The reader is evaluating the person talking to them.**

Every decision in the ad — character, voice, tone, specificity, dialogue, transformation — exists to make the narrator feel like a real person. If the narrator feels real, the product gets trusted by extension. If the narrator feels fake, no copy trick can save it.

When stuck on a decision: ask "does this choice make the narrator feel more real or more like a copywriter?"

## 2. Confession Tone, Not Testimonial Tone

The narrator is sharing because they wish someone had told them sooner. They are not reviewing a product.

**Testimonial tone:** "I tried this product and it worked great! Highly recommend!"
**Confession tone:** "I'm going to tell you something I wish someone had told me three years ago. I've been quiet about it because I felt stupid. But I'm done being quiet."

The confession narrator has a reason to share — guilt, responsibility, concern for others — not enthusiasm about a product. Confession narrators have stakes of their own in telling the story.

## 3. Belief Shift Before Product Mention

The new information about why past solutions failed must land BEFORE the product gets named. In most ads, the product should not appear until 50–65% through the copy.

If the reader doesn't understand why everything they've already tried doesn't work, introducing a product just adds it to the list of failed attempts in their mind. The belief shift has to happen first so the product lands in a context where it CAN work.

The sequence:
1. Name the problem specifically
2. Show the failure of past approaches
3. Reveal why those approaches were always going to fail (new mechanism / new information)
4. THEN introduce the product that matches the new understanding

## 4. Specificity = Credibility

Every vague detail erodes trust. Every specific detail builds it.

**Vague:** "I tried a lot of products."
**Specific:** "I tried Cerave, the $80 La Roche-Posay cream my dermatologist recommended, three different retinols, and a vitamin C serum I bought at Sephora after a TikTok video convinced me."

Use numbers. Use timeframes. Use real brand names of things that failed. Use first names of secondary characters. Use sensory details (what it looked like, felt like, smelled like). Use exact phrases from customer language research.

Specificity is NOT about word count. A short ad can be highly specific. A long ad can be entirely vague.

## 5. Real Pain Is the 2am Thought, Not the Surface Complaint

The surface complaint is what they'd say out loud at a dinner party. The real pain is what they think about alone at 2am when they can't sleep.

**Surface:** "My skin has been dry lately."
**Real:** "I caught myself angling away from the camera at my nephew's birthday party. I haven't been in a single photo this year. My daughter hasn't noticed yet. She will eventually. And I don't know what I'll tell her."

The copy speaks to the 2am thought. The surface complaint is too easy to dismiss. The real pain is where recognition lives.

## 6. Don't Over-Exaggerate the Pain

The reader should think "that's my skin/body/life" — NOT "thank god my problem isn't that bad."

Normal dryness, not a medical condition. Normal frustration, not crisis. Normal exhaustion, not mental breakdown. Normal self-doubt, not clinical depression.

Most readers live in **quiet resignation**, not crisis. Write the resignation level. "I accepted this was just how things were going to be" is more relatable than "I cried every night."

The exception: when the target audience genuinely IS in crisis (eczema flare-ups, chronic pain), match the level. But err on the side of under-dialing, not over-dialing. Under-dialed pain reads as "this is me, this person gets it." Over-dialed pain reads as "this isn't about me, this is about someone worse off."

## When Principles Conflict

If two principles appear to conflict in a specific moment of the copy, apply them in this order of priority:

1. The Prime Directive wins over everything
2. Confession tone wins over specificity (don't let specificity turn into clinical testimonial)
3. Belief shift wins over pacing (don't rush the belief shift to hit a rhythm target)
4. Real pain wins over not-over-exaggerating (if there's a tension between matching the reader and not over-dialing, match the reader first)
