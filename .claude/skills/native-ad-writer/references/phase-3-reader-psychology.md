# Phase 3 — Reader Psychology (The Invisible Layer)

These 6 principles are invisible to the reader but they control whether the reader keeps reading, believes the copy, or drifts off. You can have perfect story elements and still write dead copy without these.

## 1. Pacing and Rhythm

Short sentences create urgency. Long sentences slow down and breathe. Variation between them creates rhythm. Monotony creates fatigue.

**Speed up for:**
- The hook (grabbing attention)
- Tension building (short sentences make pain sharper)
- Transitions (moving fast between sections)

**Slow down for:**
- The mechanism (reader needs to understand)
- Emotional peaks (let them breathe)
- The transformation (let them see each stage, let them want it)

**The test:** Read the copy out loud mentally. Where you stumble or run out of breath is where pacing is broken. Fragments for impact. One-line punches after setup.

## 2. Contrast

The brain doesn't process in isolation. It processes through comparison. The GAP between two states is where belief forms.

Weak: "I sleep better now."
Strong: "I used to wake up at 2am and lie there for three hours. Now I sleep until my alarm."

**Embed contrast throughout the copy — not just in a before/after at the end.** Every major point should have a small comparison baked into it:
- "I used to complain about oily skin. Now I'd give anything to have that problem."
- "My morning routine used to take 30 seconds. Now it takes 15 minutes and still doesn't work."

Common failure: All positive. Only talks about how great things are now, no comparison. The transformation feels flat.

## 3. Emotional Sequencing

**The arc:** Each emotion sets up the next.

Fear makes hope feel like relief. Frustration makes understanding feel like an answer. Tension makes release feel satisfying. Without the setup, the payoff doesn't land — the reader processes the information but doesn't feel it.

**The general flow:** Negative emotions first → positive emotions second → action last.

**The rhythm rule:** Never stay in one emotional register for more than 2–3 paragraphs. Alternate. Low → small high → low → bigger high → small low → biggest high. 4–6 alternations, not 2 big blocks (problem block + solution block).

**Common failure:** The copy is two big blocks — all frustration for half, all hope for half. Flat emotional EKG. Good copy has jagged peaks and valleys.

## 4. Open Loops

An open loop = unanswered question, incomplete pattern, promised payoff not yet delivered. The brain treats incomplete patterns as unfinished tasks. Discomfort in not knowing the ending. Keeps reading.

**Types:**
- **Unfinished story:** "Last month, a woman came in to my clinic..." (what happened?)
- **Direct threat:** "Your body is doing something you don't know about." (what?)
- **Transformation tease:** "That's when I found something that changed everything." (what was it?)
- **Named but unexplained:** "There's one thing I changed. I'll tell you in a second."

**Rules:**
- Always have something unresolved pulling forward
- Don't open and close a loop in the same sentence — let tension build
- Don't open too many loops at once (overwhelming)
- Never open a loop you don't close

## 5. Pattern Interrupts

Attention fades even in good copy. The reader starts engaged but drifts by paragraph 4 or 5. A pattern interrupt snaps them back.

**Insert one every 200–300 words.** Types:
- **Emotional shift:** "Now here's what makes me angry."
- **Confession:** "I'm not supposed to be writing this."
- **Format shift:** One line of dialogue on its own line after a long paragraph.
- **Unexpected reversal:** "Actually, forget what I just said. Here's the real story."
- **Direct reader callout:** "If you're still reading this, you already know what I'm talking about."

**The test:** Read the copy out loud. Where does the energy drop? That's where the reader drifts. That's where you need an interrupt.

## 6. Trust Accumulation

You can't ask someone to believe a big claim cold. Start with small truths and build up.

**The sequence:**
1. Observable truths (things they can check against their own experience)
2. Commonly known truths (things they already believe)
3. New information (now you've earned the right to teach)
4. Bigger claims (each prior truth supports this one)
5. Biggest claim (the transformation promise)

Every statement that proves true = a data point of trust. By the time you make the big claim, the reader has evidence you're trustworthy.

**Common failure:** The copy skips the trust ladder and jumps from problem straight to solution. The claim gets rejected because nothing earned it.
