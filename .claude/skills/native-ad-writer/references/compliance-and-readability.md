# Compliance and Readability (Middle Ground Rules)

These rules exist because of a single observation: **the emotional power of a native ad is not coming from the flagged words. It's coming from the specific scenes, moments, and characters around them.** You can cut every flagged word and keep 100% of the emotional payload — and the copy usually gets stronger, not weaker, because you're forced to replace lazy diagnostic language with specific, concrete detail.

This is not a censorship layer. This is a craft layer. Treat it that way.

## The Core Insight

Meta's 2026 multimodal scanner penalizes two things that native copy doesn't actually need:

1. **Direct diagnostic claims** about the reader's body, health, or condition
2. **Negative self-perception framing** (hiding, avoiding, shame behaviors)

Both of these are ways of saying "I know what's wrong with you" and "you should feel bad about it." Neither is what makes native copy convert. What makes native copy convert is:

- Specific scenes the reader recognizes
- Named secondary characters
- Concrete sensory details
- First-person confusion and discovery
- External witnesses to the change
- Functional observations (what happened), not physical descriptions (what it looked like)

All six of those are compliant by default. The flagged patterns are almost always lazy shortcuts standing in for one of these six.

## The 6 Middle Ground Rules

### Rule 1: Keep the Scene, Strip the Diagnosis

Emotion lives in scenes. Flags live in the diagnostic sentences around them. Delete the diagnosis. Keep the scene. The scene is doing all the work anyway.

**Flagged:**
> "After menopause, your body produces up to 60% less sebum. Your skin is starving."

**Compliant (and stronger):**
> "I was 49. My moisturizer stopped working. I didn't know why."

Same feeling (confusion, loss). Zero flags. More specific — which means more credible.

### Rule 2: Shift Shame from Inside to Outside

Negative self-perception ("I stopped taking selfies," "I avoided mirrors," "I hid from him") is explicitly banned by Meta's sensational content policy for health-adjacent products. The fix is to shift the observation from INSIDE the narrator to an EXTERNAL witness — a daughter, a husband, a friend noticing something.

External witnesses are more emotionally powerful than internal shame anyway, because they function as external validation of the change.

**Flagged:**
> "I stopped taking selfies. I started avoiding the bathroom mirror. I'd angle away from the window in restaurants."

**Compliant (and stronger):**
> "My daughter asked why I stopped sending her selfies. That was the moment I couldn't ignore it anymore."

The second version has a secondary character (daughter), a specific behavior the reader recognizes, and an inciting incident — all things that fail in the internal-shame version.

### Rule 3: Functional Description Beats Physical Description

Physical symptom words ("crepey," "papery," "flaky," "saggy," "sunken") are banned for wrinkle/anti-aging/health products under March 2026 enforcement. Functional descriptions of what the body DOES ("doesn't hold makeup," "goes dry by lunch," "can't hold a thought past 11am") say the same thing without tripping the scanner.

Functional descriptions are also more specific, which means they're more credible and more emotionally recognizable.

**Flagged:**
> "Crepe paper neck. Papery skin. Flaky patches around my nose."

**Compliant (and stronger):**
> "My foundation started cracking by 11am. Lotion would disappear in an hour. I was reapplying like it was chapstick."

The functional version describes daily life, which is where recognition lives. The physical version describes appearance, which is where shame lives.

### Rule 4: First-Person Discovery, Not Second-Person Diagnosis

Don't tell the reader what's wrong with them. Let the narrator discover something in front of the reader. The reader learns alongside the narrator — same insight delivered, zero personal attribute assertion, and the emotional tone shifts from "being diagnosed" to "being told a secret."

**Flagged:**
> "Your body stopped making the oil your skin needs."

**Compliant (and stronger):**
> "I learned something I'd never heard before. Skin makes its own oil. And after about 40, it makes less."

Notice how the second version removes "your" entirely. The reader fills in the "me" automatically. That's how first-person discovery works — the reader does the personalization themselves.

### Rule 5: Social Proof Beats "If You're"

"If you're 45, 50, 55+" or "For people dealing with X" patterns are now flagged as indirect second-person targeting under March 2026 enforcement. Replace with social proof framing — third-person general observations that create the same "that's me" recognition without the direct targeting.

**Flagged:**
> "If you're 45, 50, 55, 60+ and your skin changed in a way nobody warned you about..."

**Compliant (and stronger):**
> "A lot of women I know have said the same thing. Same age. Same story. Same frustration."

Social proof is a stronger close anyway — it implies the reader is joining a group, not being singled out by a diagnosis.

### Rule 6: Soft Age Reference Beats Hormonal Reference

You never need the word "menopause" to reach menopausal women. You just name the age, describe the functional change, and let them self-identify. The scanner doesn't recognize "49" as a protected health attribute. The reader does.

**Flagged:**
> "After menopause, everything changed."

**Compliant (and stronger):**
> "I was 49 when everything changed."
> — or —
> "Something shifted around 48. I couldn't explain it."

The specific age is more credible than the hormonal label. Real people say "I was 49." Marketing copy says "after menopause."

## The Vocabulary Swap Table

Always prefer the right column. These are non-negotiable for health-adjacent categories.

| Flagged | Compliant |
|---|---|
| menopause / perimenopause / hormonal | "after 40," "around 48," "when my body changed" |
| your body (stopped / produces / needs) | "the body," "skin," "it" — or first-person observation |
| your skin is (starving / depleted / damaged / broken) | "skin can become dry over time," "nothing I used was working" |
| your cells / your energy | "cells," "the body," "energy" — general, not possessive |
| sebum | "skin's natural oil" or "the oil skin makes" |
| crepey / papery / flaky / saggy / sunken | functional description of what it DOES |
| Are you X? / Do you have X? | scene opener, no question |
| If you're X, Y, Z... | "A lot of women I know have said the same thing" |
| 60% less / 40% less / specific health stats | "a lot less," "significantly less," or drop the stat |
| your doctor is wrong / what your doctor won't tell you | "my doctor's advice wasn't wrong — it was incomplete" |
| cures / heals / fixes / reverses / treats | "helps," "supports," "works with" |
| "I stopped taking selfies" / "I avoided mirrors" / "I was hiding" | external witness noticing the change |
| "Your X is broken" | "I couldn't figure out what was wrong" |
| "You WILL feel different" | "I felt different" (first-person, not promise) |

## 6th Grade Reading Level Mechanics

Native copy should be readable by an average 12-year-old. This isn't about dumbing down — it's about removing friction. Every complex word is a bounce risk. Apply these rules while writing and while auditing.

1. **Sentence length cap: 15 words average.** Anything longer, break it with a period.
2. **One idea per sentence.** Don't stack clauses.
3. **Cut adverbs.** "Actually," "basically," "literally," "completely," "totally," "really," "very" — delete on sight.
4. **Active voice.** "I ordered it," not "It was ordered by me."
5. **Concrete nouns over abstract.** "Jar" over "container." "Friend" over "acquaintance." "Husband" over "partner."
6. **Common words over jargon.** Replace any word a 12-year-old wouldn't know:
   - "fatty acid profile" → cut
   - "molecular level" → cut
   - "transepidermal water loss" → cut
   - "dimethicone" → "some chemical I can't pronounce"
   - "hyaluronic acid" → only keep if it's a brand name being called out as failed
   - "composition" → "what it's made of"
   - "mechanism" → "the reason it works"
7. **Short paragraphs.** 1–3 sentences max in most paragraphs. Single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis.
8. **Read it out loud mentally.** If you stumble, simplify.

## Worked Example — Full Passage Rewrite

Taking the worst-case flagged passage from a real tallow ad and rewriting through all the rules.

**Original (flagged + college-level):**
> "She explained that after menopause, your body produces up to 60% less of something called sebum. That's the natural oil your skin makes. It's what kept your skin soft your entire life. And every moisturizer I'd been buying was water based. Water on skin that's starving for oil. Like pouring water on a sponge that needs butter."

**Violations:**
- "menopause" (hormonal language)
- "your body produces" (second-person health assertion)
- "sebum" (above 6th grade vocabulary)
- "your skin" repeated (personal attribute)
- "starving" (diagnostic claim)
- Reading level: ~9th grade
- Sentence length: 12–22 words

**Rewrite (compliant + 6th grade + same emotional punch):**
> "She told me something I'd never heard before. Skin makes its own oil. And after about 40, it makes a lot less.
>
> Every cream I'd bought was mostly water. Water doesn't replace oil. I'd been using the wrong thing for years without knowing it.
>
> It was like pouring water on a sponge that needed butter. No wonder nothing worked."

**Check:**
- Zero menopause ✓
- Zero "your body" / "your skin" ✓
- Zero "sebum" ✓
- Zero "starving" ✓
- Zero health stats ✓
- Average sentence: 9 words ✓
- 6th grade vocabulary ✓
- Keeps the "sponge / butter" metaphor (the visceral part) ✓
- Keeps the mechanism explanation ✓
- Keeps "wrong thing for years" gut-punch ✓

**Same message. Same emotional payload. Clean through the scanner. Readable by a 12-year-old.**

## Pre-Write Checklist

Before you write a single word of body copy, scan the plan for these. If any are in your plan, stop and rewrite the plan first.

- [ ] No "menopause" or "perimenopause" in the planned content
- [ ] No "your body" / "your skin" diagnostic claims in the planned mechanism explanation
- [ ] No hiding / avoiding / shame behaviors as the narrator's low point — use an external witness instead
- [ ] No physical symptom descriptions — plan functional ones
- [ ] No "If you're X" close — plan a social proof close
- [ ] No stats with percentages tied to the body
- [ ] No dermatologist / doctor character endorsing the product — they can educate, not prescribe
- [ ] No anti-medical framing ("your doctor is wrong")

## Post-Write Readability Check

After the draft is done, before delivery:

- [ ] Every sentence is under 15 words (or if longer, it's intentional for rhythm)
- [ ] Every word a 12-year-old knows — or it's a brand name / personal detail / metaphor that needs to be specific
- [ ] No adverbs on the strike list ("actually," "basically," "literally," etc.)
- [ ] Active voice throughout
- [ ] Concrete nouns over abstract
- [ ] Read out loud mentally — no stumbles

## The Philosophy

The best native ad is not the most compliant OR the most emotional. It's the one that uses compliance as a forcing function for better craft. Every time you can't use a diagnostic shortcut, you're forced to write a specific scene. Every time you can't use a symptom word, you're forced to describe a functional behavior. Every time you can't target with "if you're X," you're forced to build social proof. The constraints make the copy better.

If you find yourself wanting to reach for a flagged word, the correct question isn't "how do I get away with this?" It's "what specific, concrete, observed detail would carry this weight better?" That detail is always there if you look for it.
