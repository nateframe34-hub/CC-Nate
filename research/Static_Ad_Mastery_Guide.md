## 1. The Foundation: Why Static Ads Matter

### The Numbers
- Static images drive **60-70% of conversions on Meta** — they are not secondary to video
- Single image format accounts for **35.8%** of top-performing supplement ads
- Native-style static ads achieve **4x higher CTR** than traditional banner-style ads
- Curiosity and problem-solution headlines average **10% CTR** vs. 4% for straight CTA headlines — a 2.5x difference
- Average CPC for native ads: **$0.20-$1.00** vs. Facebook all-industries average of $1.72

### Why Static First
Origins recommends starting with images before video for three reasons:
1. **Faster to produce** — You can create and test dozens of static variations in a day
2. **Faster to learn** — Static ads isolate the hook/angle effectiveness without video production variables
3. **Use the Marksman Method** — Test multiple angles simultaneously to find direction FAST

### The 2026 Reality: Creative IS Targeting
Meta's Andromeda algorithm (introduced late 2024) flipped the game. Previously, advertisers controlled targeting through audience selection. Now, **Meta uses your creative to determine who sees your ads.** Creative quality accounts for 60-80% of ad performance. The implication: your ad's visual and copy ARE your targeting mechanism.

## 2. How Desire Channeling Works in Static Ads

### The Core Principle
**You cannot create desire. You can only channel it.**

Desire is the force that already exists and flows through the market. Every person scrolling through their feed carries permanent desires (Health, Status, Relationships, Comfort, Control, Belonging) and trending desires. Your ad's job is to tap into and redirect an existing desire toward your product.

### The Five Methods of Desire Channeling in Static Ads

**1. Direct Naming**
Formats: Relatable Hook, Specific Pain Point, Avatar Call Out
- Say the quiet part out loud. Name the pain, the situation, or the identity directly.
- "12-HOUR SHIFT SHOES" names the exact experience
- "MOMS OF 15 YEAR OLDS" names the exact identity
- "LOSE THE MUFFIN TOP FOR GOOD" names the exact pain point
- Works because: When you see your exact reality named, your brain cannot ignore it

**2. Indirect Implication**
Formats: Accidental Ideal Outcome, This Is Not [Product], Classy Offer
- Imply the desire through wordplay, reframing, or misdirection. The viewer infers the promise.
- "I Accidentally Lost 12 Pounds At 52" implies effortless transformation
- "This is a meal. Not a protein shake." implies the product is more than you think
- Works because: Self-generated conclusions are more persuasive than stated claims

**3. Borrowed Desire**
Formats: Stealing Credibility, Common Saying Hijack
- Borrow desire from something the audience already wants or knows
- "Ozempic's New Bestie" borrows Ozempic's cultural momentum
- "Good things take lime" borrows the neural pathway of a famous saying
- Works because: You ride pre-existing emotional associations

**4. Contrast/Absence**
Formats: Us vs Them (all variants), Solution Exaggeration
- Show what life is like WITHOUT your product, making the viewer desire the alternative
- "Them" [ugly, cheap, bacteria] vs. "None of that." — the viewer infers your superiority
- "14+ HOURS. NO SWELLING." — bold contrast between problem and solution
- Works because: The brain processes comparisons naturally and draws instant conclusions

**5. Identity/Relief**
Formats: Native/Camouflage, Pubity Style
- Channel desire through identification ("that's exactly me") and the promise of relief
- "I never had BV until after menopause" — the viewer recognizes themselves in the narrator
- The viewer doesn't buy the product; they follow the narrator's path to relief
- Works because: Empathy creates trust; trust creates action

### The Elevation Strategy
Moving your product UP the desire hierarchy increases emotional urgency:
- **Belonging** (lowest urgency) → **Control** → **Comfort** → **Relationships** → **Status** → **Health** (highest urgency)

Example: NMN positioned for "Comfort" (feel better) is weaker than positioned for "Health" (cellular aging, cognitive decline) or "Relationships" (be present for your family again).

### Desire Strength Formula
**Urgency × Staying Power × Scope**
- Urgency: How badly do they need it NOW?
- Staying Power: Will this desire persist?
- Scope: How many people share this desire?

The strongest ads target desires that score high on all three.

| Lead with a free offer aggressively | The Free Ad |

| Funnel Stage | Best Formats | Copy Approach |

## 5. What Makes a High-Converting Static Ad

### The 10 Universal Patterns (What ALL Successful Formats Share)

**1. Scroll-stopping first, selling second.**
Every format has ONE element designed to interrupt the scroll — a bold number, a provocative word, a curiosity gap, a visual pattern break. The selling happens AFTER the stop. None try to explain the product in the headline.

**2. Specificity over generality.**
"12 Pounds At 52" beats "Lost Weight." "12-Hour Shift Shoes" beats "Comfortable Shoes." "Muffin Top" beats "Belly Fat." "Moms of 15 Year Olds" beats "Parents." Every format that works earns its performance through ruthless specificity.

**3. One psychological lever per ad.**
Each format commits to a single mechanism — curiosity, fear, identity, comparison, social proof, or desire. None try to do everything at once.

**4. The ad must not look like an ad** (or must look like an elevated version of an ad).
Either camouflage as organic content OR transcend typical ad aesthetics through premium design. Both approaches bypass the viewer's ad filter.

**5. The viewer does the work.**
The strongest formats leave gaps for the viewer to fill in. Self-generated conclusions are more persuasive than stated claims. "None of that" communicates more than a bulleted feature list.

**6. Single-glance comprehension.**
Every world-class ad can be understood in under 3 seconds. Average ads require study. If you can't explain the concept in one sentence, it's not sharp enough.

**7. Emotional payload before rational justification.**
Every winning ad leads with feeling: humor, surprise, curiosity, the "aha" moment. Rational proof (stats, ingredients, benefits) supports the emotion — never leads.

**8. Visual and copy are congruent.**
The visual and the copy tell the same story. Nothing contradicts. The Nutella ad shows bread→breakfast. The Tesla ad shows motion blur→speed. Every visual choice reinforces the message.

**9. Category disruption.**
World-class ads break the conventions of their category. Toilet paper that's colorful. A Bible app that uses Satan. A budget airline using food photography. Average ads look like every other ad in their category.

**10. Restraint.**
Not a single world-class ad tries to say three things. Each says one thing with total commitment. Average ads try to communicate five selling points and communicate none.

### The Density Principle
The gap between average and world-class is **density of meaning per pixel and per word.** World-class ads accomplish in one image what average ads fail to do in an entire campaign.

## 6. What Makes a Low-Converting Static Ad

### The 10 Conversion Killers

| Weak Execution | Why It Fails |
|---|---|
| Generic language ("comfortable shoes," "great for parents") | No one self-identifies with vague descriptions |
| Multiple competing messages | Brain can't process competing signals — processes none |
| Visual says one thing, copy says another | Incongruence creates distrust, even unconsciously |
| Looks like a polished ad template | Triggers "ad blindness" — the unconscious skip reflex |
| Over-explains and over-sells | Removes the viewer's opportunity to draw their own conclusions |
| Uses marketing language instead of customer language | "Facilitates enhanced metabolic function" vs. "Tells your body to burn fat" |
| Product dominates with no emotional context | A packshot in white space rarely converts alone |
| Round numbers for claims (10, 50%, 100) | Feel manufactured; odd/specific numbers (12, 56.4%, 14+) feel measured |
| Multiple competing CTAs | Decision paralysis — too many options = no action |
| Over-designed / visually cluttered | Clutter kills the message; every element should earn its place |

### Additional Conversion Killers
- **No CTA at all** — If you don't tell people what to do, they won't click
- **Writing for desktop** — 85% of views are mobile. Design mobile-first.
- **Wrong aspect ratio** — Feed posts need 4:5 or 1:1. Stories need 9:16.
- **Generic stock photography** — Stock photos feel fake. Authentic imagery wins.
- **Ignoring ad fatigue** — Static images fatigue in 7-10 days. Refresh every 2-4 weeks.
- **Mismatched tone** — Casual UGC imagery with corporate copy (or vice versa) creates dissonance
- **Product without context** — Show the product in use, being held, in a real-life scenario
- **One-and-done testing** — Random creative = random results. Systematic testing wins.

## 7. The Visual Execution System

### Visual Hierarchy (The Eye Path)
Every successful ad has a clear visual path. Users scan in a Z-pattern:
1. **Visual/Image** (first impression, 0.5 seconds)
2. **Headline on image** (read before body copy)
3. **Supporting elements** (bullets, stats, proof)
4. **Brand/CTA** (last)

### Layout Principles

**1. Top-to-bottom story structure.**
Hook (top) → Visual/Evidence (middle) → Brand (bottom). This mirrors natural reading and persuasion: attention → engagement → attribution.

**2. Binary/split for comparisons.**
Left vs. right creates instant comprehension. The symmetry makes the comparison feel fair and obvious.

**3. Single focal point for non-comparison ads.**
One dominant visual element. Simplicity prevents eye scatter and ensures the concept lands.

**4. Text and image in dialogue.**
The best layouts create a conversation between text and image — each recontextualizes the other.

**5. Logo/brand at the bottom. Always.**
The brand is the last thing you see — the signature, not the frame.

**6. Feed-native aspect ratios.**
- Always: 1080×1920 (9:16)
- Image safe zones: 1080×1080 (1:1)
- Feed optimized: 1080×1350 (4:5)

### Typography Rules

| Rule | Application |
|---|---|
| Typography matches brand personality | Luxury = serif/script. Bold/direct = heavy sans-serif. Authentic = hand-drawn |
| One typeface, one weight (usually) | Consistency keeps the visual clean. Weight variation only for clear hierarchy |
| Size creates hierarchy, not color | Big text (read first), small text (read second), logo (read last) |
| Uppercase for impact, mixed case for approachability | Match the emotional tone of the message |
| Text is part of the composition | Not pasted on top — intentionally placed within the visual flow |
| On-image headline: 2-3 words max | Big, bold, unmissable. Under 42px for headings, ~24px for sentences |
| Prioritize hierarchy over brand guidelines | The ad's job is to convert, not be a brand exercise |

### Color Principles

**The 60-30-10 Rule:** 60% dominant color, 30% secondary, 10% accent.

| Color Strategy | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Restraint is the rule** | 2-3 colors maximum. Most winning ads use product color + one text color on a neutral background |
| **Background communicates mood** | Dark = authority/premium. White = simplicity/confidence. Warm = comfort. Blue = aspiration |
| **Product color as the only color** | Let the product provide the color in an otherwise neutral frame — makes it impossible to miss |
| **Contrast between problem and solution** | Warm/dark for the problem → cool/bright for the solution (literally tells the before/after story) |
| **Brand colors used sparingly** | Logo small, at the bottom. The brand earns attention through the concept, not logo size |
| **Platform context matters** | Meta is blue/white. Colors that contrast with the feed capture attention. Blue blends and gets scrolled past. |

### Visual Patterns from World-Class Ads

1. **Minimal element count** — 2-3 visual elements maximum. Clutter is the enemy.
2. **Generous white/negative space** — Communicates confidence ("we don't need to fill every pixel")
3. **Product as hero, not decoration** — When products appear, they are central and prominent, but they serve the concept
4. **Real-world context over studio perfection** — Feels discovered, not produced
5. **The visual proves the concept** — Every element earns its place. Motion blur proves speed. Six rolls prove generosity.

## 8. The Copy Execution System

### The Origins Copy Chain
**Feature → Benefit → Desire → Angle → Hook**

This is the fundamental path from product knowledge to ad creative:
- Feature: Contains 500mg NMN
- Benefit: Supports cellular energy production
- Desire: "I want to feel like I did 10 years ago"
- Angle: "Get your old energy back"
- Hook: "Remember when 3pm didn't feel like a wall?"

### The Seven Rules of Copy

1. **Nobody cares about you, your company, or your product.** Frame everything through THEIR lens.
2. **Results are all that matter. Remove your emotion.** Test, measure, let data decide.
3. **Write how people talk, not how they read.** Contractions. Short sentences. 6th grade reading level.
4. **Copy is never too long, only too boring.** Interested people read everything — if it's engaging.
5. **Every sentence creates a slippery slide.** Open loops, curiosity gaps. No natural stopping points until the CTA.
6. **Research is 80% of the work.** Great copy = great research, organized. 2-3 hours research per 1 hour writing.
7. **Daily practice.** Read a piece of copy, write a page of copy, come up with an idea. 90 days = better than 90% of copywriters.

### Headline/Hook Rules

**Character limits:**
- On-image headline: 2-3 words maximum. Big and bold.
- Meta headline (below image): Under 40 characters
- Meta description: ~30 characters (urgency or social proof)
- Primary text hook: First 125 characters must create enough curiosity to stop scrolling (before "See more..." truncation)

**Scroll-Stopping Hook Formats:**
1. **Lead with the result** — Make it a number + timeframe. "3 days to noticeable energy."
2. **Be specific** — "500mg NMN" beats "high-dose NMN." Specificity = credibility.
3. **Name your target customer** — "Women over 40..." — the right person snaps to attention.
4. **Use unique numbers** — 12, 52, 56.4%, 14+, $2.65. Never round numbers. Odd numbers feel measured.
5. **Negative/Warning hooks** — "Stop," "Don't," "Avoid" create pattern interrupts.
6. **Question hooks** — "Still hitting the 3pm wall?" Names the pain and invites self-identification.
7. **Curiosity gaps** — "The one thing I changed after 40..." — enough to be interesting, not enough to satisfy.

### Copy Patterns from World-Class Ads

1. **Hijacking existing phrases.** One-word change to a known saying redirects meaning toward the brand. "Good things take lime." "Grab life by the bowls."
2. **Extreme brevity.** Most winning headlines are under 8 words. Every word is load-bearing.
3. **Double meanings and wordplay.** Find words that mean two things simultaneously — one in culture, one for your brand. Shots, rolls, bowls, lime.
4. **Questions over statements.** Questions invite participation and avoid the "being told what to think" resistance.
5. **Reframing, not describing.** Not "Nutella tastes great on toast" but "This is breakfast."
6. **The punchline structure.** Setup → payoff. Read the quote, see the bottle. The delayed reveal creates memorability.
7. **First person or second person** — never corporate third person. Always intimate.
8. **Staccato, short sentences.** "14+ Hours. No Swelling." Short declarative sentences convey confidence.
9. **Implied rather than stated claims.** "None of that." The strongest claims are ones the viewer completes in their own mind.

### The Four Copywriting Frames
Every piece of copy should hit:
- **NEW** — Novel mechanism or approach
- **EASY** — Low effort/sacrifice
- **SAFE** — Risk reversal, guarantee
- **FAST/BIG** — Speed of results or magnitude of outcome

Bad: "Lose weight fast" (only FAST)
Good: "12,000 women blocked the enzyme destroying their appetite hormone and ate 24% less in week one — without trying. Try it risk-free." (all four)

### Show, Don't Tell
- **TELLING** triggers the logical brain (skepticism): "You'll have more energy"
- **SHOWING** triggers the emotional brain (belief): "Imagine waking up without hitting snooze three times. Walking past the vending machine at 3pm without even thinking about it."

Use **visual keywords** that create mental images: "flushed," "drained," "blocked," "dissolved," "crashed."

## 9. The Body Copy System

### Three Lengths, Three Use Cases

**Short (1-3 sentences)**
- For: Meme ads, engagement bait, product emphasis, entertainment statics
- The image does the selling. Body copy provides context, a question, or a simple CTA.
- Example: "What does your coffee taste like on Mondays?"

**Medium (3-8 sentences)**
- For: Solution exaggeration, product photography, avatar call-outs, benefit-stack ads
- Opens with problem statement or avatar call-out → 3-6 benefits → offer/CTA
- Example: "Tired of achy, swollen legs? Meet our NEW Alpaca Compression Socks! [benefits] Shop up to 30% OFF"

**Long (Full Story — 500-2,000+ words)**
- For: Native/camouflage ads, "image with context" ads, visual keyword ads
- First-person narrative. Uses OBJECTION → CLAIM → PROOF → BENEFIT cycle multiple times.
- Day-by-day timelines. Embedded testimonials. CTA + risk reversal.

### The OBJECTION → CLAIM → PROOF → BENEFIT Structure
Cycle through this for each objection the reader might have:
1. **OBJECTION**: What they're thinking/doubting (address head-on)
2. **CLAIM**: Your counter-statement
3. **PROOF**: Evidence (study, testimonial, mechanism)
4. **BENEFIT**: Emotional payoff (what life looks like after)

### Native/Camouflage Body Copy Structure (Long-Form)
1. **Personal Hook** — Deeply personal, vulnerable statement the avatar identifies with
2. **Symptom Stack** — Everything they've tried or experienced. Mirror their exact language.
3. **The Turning Point** — Doctor visit, friend's discovery, moment of reframe
4. **New Mechanism Reveal** — WHY previous attempts failed. Scientific but 6th-grade readable.
5. **Social Proof Stack** — Short, specific, emotional testimonials from others
6. **Product Introduction** — Connect every ingredient/feature to the mechanism just explained
7. **Personal Results Timeline** — Day-by-day or week-by-week. Specific and progressive.
8. **Emotional Resolution** — Relationship/life transformation. Quote from partner or family.
9. **Reframe + Validation** — "It's not your fault. It's not [common misconception]."
10. **Competitor Teardown** — Why other solutions fail
11. **CTA + Risk Reversal** — Link + guarantee + trust signals
12. **Urgency Close** — "Every day you wait" language. Future self framing.

### Key Body Copy Characteristics
- Written entirely in first person (as a real person, not a brand)
- Uses "========" section breaks as chapter dividers
- 1,000+ words per ad for native format
- Heavy on emotion, backed by specific scientific claims
- Includes statistics and study citations
- Named characters and specific details (Dr. Patel, Valentine's Day, 3:17 AM, $12,000)
- Grammatical imperfections and emotional outbursts reinforce authenticity
- Product doesn't appear until 50-70% of the way through
- CTA framed as sharing, not selling ("I'll put the link below")

### Universal Body Copy Rules
1. Write at 6th-grade reading level (use Hemingway App)
2. Use the customer's exact language (from review mining)
3. Every sentence pulls toward the next (slippery slide)
4. Address objections directly, don't ignore them
5. Use specific numbers (not round) for believability
6. Use visual keywords that create mental images
7. End with clear CTA and risk reversal
8. Body copy should COMPLETE the image, not repeat it
9. For Meta: provide 2 versions (short + long, or two different approaches)

### Meta Ad Text Hierarchy
1. **Primary text** (above image): Sets context, delivers the hook, builds the case. Most impactful.
2. **Headline** (below image): Under 40 characters. Reinforces core promise or CTA.
3. **Description** (below headline): ~30 characters. Urgency or social proof.

## 10. The Testing & Feedback System

### The 3:2:2 Testing Structure
- **3** creative variations of 1 concept
- **2** body copies
- **2** headlines
= 12 possible combinations from one concept inside Meta

### Marksman Method (USE FOR STATIC ADS)
Test multiple angles at once to find direction FAST.
- Take 3 sub-avatars, pick the best angle for each, put the best hook for each angle into a 3:2:2
- **Pros:** Fluid, identifies direction faster when you lack data
- **Cons:** Can't definitively say WHY something is working
- **Rule:** For images, ALWAYS use Marksman unless you have a true winner to optimize

### The Testing Cycle
1. **Marksman first** — Find which angles get traction (spend/sales)
2. **Sniper second** — Go deep on the winning angle to optimize (usually video)
3. **Marksman again** — When performance plateaus, test new angles

### The Three-Step Process
1. Test multiple angles initially to find something that wins
2. Make hypotheses on WHY something is winning
3. Test those hypotheses to narrow down and optimize

### Feedback Loops (MANDATORY for Every Ad)

**Step 1: Hypothesis**
For winning ads: Why did this work? (Ranked by confidence)
For losing ads: Why did this NOT work? (Ranked by confidence)

**Step 2: Action**
What am I doing differently next time? (Ranked by confidence)

### The 14-Point Creative Checklist (Pre-Submission QA)
Before submitting any brief, ask:
1. Does the first 1-2 seconds hook the RIGHT user and KEEP them hooked?
2. Is this content your potential customers would want to see?
3. Does the copywriting create a clear visual in the user's mind?
4. Does this feel native to the platform?
5. Does this contain a label for who the type of person is?
6. Would this make someone smile or laugh?
7. Is it relatable?
8. Does this actually look like an ad? (It should NOT for native formats)
9. Gut check — does this feel authentic?
10. What learnings from previous winning ads have been applied?
11. Does the ad educate the user creatively on how to achieve their core desire?
12. Does this tell a story your ideal customer would find interesting?
13. Have you applied escalation to exaggerate your claim in a clear and relatable way?
14. Does this separate your brand from the competition?

### Testing Best Practices
- Test one variable at a time for clear learnings
- Run for 72 hours or until 2,000 impressions per variant
- Same creative with 3 different hooks shows 20-40% CTR variance
- Static ads fatigue in 7-10 days. Refresh every 2-4 weeks.
- Refresh cadence: Small accounts ($1K-5K/mo) = monthly. Large ($10K+) = weekly.

## 11. Format-by-Format Deep Dive

### Format 1: Native / Camouflage Ad

**What it is:** A paid ad designed to be indistinguishable from organic social media content. No logo, no brand, no polish. The image could be a personal health post, a friend's confession, a support group share.

**Visual execution:**
- First-person/POV photography (looking down at own legs, hands, bed)
- Domestic/personal settings (living rooms, bedrooms, bathrooms)
- Medical/educational imagery (X-rays, anatomical diagrams)
- Candid couple/family photos
- Phone-camera quality — not professionally lit
- Photo collages mimicking Facebook photo dumps
- ZERO brand elements in the image

**Copy execution:**
- First-person narrative throughout ("my story")
- Long-form: 500-2,000+ words
- Delayed product reveal (50-70% through the copy)
- Named characters and specific details
- Raw vulnerability, righteous anger, dark humor
- Colloquial, imperfect language ("I gotta share," "figured what the hell")
- CTA framed as sharing: "I'll put the link below"

**Best for:** Stage 4-5 markets, cold audiences, health/supplement categories, building trust with skeptical buyers

**Why it works:** Bypasses the sophistication filter entirely. The reader processes it as a peer's personal experience, not advertising. The length filters for high-intent readers. The story builds emotional investment before the product reveal.

**Strong vs. weak execution:**
| Strong | Weak |
|---|---|
| Hyper-specific suffering ("gray-green discharge") | Vague descriptions ("embarrassing symptoms") |
| Named enemy (Gluteal Tendinopathy, bacterial dysbiosis) | Generic condition naming |
| Emotional turning points (Valentine's Day scene) | Flat emotional arc |
| Authentic, distinct voice per narrator | Sounds like a copywriter |
| Product appears at 50-70% mark | Product mentioned in first paragraph |
| Narrator was skeptical too | Narrator is a product advocate from sentence one |

---

### Format 2: Avatar Call Out

**What it is:** Bold headline naming a hyper-specific segment of your audience, paired with matching social proof.

**Visual execution:**
- Bold, oversized headline text dominates the visual
- Customer review styled as social media comment/text bubble
- Product shots secondary
- Clean, uncluttered layout

**Copy execution:**
- Headline = the avatar identity ("MOMS OF 15 YEAR OLDS")
- Review must EXACTLY match the headline specificity
- Body copy mirrors the call-out

**Best for:** Stage 1-2, products serving identifiable demographic groups

**Critical rule:** The narrower the callout, the more powerful. "Moms of 15 year olds" > "Parents" > "Hey everyone"

---

### Format 3: Us vs Them (Minimal)

**What it is:** Split comparison — competitor's problems on one side, your confident minimal response on the other.

**Visual execution:**
- Clean split-panel layout
- Competitor product is RECOGNIZABLE (not a generic stand-in)
- Negative callouts in badge/label style on competitor
- Your product stands alone with maximum white space
- "None of that." floats with confidence

**Copy execution:**
- Only list negatives on the COMPETITOR
- Say nothing about your own features
- Let the viewer draw conclusions

**Best for:** Stage 4-5, competing against a well-known market leader with recognized flaws

**Critical rule:** The competitor must be recognizable. If nobody knows the "them," the comparison has no power.

---

### Format 4: Us vs Them (Simplified)

**What it is:** Transformation comparison stripped to absolute essence. Two images, minimal text, showing what your product transforms.

**Visual execution:**
- Pure white/minimal background
- Side-by-side of two nearly identical images with one variable changed
- Maximum simplicity — six words or fewer

**Copy execution:**
- "This is [before]. This is [after]."
- Heritage/credibility line optional ("since 1964")
- Logo placement confident and minimal

**Best for:** Stage 5/brand-level. Mature markets where the product is known. The simplicity is a flex.

**Critical rule:** The transformation must be so clear it needs no explanation. If it requires a paragraph, this format isn't right.

---

### Format 5: Relatable Hook

**What it is:** Names the exact situation/lifestyle the avatar lives, with product benefits mapped to that life.

**Visual execution:**
- Clean, minimal background
- Bold, blocky headline typography
- Bulleted benefit list with colored accent markers
- Single product shot, well-lit, floating
- No lifestyle imagery — the WORDS create the context

**Copy execution:**
- Headline names the SITUATION, not the product ("12-HOUR SHIFT SHOES")
- Every bullet maps to a specific pain point of the target
- Customer-written, story-focused body copy

**Best for:** Stage 2-3, products serving specific professional or lifestyle groups

**Critical rule:** The situation must be specific enough to feel personal but broad enough to capture a meaningful audience.

---

### Format 6: Solution Exaggeration

**What it is:** Bold, specific time/outcome claim that feels almost too good but is specific enough to be believable.

**Visual execution:**
- Dark, high-contrast background with lifestyle photography
- Bold, large white headline typography
- Product shown IN USE, not on a shelf
- Real-world context (work boots, real setting)

**Copy execution:**
- Staccato, declarative headline ("14+ HOURS. NO SWELLING.")
- Unique, specific numbers (14+, not 12 or 24)
- Benefits are outcome-focused, not feature-focused

**Best for:** Stage 2-3, products with measurable benefits

**Critical rule:** Find the sweet spot between impressive and believable. Too far = distrust. Not far enough = ignored.

---

### Format 7: Specific Pain Point

**What it is:** Colloquial, slightly provocative name for the audience's problem, paired with product and offer.

**Visual execution:**
- Warm-toned product photography
- Prominent price slash with deal badge
- Bold headline in contrasting color
- Risk reversal (money-back guarantee)

**Copy execution:**
- Use language the avatar uses about THEMSELVES ("muffin top")
- Slightly provocative — makes them cringe or laugh (either = engagement)
- Price anchoring + risk reversal

**Best for:** Stage 2-3, products where the pain is acute and nameable

**Critical rule:** The language must be what THEY call it, not what marketers call it. "Muffin top" > "abdominal adiposity."

---

### Format 8: Accidental Ideal Outcome

**What it is:** "I accidentally got [desired result]" — implies the product is so effective, the result happened without trying.

**Visual execution:**
- Dark/premium background
- Clean product photography
- Large serif headline typography
- Bullet points with specific benefit claims

**Copy execution:**
- "Accidentally" is the engine word
- Unique, specific numbers (12 pounds, age 52, 56.4%)
- First-person testimonial framing
- Bullet points provide mechanism/proof after curiosity hook

**Best for:** Stage 3-4, products with multiple benefits where you can lead with a surprising secondary outcome

**Critical rule:** The "accidental" framing must be plausible. If it stretches too far, the curiosity becomes skepticism.

---

### Format 9: Stealing Credibility

**What it is:** Borrows fame, trust, or cultural momentum from something the audience already follows.

**Visual execution:**
- Bright, high-contrast colors (scroll-stopping)
- Grid layout — organized, easy to scan
- Visual curiosity element (army of gummy bears)
- Stats grid for rational proof

**Copy execution:**
- Reference must be famous AND logically connected to your product
- Positioning is complementary, not competitive ("Bestie," not "Alternative")
- Stats provide rational backing after emotional hook

**Best for:** Stage 3-4, when a dominant trend/brand exists that your audience follows

**Critical rule:** The reference must be genuinely relevant. Name-dropping without a real connection feels desperate.

---

### Format 10: This Is Not [Product]

**What it is:** Category reframing — tells the viewer your product is NOT what they think it is, then proves the new category.

**Visual execution:**
- High-contrast split layout (dark product photo / bright info panel)
- Bold, clear typography with strong hierarchy
- Stats in highlighted blocks for easy scanning
- Per-unit price for value reframing

**Copy execution:**
- "This is a [elevated category]. Not a [current category]."
- Supporting stats chosen to PROVE the new category claim
- Price reframed per use ("$2.65 per meal")

**Best for:** Stage 4-5, products that look like something the audience already knows but are actually more/different

**Critical rule:** The reframing must be TRUE and PROVABLE. If your stats don't back the claim, the reframe fails and breaks trust.

---

### Format 11: Common Saying Hijack

**What it is:** One-word twist on a universally known saying that connects directly to your product.

**Visual execution:**
- Clean, mood-driven composition
- Product positioned naturally in context
- Text dominates with the clever twist
- Brand subtle at bottom

**Copy execution:**
- Change ONE word in a universal saying
- The changed word must connect to your product ("lime" for Corona, "bowls" for Huel)
- The saying itself becomes a mnemonic device for the brand

**Best for:** Brand-aware audiences, products with a distinctive feature that maps to a common word

**Critical rule:** The wordplay must feel earned and natural, not forced. The connection between the original saying and your product must be obvious.

## 12. The Pre-Launch Checklist

### Before Creating Any Ad
- [ ] Product Overview Document completed (Features → Benefits → Desires → Mechanisms)
- [ ] Market sophistication level identified (Stage 1-5)
- [ ] Strategic response chosen (New Mechanism, New Information, or New Identity)
- [ ] Core avatar and sub-avatars defined
- [ ] Desire Hunting Template completed
- [ ] Angles extracted from sub-avatars (Desire + Behavior + Gap = Angle)
- [ ] Direct hooks written for each angle

### Before Submitting Any Brief
- [ ] Concept defined (what you're testing)
- [ ] Angle(s) explicitly stated (customer's reason to buy)
- [ ] Testing method selected (Marksman for images)
- [ ] Format selected from catalog
- [ ] Messaging written for each variation
- [ ] 2 body copies + 2 headlines for Meta
- [ ] Run through 14-point Creative Checklist
- [ ] Sizes: 1080×1920 (always) + 1080×1080 (image safe)

### After Running Any Ad
- [ ] Feedback loop completed (winning OR losing)
- [ ] Hypothesis ranked by confidence
- [ ] Action items defined for next batch
- [ ] If winner → transition to Sniper for deeper optimization
- [ ] If loser → generate new angles from different sub-avatars

## Quick Reference Cards

### The Strong vs. Weak Creative Cheat Sheet

| Strong | Weak |
|---|---|
| Hyper-specific (12-hour shift, moms of 15 year olds, 14+ hours) | Generic (comfortable shoes, great for parents, long-lasting) |
| One clear psychological lever | Multiple competing messages |
| Visual and copy tell the same story | Visual says one thing, copy says another |
| Feels native to the feed | Feels like an ad template |
| Leaves room for viewer to draw conclusions | Over-explains and over-sells |
| Uses real language the avatar uses | Uses marketing language the brand wishes people used |
| Product present but not the hero | Product dominates without emotional context |
| Specific, odd numbers (12, 56.4%, 14+) | Round numbers (10, 50%, 100) |
| One clear CTA | Multiple competing calls to action |
| Matches product's actual positioning | Claims the product can't support |

### The Format Selection Quick Guide

| Your Situation | Best Format(s) |
|---|---|
| Cold audience, no data yet | Native/Camouflage, Relatable Hook, Avatar Call Out |
| Testing multiple angles | Marksman with 3 different format-angle combos |
| Competing against a known player | Us vs Them (Minimal), Stealing Credibility |
| Product is in a crowded category | This Is Not [Product], New Mechanism angle |
| Audience is highly skeptical | Native/Camouflage (long-form story) |
| You have a strong offer to promote | Classy Offer (premium), The Free Ad (aggressive) |
| You need engagement/page growth | Pubity Style, Meme, Engagement Bait |
| You have a bold measurable claim | Solution Exaggeration |
| There's a cultural trend to ride | Stealing Credibility, Common Saying Hijack |
| Your buyer ≠ your user | Indirect Avatar |

### The Body Copy Length Guide

| Length | When | Image Does | Copy Does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short (1-3 sentences) | Meme, engagement bait, warm retargeting | ALL the selling | Context + CTA only |
| Medium (3-8 sentences) | Benefit stacks, offers, product photos | Stops the scroll | Lists benefits + drives click |
| Long (500-2,000+ words) | Native/camouflage, cold audiences | Creates curiosity | EVERYTHING — story, proof, emotion, CTA |
