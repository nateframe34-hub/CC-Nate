# Native Course Analysis — Part 6: Phase 5 — Native Image Psychology

> **Source file:** `Native Course/01-Phase 5_ Native Image Psychology.docx`
> **What this module is:** The framework for creating images that stop scrolls before anyone reads a word of copy. Not a template library — a way to THINK about what image belongs with what ad. This is the module we should've had BEFORE attempting batch 1 creatives the first time.

---

## The Core Thesis

> **"Make someone mid-scroll stop and think 'wait, that's me' before they even read a word of copy."**

This is the exact principle we validated the hard way when our first batch of creatives failed. The course states it as the primary goal, and every subsequent rule flows from it.

---

## Why Native Images Work (The Psychology)

### The central insight
> **"People trust documentation, not marketing."**

When someone posts about their life — messy kitchen, kid's struggle, their own frustration — it doesn't feel like they're trying to convince you of anything. It feels like they're sharing.

### The course's direct comparison

**Scenario 1:** Perfectly lit photo of a woman smiling, holding a supplement bottle, text overlay "FINALLY LOST 30 LBS."
**Reader's brain:** "Ad. They paid her. Scrolling."

**Scenario 2:** Bathroom scale photo from above, someone's feet standing on it, number visible, taken with phone camera in harsh bathroom lighting.
**Reader's brain:** "Someone documented something. Wonder what happened?"

**Same topic. Completely different response.**

The second image "bought time." Got the reader curious. Now the copy can do its job.

This is the exact failure mode we had with our first round of creatives. We generated polished beauty photography when we should have been generating documentation.

---

## The Core Question

Before making any image, the course says to ask:

> **"If someone experiencing this problem grabbed their phone right now to document their reality — what would they actually photograph?"**

NOT:
- What would look good?
- What tells the story clearly?

Instead: **What would THEY capture in a private, frustrated, or desperate moment?**

### Application to tallow
Running our ads through this question:

**B1C1 (desert dry):** What would she photograph? Her own reflection in harsh bathroom mirror light. Or her foundation cracking in a close-up. Or a close-up of her dry cheek skin under the bathroom fluorescent.

**B1C2 (husband noticed):** What would she photograph? Maybe nothing — or the jar sitting on her bathroom counter the morning after. Maybe a selfie she actually posted for the first time in months.

**B1C3 (dermatologist):** What would she photograph? Her dermatologist's business card. A diagram the dermatologist drew on a pad. The back of a moisturizer bottle with bad ingredients. A waiting room. A prescription bag.

**B2C1 (cow fat):** What would she photograph? Her bathroom counter with all the failed products. Her drawer pulled out with everything inside.

**B2C2 (all the same):** What would she photograph? A lineup of products with labels facing the camera. A close-up of an ingredient list with water circled.

**B2C3 (daughter):** What would she photograph? The new clean counter with one jar. Her daughter noticing.

**These are the right answers.** Most of what we generated in round 2 aligns with this. Some of it doesn't.

## The Three Types of Images That Stop Scrolls

### Type 1: The Thing They're Staring At

**Definition:** The object of their frustration. The thing they deal with daily that reminds them the problem isn't solved.

**Psychology:** People in chronic problems develop hyperawareness of specific objects. Seeing that object triggers instant recognition.

**Examples the course gives:**
- Pill bottle they hate taking
- Scale showing weight they can't lose
- Product graveyard of failed attempts
- The mess they're tired of cleaning

**Why it works:** No explanation needed. They SEE it, they FEEL it immediately.

**Application to tallow:** This is the product graveyard image (B2C1). It's also the "mirror in harsh light showing dry skin" image (B1C1). These are the things our avatar stares at and flinches from daily.

### Type 2: The Evidence No One Else Sees

**Definition:** The private proof the problem is real. What they'd show a doctor, partner, or friend to say "look, I'm not making this up."

**Psychology:** Dismissed problems create documentation behavior. People photograph evidence to validate their experience.

**Examples the course gives:**
- Physical symptoms (sweat, blood, mess)
- Time stamps showing when things went wrong
- Before photos they took but never posted
- Damage or consequences of the problem

**Why it works:** It's intimate and slightly uncomfortable — makes you look because it's real.

**Application to tallow:** This is the extreme close-up of dry skin texture (B1C1 current version). It's the "look, my skin actually changed" evidence. For the Invisible Woman avatar specifically, this might be even more powerful than a product shot — she's been DISMISSED, and evidence is what she wishes she could show to people who didn't believe her.

### Type 3: The Moment They're Alone With It

**Definition:** Not performing emotion. Just existing in the reality of dealing with it.

**Psychology:** Problems create isolation. Showing that isolation moment triggers "that's me" recognition.

**Examples the course gives:**
- Person in car/bathroom/bedroom dealing with it
- Physical distance from others
- Waiting rooms, parking lots, late nights
- The spaces where they hide the struggle

**Why it works:** Everyone has a private moment with their problem. Showing it creates instant connection.

**Application to tallow:** The late-night bathroom moment. The woman sitting on the edge of her bed looking at her phone at 11pm researching "why did my skin change." The mother hiding in the bathroom during a family event because she didn't want to be in photos. These moments aren't performances of sadness — they're small documentary moments of existing with the problem.

**We haven't generated any Type 3 images.** All our creative attempts have been Type 1 (the product graveyard) or Type 2 (skin evidence). Type 3 would be a new direction to test — the "alone with it" isolation moment.

## How to Choose Which Type

The course gives a clear decision rule:

> **"Read the copy. Find the sentence that would make the target audience say 'fuck, that's exactly it.' That sentence tells you what to show."**

### The three-way fork:
- **Is it about an OBJECT they hate?** → Show the object (Type 1)
- **Is it about a SYMPTOM or CONSEQUENCE?** → Show the evidence (Type 2)
- **Is it about FEELING ALONE or DISMISSED?** → Show the isolation (Type 3)

### The one-thing rule
> **"Don't show multiple things. One image = one gut punch."**

One thing. Not a product AND a face AND a lifestyle moment. One thing.

### Application to tallow
Running each ad through the decision rule:

**B1C1 (desert dry):** The "fuck that's me" sentence is "I used to complain about my oily skin. Now I'd give anything to have that problem back." That's about a SYMPTOM (dry skin). → **Type 2: Show the evidence.** Current macro skin shot is correct.

**B1C2 (husband noticed):** The "fuck that's me" sentence is "My husband noticed my skin before I did and he never notices anything." That's about FEELING INVISIBLE and then suddenly being seen. → **Type 3: Show the isolation or the moment of being seen.** A candid kitchen-table photo with her husband looking at her. We haven't tested this.

**B1C3 (dermatologist):** The "fuck that's me" sentence is about being dismissed by doctors ("it's just aging"). → **Type 3: Show the isolation** (waiting room, prescription pad, doctor's note). OR Type 2 (medical diagram). We went with the diagram approach which works for Type 2.

**B2C1 (cow fat):** The "fuck that's me" sentence is the drawer full of failed products. → **Type 1: Show the object.** Current messy bathroom counter is correct.

**B2C2 (all the same):** The "fuck that's me" sentence is realizing all her products have the same ingredients. → **Type 1: Show the object** (lineup with ingredient labels facing camera). Current approach is correct.

**B2C3 (daughter):** The "fuck that's me" sentence is about her daughter noticing the counter is empty. → **Type 1: Show the object** (empty counter with one jar) OR Type 3 (daughter in the doorway looking in). We went with Type 1. Type 3 might be stronger for emotional pull but harder to generate well.

## The POV Rule

> **"Shoot from THEIR perspective, not an observer's."**

### The rule:
- Looking down at own hands/body
- What they see when dealing with it
- Phone camera quality, not professional

### Why it matters:
- **Observer perspective** = watching someone else's problem = not my problem
- **First-person perspective** = I'm IN this moment = this is me

### Application to tallow
**This is a critical rule we've been breaking.**

Our macro skin shots are from an OBSERVER perspective — we're looking AT someone's cheek. The POV rule says we should be looking DOWN at our own hands or into a mirror showing our own face.

**Examples we should test:**
- POV looking down at her own hands rubbing moisturizer in (not a close-up of someone else's cheek)
- POV of the bathroom mirror showing her own tired reflection (not a third-person face crop)
- POV looking down at the product graveyard in a drawer (this we already have — it's correct POV)
- POV of her daughter looking at her counter (third-person relative to her daughter = correct for B2C3)

**Rough assessment:** Our B2C1 (drawer) and B2C3 (counter) are correct POV. Our B1 skin macros are technically NOT first-person — they're observer perspective.

**Fix for next iterations:** Try a POV shot for B1C1 — her own hands cupped on her cheeks looking down in a bathroom mirror, showing the dry skin on the backs of her hands and reflected in the mirror glass. That's what SHE would see doing her morning routine, not what a photographer would see photographing her.

## What Makes It Native vs Ad

### The test
> **"Would this image make sense posted to Reddit with 'dealing with this shit again' as the caption?"**

- If yes → native
- If no → too polished, too staged, too much production value

### Application to tallow
Running our current creatives through this test:
- **B1C1 skin macro:** Borderline. It's realistic but very aesthetic. Reddit-caption test: "dealing with this dry skin winter" — passes.
- **B1C3 diagram:** Passes. Looks like a Healthline screenshot.
- **B2C1 counter chaos:** Passes clearly. Looks like a venting post.
- **B2C2 ingredient lineup:** Passes. "Look at all this stuff I've bought" is a natural caption.
- **B2C3 empty counter:** Passes. "Finally simplified my routine" is a natural caption.

We're doing okay on the Reddit test after the v2 rewrites. The v1 beauty photography would all have failed.

## The 5 Common Mistakes

The course lists these explicitly:

### 1. Showing the solution
> **"No. Show the problem moment."**

**Application:** We mostly do this right in v2. But we have a temptation to show the jar in every image. The course says: don't. Show the problem.

### 2. Making it pretty
> **"No. Real life isn't pretty. Harsh lighting. Messy spaces. Unflattering angles."**

**Application:** The product graveyard image needs to be MESSIER, not prettier. Our instinct to make it look good is wrong. The messier the better.

### 3. Adding text/labels
> **"No. The copy explains. The image just shows."**

**Application:** Our B1C3 diagram has text because it's an educational reference image. Debatable whether this breaks the rule — the course says "the copy explains" but also allows "medical illustrations" as a format elsewhere. Judgment call: keep it for B1C3 since the diagram IS the content.

### 4. Showing faces with clear emotions
> **"Usually no. Faces performing sadness looks like acting. Physical reality works better."**

**Application:** This rules out most generated woman-crying-in-bathroom type images. The course says physical reality (the mess, the evidence) beats emotional performance every time.

### 5. Stock photo composition
> **"No. Think phone camera. Think quick documentation. Think 'I need to show someone what I'm dealing with.'"**

**Application:** This is the one we failed hardest on in v1. The v2 rewrites fix it. Keep going in this direction.

## The Real Test (Course's Final Checklist)

Before finalizing any image, ask:
1. **Would the target audience actually take this photo themselves?**
2. **Does it show a specific moment from the copy or a general vibe?**
3. **Can I look at it for 1 second and know exactly what problem this is about?**

If any answer is no, it's not ready.

### Applying to our current creatives:
| Image | Q1: Would avatar take this? | Q2: Specific moment? | Q3: 1-second problem ID? |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1C1 dry skin macro | MAYBE (she might try) | General (not a moment) | YES |
| B1C2 (current TBD) | - | - | - |
| B1C3 diagram | NO (she wouldn't take it) | General | YES (but educational not emotional) |
| B2C1 messy counter | YES | YES (looks like right now) | YES |
| B2C2 ingredient lineup | YES | YES (she set this up) | MAYBE (needs 2 sec) |
| B2C3 empty counter | YES | YES (her after photo) | MAYBE (gentle) |

**Overall pass rate: 3-4 of 6 fully pass.**

The weakest ones on Q1 (would the avatar take this photo themselves) are the medical diagram and the dry skin macro — because SHE wouldn't photograph her own cheek in studio lighting. That's an observer perspective.

## Key Takeaways From Phase 5

1. **Documentation, not marketing** — this is the one-sentence summary of the entire module. Always ask: "Is this a photo or an ad?"

2. **The core question** — "What would THEY photograph?" — is a better mental anchor than any prompt rule. If we'd started here, we wouldn't have wasted rounds on beauty photography.

3. **Three image types** — object, evidence, isolation. Most of our current creative is object (Type 1) and evidence (Type 2). We haven't tried isolation (Type 3) yet.

4. **POV rule is one we're partially breaking.** Our skin macros are observer perspective, not first-person. Worth testing a first-person POV variation.

5. **The one-thing rule** — don't stack multiple elements. One gut punch per image.

6. **Reddit caption test** is the fastest way to diagnose if an image is "native enough."

7. **Common mistakes we've been making intermittently:**
 - Making images too pretty
 - Showing face performing emotion instead of physical reality
 - Stock-photo composition
