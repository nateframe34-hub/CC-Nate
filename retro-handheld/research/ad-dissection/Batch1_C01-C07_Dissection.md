# Competitor ad dissections, batch 1: C01–C07 (2026-11-13)

**What this is:** seven ads the founder pulled from GetHookd as currently running winners, taken apart one at a time with the `learnings-on-native-ads` method, then compared against our own ads (Part 2, at the founder's request).

**How:** each ad was run through `dissect.py` (frames of the first 3 seconds, 12 frames of the rest, transcript with timings, cut counts). Sheets and numbers are in `competitors/`.

**Limits:** I read sampled frames and transcripts. I can't hear tone of voice or music. I don't know how long each ad has run or what it spends. C02 is German, C03 Finnish, C05 Spanish (transcribed with a multilingual model; the Finnish is rough). C02 is a big-brand studio ad, so "running" does not mean "converting" the way it does for the others.

**Frameworks cited:** Origins Reference (`origins-training/ORIGINS_REFERENCE.md`, by section), Allen Sauce takeaways (`origins-training/Allen_Sauce_Takeaways.md`), Schwartz's awareness and sophistication stages as given in the skill and Origins §4.

# Part 1: the seven ads, each on its own

## C01 · Click & Grow smart garden · 37 s · vertical · English

**Awareness level: solution-aware, entered through the product.** The first thing on screen is a comment sticker, "wait is that a real mini garden???", over hands moving through real herbs, and the first line is "This is my newest obsession, my mini garden. And yes, this is a real garden from Click and Grow." The viewer needs to know nothing about the brand. She only needs to have wanted fresh herbs at some point. Schwartz's rule is that the awareness level is where the ad enters, and this one enters at "a thing like this exists, is it real?" The problem arrives later, at 7 to 12 seconds.

**Narrator: first-person peer** (a paid creator, with a disclosure line). The barrier for this buyer is "I'd kill it" and "I have no room". Only a peer can remove that, by saying it first: "I don't have a ton of space. I don't have a green thumb."

**Sophistication: Stage 3.** Home herb kits are an old promise. The ad makes it new with a mechanism (Origins §5, New Mechanism): "These pods come pre-seeded with nutrients your plants need and the garden waters them automatically."

**Angle:** you can grow your own herbs with no space and no skill, because the garden does the work. It is belief shifting: the old belief is that growing things takes room and talent.

**Concept / door: the viewer's-doubt door.** Someone else asks "is that real?" and she answers. This door pulls the curious skeptic. Other doors into the same belief: the tea-recipe door, the small-apartment door, the gift door.

**The hook, second by second.** 0.0 s: close on leaves, two hands working through them, the comment sticker, and a caption in app-style text ("My newest obsession, my mini garden"). Her voice starts at 0.0. 2.1 s: first cut, wider on the garden. 3.0 s: her face pops up behind it, laughing, caption "Yes, it's a real garden". Three things are stacked into frame one: a question asked by a stranger (so the curiosity is borrowed, and it proves people ask), the word "obsession" (a feeling before any feature), and hands touching the product (proof it is real, and movement). The hook is an objection. Origins §9, Strategy 5 gives the body pattern OBJECTION → CLAIM → PROOF → BENEFIT. This ad starts that cycle in the hook: the comment is the objection, "yes, this is a real garden" is the claim, the hands in the leaves are the proof.

**Body.** 5–12 s: names the product, then her desire and her two doubts in her own words. 12–19 s: a specific outcome, "grow all my own herbs at home to make tea. We have some lemon balm, some tulsi, some peppermint." Named herbs, not "herbs". 19–27 s: the mechanism as three actions on camera: pods go in, "You literally just fill up the water tank, plug it in and let it do its thing." That is the EASY frame from Origins §9, Strategy 2. 27–31 s: the outcome again, "all year round". 31–37 s: a soft CTA, "I would definitely go check this out at clickandgrow.com." No offer and no urgency. The picture alternates between her face in her kitchen and close product shots, a cut about every 2 seconds, captions the whole way.

**Avatar inventory.** Desires: "really wanting to grow my own herbs"; "grow my own tea and herbs right at home all year round". Beliefs: "I don't have a ton of space"; "I don't have a green thumb". Habit: making tea at home.

**Techniques.** Objection-first hook via comment reply (Origins §9, Strategy 5). Admitting her own weakness, "I don't have a green thumb" (Origins §9, Rule 1: frame it through their lens). Show, don't tell: filling the tank, plugging it in (Origins §9, Strategy 4). Writing how people talk: "literally just", "let it do its thing" (Origins §9, Rule 3). Format: authentic UGC talking head plus b-roll (Origins §12).

**What worked, what didn't, what to test.** Worked: the doubt is voiced by a viewer, and the mechanism is shown as three easy actions. Didn't: no proof beyond herself, no offer, a weak CTA. Test: the door farthest from this one, such as a gift door ("what I got my mom who kills every plant").

**Final summary.** This ad works because it lets a stranger say the doubt out loud and then answers it with her hands. The comment sticker turns "an ad about a garden" into "a reply to someone who didn't believe it", which is a video people watch on purpose. Everything after that is a peer showing that the hard part has been removed.

**Learnings.** (1) Put the buyer's biggest doubt on screen as someone else's question, then answer it by touching the product. (2) Give the mechanism as actions the viewer could do tomorrow, not as a description. (3) A named, small outcome ("tea with lemon balm") beats a big general one.

## C04 · Socialize app · 21 s · vertical · English

**Awareness level: unaware.** "Oh my god, what happened to my Instagram? There's no Reels." The caption reads "I broke my own Instagram. 😭" No problem is named and no product is hinted at. The viewer walks in on what looks like a mishap. The problem (phone addiction) is not named until 15 seconds.

**Narrator: first-person personal,** selfie to camera in a living room.

**Sophistication: Stage 4.** Screen-time blockers are a known category with known mechanisms. This one enlarges the mechanism (Origins §4, Stage 4: overcome limitations): it removes only the endless feed. "You can still get your home page… you can also still look at stories… you can still look at what people send you."

**Angle:** keep Instagram, lose the scrolling. Belief shifting: the old belief is that the only way to stop scrolling is to delete the app.

**Concept / door: the something-is-wrong-with-my-phone door.** A fake glitch that turns out to be the product.

**The hook, second by second.** 0.0 s: her face, hand over her mouth, and her phone held up to the lens showing an Instagram with no Reels. Small caption at the top, and large one-word captions that follow her voice. Speech starts at 0.0 and runs fast (about 5 words a second). By 3 s she has said the whole alarm. At about 4 s: "I'm just kidding, that is literally done on purpose by the app Socialize." The loop opens with a face reacting and closes with a reversal. The subject of the hook is the platform the viewer is using at that moment, which is about as native as an ad can be. The phone held to the camera is proof before any claim is made.

**Body.** One take, three cuts in 21 seconds. She demonstrates on the phone what still works, then what doesn't: "if you try to scroll past these, you're just not going to be able to." Three "you can still" lines handle the objection (I'll lose my messages) before it forms.

**Ending.** An identity outcome, not an offer: "that's how you're going to get rid of your phone addiction and actually start living the summer you should be living." Origins §5 calls this New Identity: who the customer becomes.

**Avatar inventory.** Pain: "phone addiction". Desire: "actually start living". Belief answered: that quitting the scroll means losing friends' stories and messages.

**Techniques.** Pattern break through a false alarm. Reversal ("I'm just kidding"). Proof by live demo (Origins §9, Strategy 4). Objection handled inside the demo (Origins §9, Strategy 5). Format: authentic UGC talking head, single take (Origins §12).

**What worked, what didn't, what to test.** Worked: a face reacting in the first frame, and a demo disguised as a problem. Didn't: the "just kidding" can feel like bait, and there is no spoken CTA. Test: the same reveal from a second person's view (a friend grabs the phone: "why can't I scroll?").

**Final summary.** The ad turns its product demo into an event the viewer thinks they are witnessing. For four seconds there is no ad, only a person alarmed by her phone. By the time the product is named, the viewer has already seen it work.

**Learnings.** (1) A face reacting to something beats a face describing something. (2) If the product changes what a screen shows, hold the screen up in frame one. (3) You can enter unaware in a short ad if the first seconds are a moment, not a message.

## C05 · ForeverSongs custom song · 76 s · vertical · Spanish

**Awareness level: unaware.** The on-screen hook reads "Pensó que era un día como cualquier otro, pero su familia tenía ESTO preparado…" (She thought it was a day like any other, but her family had THIS ready). There is no product and no problem. It is a story about a person. The product is named for the first time on an end card at 57 seconds, three quarters of the way through.

**Narrator: someone who loves her, watching.** The camera is the family. Small notes on screen narrate what she is thinking: "*No tiene idea que la canción es para ella*" (she has no idea the song is for her). The buyer of this product is the giver, and the giver is the narrator. The person the gift is for never speaks to us.

**Sophistication: Stage 3–4.** Personalised gifts are crowded. A song written about the person is the new mechanism, and the ad lets the lyrics show it rather than explain it.

**Angle:** give her a gift that makes her cry happy tears, a song about her own life. Belief proving: the giver already believes the best gifts show you know the person.

**Concept / door: the unsuspecting-recipient door.**

**The hook, second by second.** 0.0 s: a woman of about sixty with grey curls and glasses sits at a cluttered kitchen table, mugs on the shelf behind her, busy with a small chore. She is not looking at the camera. Nothing dramatic happens in the picture for three seconds and there are no cuts. The caption does the work: the viewer is told something she doesn't know. At 3 s the first note appears and the song's lyrics begin as captions. The open loop is: when will she notice, and what will she do? The viewer knows more than the person on screen, and is waiting for an emotional moment that has been promised. The skill calls a loop that spans at least half the ad "advanced loop architecture". This one spans three quarters of it.

**Body.** Her reaction, in stages, each with its own note: "???" at 9 s; "*Espera…*" (wait) at 21 s; her name, "Paty", in the lyrics at 27 s with "*Ahora no hay dudas*" (now there's no doubt); "*Ayyy*" and laughing at 33 s; "*No se lo esperaba*" (she didn't expect it) with her hand to her face at 45 s; tears at 51 s, "*Que canción tan linda*" (what a beautiful song). The lyrics on screen are the product demo: "Luchando por mí" (fighting for me), "Nunca dejas de soñar" (you never stop dreaming), "A pesar de todo" (in spite of everything).

**Ending.** Black cards with a voice: "Escucha un adelanto de tu canción gratis en 5 minutos. Si te encanta, la compras. Si no, no pagas nada. 4.9 estrellas en Trustpilot y más de 15.000 reseñas. Tu canción completa lista en 24 horas. Haz clic abajo y crea la tuya." (Hear a free preview in 5 minutes. If you love it, you buy it. If not, you pay nothing. 4.9 stars, 15,000+ reviews. Full song in 24 hours. Click below and make yours.) Origins §9, Strategy 1 lists what people value: the dream outcome, how likely it is, how long it takes, how much effort and sacrifice. The body delivers the dream outcome. The end card answers the other four in twenty seconds.

**Avatar inventory.** Desire of the giver: to see her moved, to give something that shows they know her. Experience: an ordinary day at the kitchen table. No pains are named at all.

**Techniques.** Dramatic irony as the open loop (Origins §9, Rule 5, the slippery slide). Show, don't tell, for 56 seconds (Origins §9, Strategy 4). The recipient's unprompted reaction as proof: the skill notes that when another person shows the change unasked, it is far more believable than a claim. Risk reversal placed after the desire is built, where the skill says it belongs.

**What worked, what didn't, what to test.** Worked: it sells a gift by showing the person receiving it, and that person is the age of the audience or the audience's mother. Didn't: 56 seconds before any offer is a long hold, and the black end cards break the home-video feel. Test: a father or husband as the recipient.

**Final summary.** This ad never sells the song. It sells the forty seconds in which a mother works out that her family wrote about her. A gift buyer is not shopping for an object. She is shopping for a reaction, and this is the only kind of ad that lets her see the reaction before she pays. Everything else, the price, the reviews, the speed, is held back until she already wants it.

**Learnings.** (1) For a gift, the demo is the recipient's face, not the product. (2) Tell the viewer something the person on screen doesn't know, and they will wait for the moment she finds out. (3) Keep the offer and the proof for the end, and make the first purchase step free of risk.

## C06 · Travel toiletry bag · 31 s · vertical · English

**Awareness level: problem-aware, with the product in hand.** "If you're a clean freak, you need to bring this when you travel." The viewer must already feel uneasy about hotel bathrooms. The ad then does what Schwartz asks of a problem-aware ad, to dramatise the need, in one image: "Imagine if the last person got sick right there. So gross."

**Narrator: a faceless peer.** Hands and a voice, speaking to "you".

**Sophistication: Stage 3.** Everyone owns a toiletry bag. The mechanism that makes this one new is five words long: "It stands up on its own."

**Angle:** your toothbrush never touches the hotel counter. Belief proving: clean freaks already think hotel surfaces are dirty.

**Concept / door: the hotel-counter disgust door.**

**The hook, second by second.** 0.0 s: two hands hold a flat mesh bag in a real bathroom with bottles on the shelves. 0.5 s: a hand pulls the zip. 1.0–1.5 s: the bag opens out and stands, toiletries visible. 2.0 s: a wider shot as it goes on the counter. 3.0 s: it stands by the sink with a toothbrush upright in it. Two cuts in three seconds. Over this: "If you're a clean freak" (0 to 1.0 s), "you need to bring this when you travel" (1.3 to 2.6 s). The picture answers "what is it" by changing shape, and the words answer "who is it for" with a name people use about themselves. Allen Sauce puts the ad's job as filtering the avatar from everyone else "in the first 0.5–3 seconds". This does it in one.

**Body.** One disgusting image for the problem, then a claim every second or so, each with its own shot: "It holds a lot" (stuffed full); "You can see everything at a glance" (mesh); "fully waterproof, so nothing gets ruined" (held under a running tap); "foldable… just a bit bigger than your palm… slip it into the side of your suitcase". That is CLAIM → PROOF → BENEFIT on a loop (Origins §9, Strategy 5). 25 cuts in 31 seconds.

**Ending.** Offer and urgency: "today, order now and get an early 50% discount with free shipping. The link is in the bottom left corner. The offer only lasts 24 hours."

**Avatar inventory.** Identity: "clean freak". Pain: toothbrush and face wash "on the hotel counter". Belief: other guests leave germs. Habit: packing for travel.

**Techniques.** Identity call-out in the first second (Allen Sauce, ads are filters). Twisting the knife with one image rather than describing the problem (the skill's failure mode 2 is describing pain instead of twisting it; this ad avoids it). Every claim proved on camera (Origins §9, Strategy 4). Format: voiceover plus b-roll, heavily edited (Origins §12).

**What worked, what didn't, what to test.** Worked: the product does something in the first second and a half, and no claim is left unproved. Didn't: the 24-hour deadline is generic, and there is no person and no social proof. Test: a gift door ("for the friend who wipes down the hotel remote").

**Final summary.** A hands-only product demo usually lacks two things, a person and a villain. This ad supplies both in under ten seconds without showing a face: the person is "a clean freak" and the villain is the hotel counter. After that the demo only has to keep its promises, one shot per promise.

**Learnings.** (1) Call the viewer by a name they already use for themselves. (2) Make the product change shape or state in the first second. (3) One vivid image of the problem is worth more than a description of it. (4) One shot per claim, and the shot must prove the claim.

## C07 · TerraShell by Quiet Protector · 182 s · 4:5 · English

**Awareness level: solution-aware.** "I'm the founder of Quiet Protector and a lot of people ask me what the difference is between TerraShell and all those cheap space blankets out there." The viewer is assumed to know emergency blankets exist and to be weighing them. A yellow sticker reads "YOUR FAMILY NEEDS THIS".

**Narrator: authority.** The founder, speaking from what he built. The barrier here is "isn't this the same as the $5 ones?", and the person who made it is the one who can answer.

**Sophistication: Stage 3 moving to 4.** He refuses the category: "TerraShell isn't a flimsy foil blanket. There is really no comparison" (Origins §9, Strategy 3: avoid categorising yourself). Then the mechanism: "engineered… around the full hypothermia wrap protocol used in wilderness medicine and search and rescue", a lining that "reflects 90% of your body heat back to your core, sealed inside a windproof and waterproof tactical shell."

**Angle:** this is not a space blanket; it is a shelter that keeps your family warm when the power is out, with nothing that can break. Belief shifting.

**Concept / door: the founder-answers-the-question door.** An us-against-them comparison, framed as a reply to something people ask him.

**The hook, second by second.** 0.0 s: his face low in the frame, cap and beard, outdoors, over a large shot of the product bag, with the sticker above and captions below. 2.0 s: the background becomes a man holding the product in front of an American flag. 3.0 s: a woman doing the same. Inside three seconds the viewer has the authority ("I'm the founder"), the product, the family, an identity signal for this audience (the flag), and a reason to keep listening ("a lot of people ask me"). It is plainly an ad. It can afford to be, because the viewer it wants is already comparing products.

**Body, in order.** The villain: cheap blankets are "thin, crinkly Mylar sheets that rip the second you shift around", "bright reflective orange so you look like a traffic cone", open at the sides. The mechanism. What can't go wrong: "no batteries to die, no fuel tanks to fill and nothing mechanical that can break down on you." Scenes, not benefits: "your living room when the power grid fails and the house drops to 40 degrees"; "your glove box for highway breakdowns in a blizzard." Size as a comparison: "four ounces, packs down smaller than a soda can." A competitor closed without naming one: "regular cotton blankets actually pull warmth away from your body." Proof: "Over 50,000 American families." Scarcity of channel: "You will not find TerraShell on Amazon or eBay." Bonuses: free shipping, a whistle, a checklist. Risk reversal: "a 120 day money back guarantee plus a two year warranty." His own stake: "something I keep in every single one of my own family's cars." A choice: "stop relying on hope and cotton blankets." The offer: "multi packs are on sale for up to 55% off." Then: "if you have any questions, let me know in the comments."

**Pace and format.** A continuous talking head with a cutaway every two to four seconds, slow speech (2.7 words a second), captions throughout. The 4:5 shape is built for the Facebook feed.

**Avatar inventory.** Fears: a blackout in winter, a breakdown in a blizzard, the family freezing. Beliefs: all emergency blankets are the same; things with batteries fail. Desires: "peace of mind"; "privacy… during a crisis". Experience: buying a cheap one and having it rip.

**Techniques.** Refusing the category (Origins §9, Strategy 3). New mechanism (Origins §5). OBJECTION → CLAIM → PROOF → BENEFIT repeated for each doubt (Origins §9, Strategy 5). Show, don't tell, through scenes (Origins §9, Strategy 4). "Copy is never too long, only too boring" (Origins §9, Rule 4). Risk reversal after the mechanism, as the skill prescribes. An invitation to comment.

**What worked, what didn't, what to test.** Worked: every doubt a careful older buyer has is answered in the order he would raise it, and the offer is built around buying for the family. Didn't: three minutes, and an opening that is an ad from the first frame. Test: the same answers through a customer's story.

**Final summary.** This is a sales page read aloud by the person with the most to lose if it is wrong. It can run three minutes because it starts on a question the viewer already has, "what's the difference?", and never answers one he didn't ask. Length is not the risk in an ad like this. Answering the wrong question is.

**Learnings.** (1) For a skeptical buyer, the strongest hook can be the comparison he is already making in his head. (2) Describe the cheap alternative so exactly that he recognises it. (3) Sell the family pack by talking about the family, not the discount. (4) End by asking for questions in the comments.

## C02 · essie gel couture · 20 s · square · German (brand ad, short treatment)

A studio-shot brand film: polished hands, product close-ups, the logo on every frame, a cut almost every second. Voice and text: "Feiertagsstress? Nicht für meine Nägel. Mit gel by essie." (Holiday stress? Not for my nails.) "Gel-Glanz ganz ohne UV-Lampe, bis zu 15 Tage Halt. Kein Absplittern, kein Stress, mehr Zeit zu feiern." (Gel shine with no UV lamp, up to 15 days. No chipping, no stress, more time to celebrate.) "Merry Maniküre." Then a second product, a glitter top coat.

It enters problem-aware with a one-word seasonal question, makes three claims and closes on the pack. This is the "Professional Shot" format, which Origins §12 marks "Not recommended - doesn't convert well right now" for brands buying sales rather than reach. A household brand runs this to be remembered in a shop. It is the opposite of native, and its being live tells us nothing about whether it would sell for us. What it does share with the other six: text in every shot from the first frame, and a seasonal worry named in the first second.

## C03 · Mad Lady "Holly" trousers · 33 s · vertical · Finnish (sale ad, short treatment)

A creator-shot sale ad for a warm audience. Text from frame one: "SINGLES WEEK, 20–50% alennus kaikesta" (off everything), then a code. The visual hook lands at 1.0 to 1.5 seconds: she hooks her thumbs in the pockets and pulls the trousers out wide to show the stretch, then lets go. The voice, roughly: you don't want to miss this; the sale; an extra 5% with the code; "they look like office trousers but feel like loungewear"; office, dinner, home; many lengths and sizes; "they're almost never on sale, so now is the moment"; get a new colour if they're already your favourite.

It enters most-aware and pushes on price. Allen Sauce notes that fast-scaling short-video ads are often "Most Aware → Most Aware", pushing an audience that already knows the product "over the edge with price". Useful for us: a product demo that is also the pattern break, inside the first second and a half; a one-line angle in the "this, not that" shape Origins §10 uses for direct hooks; and a line aimed at people who already own one, which is how a sale ad sells second units.

# Part 2: across the seven, and against our ads (asked for by the founder)

## The seven side by side

| Ad | First frame | Product on screen | A face within 5 s | Text on screen at 0 s | Hook type | Proof on camera | Ends on | Cuts per 10 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C01 garden | Hands in the plants + a viewer's comment | 0 s, in use | Yes (3 s) | Yes | Question (a doubt) | Hands in real leaves; filling the tank | Soft CTA | 5.1 |
| C04 app | Her face + phone held up | 0 s, as evidence | Yes | Yes | Story (a mishap) | Live demo on her phone | Identity outcome | 1.4 |

## What repeats
1. **A person is doing something in the first frame of all seven.** Hands working, or a face reacting. None opens on a product lying still.
2. **Six of seven have text on screen from the first frame.**
3. **Five of seven show a face in the first five seconds.**
4. **The product is in the first frame in five of seven.** So hiding the product is not the rule. The rule is that the product is being used, tested, doubted or reacted to. (This corrects point 1 of the hook brief in `Hook_Research_v1.md`, which said never to open on the product.)
5. **The hook names a person or a doubt, never a feature:** "my newest obsession", "is that real?", "I broke my own Instagram", "she thought it was a day like any other", "if you're a clean freak", "your family needs this".
6. **All five direct-response ads prove their main claim on camera.**
7. **Four of the five direct-response ads end with an offer or a CTA.** The three that state an offer also reduce the risk (pay nothing if you don't love it; a 120-day guarantee) or add a deadline.
8. **There is no single right pace or length.** Talking heads barely cut (1.4 per 10 s). Hands demos cut constantly (8.2). Lengths run from 20 to 182 seconds.

## Against our ads

| | The seven | AD 7 (sells, dear reach) | AD 8 Target (cheap reach, no sales) | S2 (new) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A person doing something in frame 1 | 7 of 7 | **No.** Boxes lying on a rug | Yes: walking to a store | Partly: hands at a gift bag |
| Text on screen at 0 s | 6 of 7 | **None** | Yes | Yes |
| A face, ever | 5 of 7 | **No** | No | No |
| Hook names a person or a doubt | 7 of 7 | Yes, but only in the voice | "Gamers" | Yes ("my husband every year…") |
| Main claim proved on camera | 5 of 5 | **No.** "A hit with kids, a nostalgia trip for adults" and nobody is shown enjoying it | n/a (no claim) | **No.** "He opened it… started playing" over footage of someone else's hands |
| Offer or CTA in the video | 4 of 5 | **No** | No | No |
| Pace for a hands demo | 8.2 cuts per 10 s | **2.0** | 3.4 | 6.4 |

**What we lack, most important first**
1. **A person.** We have never shown a face or a reaction. For a gift, the claim that matters is "he'll love it", and the only proof of that is him. C05 sells a gift to the giver using nothing but the recipient's face, to an audience our buyers' age.
2. **An opening where something is happening.** AD 7's first frame is a product layout.
3. **Text from the first frame on AD 7.** Text alone is not enough, though: AD 13 added text to AD 7's opening and the hook rate only went from about 25% to 31%. The footage has to change too.
4. **A doubt, answered.** C01 and C07 are both built on the buyer's doubt. Ours is "10,000 games for $75, is it junk?" and "will I be able to work it?". No ad of ours raises either.
5. **An offer at the end of the video.** We decided to end on the outcome. C05 does both: the outcome for 56 seconds, then a short card with the offer and the risk removed.
6. **A cut pace that fits a hands demo.**

**What we already do that matches**
- AD 7's first line is a callout ("If you're still looking for that Christmas gift"). In GetHookd's own filter counts, Callout is by far the most common hook type (about 9,000 ads against 1,200 for Story).
- Voiceover plus b-roll is a standard format (Origins §12).
- The S scripts name the giver's problem and tell a story.

## Origins feedback loop on AD 7 (Origins §14)

**Step 1: hypotheses, ranked by confidence**

*Why does AD 7 sell but cost about twice as much to deliver as the Target ad?*
1. **High:** it looks like an ad in its first frame (a staged layout, a still product, a polished voice), so most people leave before three seconds, and Meta charges more to deliver an ad people skip. Evidence: 25–33% hook rate against 44–55% for the store walk-in; all seven winners open on a person in action.
2. **High:** muted viewers get no hook at all, because the hook is only in the voice.
3. **Medium:** nobody is on screen to identify with, and the gift claim is never proved.
4. **Medium:** it is cut too slowly for a hands demo, so nothing new arrives to hold attention.
5. **Lower:** no offer in the video. This would affect clicks more than reach.

*Why did the Target ad deliver cheaply but not sell?* It opens on a place, with movement and a caption (points 1 and 2 above), then has no voice, no problem, no proof and no offer, and it calls out gamers, not givers.

**The 15-checkpoint cheatsheet, run on AD 7**

| # | Checkpoint | AD 7 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook grabs the right user | Yes with sound; no when muted |
